	•	People consulted
Consultants Adam Buss, Clare Limb and Iain Bennett conducted a series of interviews with key staff, participants, partners and stakeholders during w/c 21st August. This was a mix of in-person, online and phone conversations that sought to understand better the opportunities and challenges for the Education and Learning team and programme at Theatre Royal Plymouth (TRP). 
Our approach was ‘inside/out’, meaning we started by having conversations with people ‘on the inside’ of TRP who could provide the greatest insight into past and present work and the impact this had. Those conversations informed what we would ask the organisations and individuals on the ‘outside’ of the organisation and enable us to test perception and key opportunities. 
The following individuals and groups were consulted with either in person, online via Zoom or over the telephone:
	•	TRP Education & Learning (E&L) Team 
	•	TRP Production staff 
	•	Freelancers/Creative Practitioners linked to TRP
	•	Young Company 2022/2023 Cohort
	•	Young Company Alumni
	•	Young Create
	•	Funky Llama Steering Group

	•	Headline Findings
Throughout our consultations both internally and externally we found a deep sense of care and passion for the work undertaken by TRP to date. 
Internally, a sense of pride was shown around the variety of activities on show and the impact these have had and externally. There was a sense of great optimism about the future and possible collaborations. 
Externally, most of the interviewees were quick to find a ‘best’ memory of TRP (this was the only question we asked every interviewee) and these ranged from performances that they will always remember to the welcome given by staff on arrival at the theatre, making it feel a ‘safe space’.  
All participants and practitioners reported a deep bond with TRP and a recognition of the significant positive impact it has on their lives. Many cited an E&L project as their best memory of engaging with TRP. 
The word ‘family’ was used more than once by those consulted. Funky Llama SG members said that, ‘It was much more than a theatre group’. A member of Young Company said of their experience of being in the Sad Club,
‘It has been a really amazing experience and I’ve made so many friends that I love and cherish’.
Participants were overwhelmingly positive about the chance to perform on stage, and The Lyric is seen as an ultimate privilege/opportunity. Many cited the 40th Anniversary production as a highlight and one participant spoke very movingly about their opportunity to be involved with the ‘We are the land’ project as part of the retelling of the story of The Wampanoag.
TRP was described by one participant in People’s Company as, ‘A remedy for isolation’. Another, from Funky Lama Steering Group, said that, 
‘You can be yourself here. You don’t have to put a mask on’. 
One parent from Young Create told us that TRP provided, ‘A weekly lifeline’ for their family and that they would all be, ‘devasted if it (Young Create) were cut’. 
A great deal of praise was heard by the consultants for the work of the practitioners from TRP: comments such as, 
‘TRP are a goldmine of accommodations.’ 
‘The management and facilitation of the group is very good. There is no pressure and yet [the group leaders] enable us to be creative. They take ideas from the community and facilitate with great amounts of skill.’
‘They are quietly skilful, cleverly enabling us to work in small groups.’
Lab Company Alumni felt that TRP was responsible for creating a network of artists in Plymouth through the Lab Company and Assistant Practitioner Scheme: ‘It is very important to me as it builds relationships between creatives as well as an artistic community.’ They said that TRP had also done an incredible job of,‘Bringing in artists from out of the city to guest on the programme’. Lab Company alumni also voiced the importance of the programme and the need for it to continue, as in their view it is,
‘The one talent development programme that is key for young people in Plymouth’.
Members of the freelance community have a huge passion for the work undertaken at TRP. They all feel that the level of professionalism and ‘buy in’ from production staff is a key factor in the success of programmes.
Many participants had enjoyed seeing large productions in The Lyric, including ‘Six the Musical’ and ‘The Ocean at the End of the Lane’. Other productions were also mentioned as highlights, including ‘Of this place’ and ‘Citizen’. Discounted or complimentary tickets to shows in the Drum and/or The Lyric were widely and highly valued. One participant described going to see professional theatre at TRP as, ‘Part of our artistic education’, and that they enjoyed going to see shows in The Drum because it was more ‘edgy’. Many said they would not have been able to afford to go and to see so much (or any at all) if it hadn’t been for the ticket deals offered by TRP.  
The Breaking Convention was cited by more than one interviewee as a moment where a ‘new energy’ was in the building and Plymouth. One participant, who described themselves as a Hip Hop dancer, said of their experience of the BC workshops, 
‘There was something deep about being able to participate in and build skills through the radical and special ethos that TRP sets up.’
External partners all praised the overall approach to learning and engagement and agreed that TRP had an excellent pedagogical approach. Understanding of the overall offer differed, largely depending on the proximity of external partners to Plymouth itself.
A holistic approach to learning about theatre making was echoed by the teacher whose Year 5’s had been involved in With Flying Colours project:
‘This project raised the children’s awareness of other roles in the theatre, not just the actors they see on stage, and it was made clear from the beginning by TRP staff that the children did not have to act and that they could get involved in any of the aspects of playmaking such as script writing, prop-making, music, stage management etc.’ 
One adult participant suggested that TRP could create, ‘An atelier model of learning,’ where people learned from the masters through an apprenticeship with them. 

Issues raised through consultation
	•	Staff and the Board Members interviewed agreed that, due to the breadth and depth of activity across TRP, it is hard for things to be ‘connected’. One participant from Act-up told us that there is a sense of an ‘ecosystem’ of opportunities programmed, but there is a need for more, ‘Fine sewing in between the net’. Some participants said that the website was difficult to navigate to find out about the different community groups that TRP runs.
	•	Freelancers reported that they sometimes feel there is ‘nowhere else’ for participants to go to and that the wider cultural sector in Plymouth is not good at collective working. They expressed a general sense of under-appreciation for the level of work and expertise that the E&L team (of which they feel part) is given. 
	•	Participants cited transport issues with getting to TR2. One participant felt that some kind of shuttle service between the two buildings could ease this sense of physical disconnectedness. 
	•	We note that the E&L team are based at TR2 and as a result have a closer relationship with the production team, which is also based at TR2, than with the departments that are based at the main Theatre.
	•	TRP E&L and Production staff feel that the impact of the E&L work is not fully understood internally and that little is done to communicate this externally. There is a sense that TRP and TR2 are very separate, and this creates silo working and misunderstanding at times.
	•	Most people consulted agreed that Plymouth lacked a coherent set of ‘shared objectives’ around the opportunities/challenges facing people in the city and the wider area. One participant said that there was lots going on in Plymouth but that it was ‘Liminal’ and ‘alternative’ in nature and therefore hard to find out about/access. 
	•	Interviews with Board members revealed that there are over 2,000 CiC’s in Plymouth, which we think is a very large amount of social capital which could be harnessed.
	•	Most agreed that there is a greater opportunity for collaborative working across partners and sectors, but this is hard to achieve with the current relationships/structures.
	•	‘Could you deliver it? It's like it always seems such a shame to me, that we're not talking to those artists that are working in those areas to say, well if you've got on your backburner, what ideas have you got that you are thinking of that you would love to bring to fruition? And is there a Venn diagram of your ideas and the cultural strategic organisations thinking?’



	•	Participants (and parents/carers) described the potential impact of loss of programmes activity as being ‘catastrophic’ and that they ‘don’t know what they would do if it was lost’. This sentiment was echoed by participants in several groups including Young Create and People’s Company. 
	•	We heard from participants, parents/carers and teachers that provision of activities for YP and adults is not good in Plymouth, especially for those on low incomes or with disabilities. Young Create parents/carers had said that they had felt the lack of a TRP summer school this year and had made efforts to enable their children to meet their friends socially in the long break. These parents said that they would be happy to pay for activities led by TRP but had not been asked. 
	•	One Disabled participant said that the summer is a very difficult time for Disabled People as many activities cease. They said that summer is worse for people’s mental health than winter as people are expected to be happy in the summer. 
	•	The teachers interviewed reported that there isn’t much for children and young people from low-income families to do and that there are not many play spaces which are also safe for the children to go to in the city. 
	•	Personal safety was also an issue that the consultants heard several times from different groups and individuals throughout the week. 
	•	Young people also told us that creative arts provision in schools across Plymouth was very varied. We had reports of parents and children travelling distances across the city to attend schools that valued creative arts subjects.
	•	Plymouth can be, ‘a divided city’ around key issues like colonialism and representation of the LGBTQ+ community; TRP is seen as the only agency that can be the interface between differing points of view in a ‘safe space’. 
	•	Clearly there are many untold/hidden stories and voices that are currently underrepresented in and of Plymouth and the surrounding area. Ideas ranged from a story about England’s first Black footballer, Jack Leslie, who played for Plymouth Argyle, to the story of the cutting down of the 100-year-old trees in Plymouth city centre. One young participant suggested that a good idea for a piece of theatre could be, ‘If the Sealy Tower could talk what would it say?’ and another suggested the idea of a ‘Devonian Barbie’. The story of Plymouth’s connection with the Slave Trade was mentioned by more than one participant, with one person saying that they feel uncomfortable walking around Plymouth because of the links to colonialism and Drake’s involvement in the Slave Trade. One participant thought that TRP had a role to play in making/commissioning more site-specific work that is made in nature, and which included foraging and lighting fires. They said that, 
‘It is up to theatre to increase people’s Literacy of the Land, which if we are not careful will be lost from society’s collective memories.’
	•	Excitement around the opportunities for TR2 to be more of a Learning Centre is tempered by the practicalities. Some felt that the offer needed to be aligned to the skills in the organisation. Some parents/carers of young people in Young Create felt that it would be a great experience for their children to make a show from start to finish making everything in it including props, set and costume. The same parents and carers also thought that TRP could be more proactive in developing roles across the theatre for people with disabilities and that progression routes in theatre were important to them. 
	•	All of the proposed headline recommendations were positively received however there were nuances added including the challenge of distance and resource available for travelling to Plymouth for events, the impact of creating a skills centre on the wider organisation, the level of opportunity of bringing together wider cultural partners and the external resources available to support any new plans. 
	•	Five Key Opportunities
Our interviews with those on the ‘inside’, which included Education and Learning team members, Production Staff, Board Members, Freelance Practitioners and senior staff members, helped us to identify five key opportunities and themes. These were voiced in a variety of ways, from participants and carers considering what other activities they would benefit from to staff recognising both the excellence of previous work and demonstrating a great understanding of the needs of Plymouth and the wider South West. These five opportunities were refined and agreed as a key drivers for discussions with those on the ‘outside’. We sought to test a set of key propositions while allowing for free conversation around opportunities as well as trying to identify ‘route maps’ for success. 
3.1	Excellence in Pedagogy – Define, Celebrate and Develop
When we use the term ‘pedagogy’ in this context we mean the overall approach to learning, engagement and participation at TRP and the set of wraparound considerations applied to all activities. We identified an approach categorised by caring and understanding of participants needs and a high level of professional input from across the TRP team. Emotive words such as ‘love’, ‘family’ and ‘caring’ were all used more than once by participants and echoed by the practitioners when describing their approach. We observe there is a ‘TRP Way’ of doing things which, although not entirely unique, can be seen as best practice nationally. This ‘TRP Way’ is inherently understood by practitioners and staff alike; however it is relatively uncodified and vulnerable to loss if not fully defined and celebrated regularly. The loss of key staff and freelancers could harm this process: consideration is needed in how TRP’s pedagogical practice can be codified and disseminated throughout the existing staff team and indeed used as part of induction for new staff, freelancers and external partners. A greater amplification of the pedagogical approach will (we believe) also support partner relationship development and ultimately increase funding opportunities. 
Our recommendation is to see this as a ‘line in the sand’ moment to stop and reflect on what the ‘TRP Way’ is and to document and share this. This could be in the form of a document, a film, a podcast series or a mix of one or more of these. Alongside this we recommend the ‘TRP Way’ is incorporated into induction plans and referred to when considering recruitment and training. Locally and regionally we believe the opportunity lies in the sharing of this newly codified approach to highlight both excellence and a ‘hard wired’ approach that is based on understanding and collaboration. The emerging and developing Cultural Education Partnership (CEP) is an obvious opportunity for sharing and this sharing could be a catalyst for discussions around joint funding initiatives and cross artform working in ways that have not happened before. Subsequently greater understanding of best practice in an approach to education and learning will allow for pathways to be created for participants that do not always start and end at TRP. 
Recommended immediate next steps
	•	Create an internal working group including E&L team and relevant Associate Directors/Artists for Change
	•	This group to workshop what the ‘TRP Way’ is and how this can be best described.
	•	Set a short-term timeline for completion of this piece of work and consider options for end document/dispersal and how this can be created (consider working with an appropriately aligned HE department to set a ‘live brief’).
	•	Hold conversations with partner HEI’s around the methodology of codifying TRP’s pedagogical approach, begin to develop links which connect with academic study and research.
	•	Share best practice with staff from the Discovery MAT around their approaches to teaching and learning (Thrive approach) and how TRP practitioners are working in a complimentary way to this. Explore how this knowledge exchange can be shared more widely (possibly through the PCEP).

3.2	TR2 as a place for practical, accredited skills development
TR2 is already a key asset for TRP and indeed the City of Plymouth. Our consultation (both internally and externally) suggests that it could become an even more important part of skills development for the whole of the South West. Currently operating as the production centre for TRP and external clients, but also supporting the work of the E&L team on an ad hoc basis, TR2 has the potential to grow into an alternative education centre focused on practical production skills including carpentry, electrics, set and costume design and more, all through the lens of theatre production. There is a clear understanding that YP in Plymouth face some serious challenges for employment and skills development and that there is no overall focal point for the types of skills described above. These are clearly transferable across a range of disciplines and provide a pathway to employment not served by traditional education providers. There are a number of routes to achieve the ambition, from being an alternative education provider and receiving referrals from the local authority (amongst others) to a purpose built new space on the site of TR2 with fully accredited courses overseen by regular Ofsted reviews. Skills team members at Plymouth City Council also discussed the possibility of developing a ‘community levy’ or similar funding stream to support skills development in Plymouth including this work.
Our recommendation is to take an iterative approach that allows time to explore the best options whilst action research takes place. To this end we recommend first undertaking a feasibility study to show the breadth and depth of opportunities and challenges around accredited skills development. Through our conversations we have assessed there is appetite for a partnership approach from both Plymouth City Council and Creative and Cultural Skills. This triumvirate could co-commission a feasibility study that is externally funded to limit risk and provide a platform for growth.
Recommended immediate next steps
	•	Reach out to CC Skills and Skills at Plymouth City Council to follow up on initial conversations.
	•	Sense check an agreed initial direction of travel (creation of a feasibility study).
	•	Internal workshop about the ‘what’ and ‘how’ of possibilities with key TR2 staff.

3.3	An annual celebration of the impact of education and learning in theatre
Building on the strength of the pedagogy at TRP, an annual celebration of the role of Theatre in Education, Learning and Wellbeing could be a game changing addition to the regular programme. An event of this kind would allow TRP to share best practice, host academic research on the subject and create a space for collaborative planning with local and regional partners. The annual celebration could take many forms, from a nationally recognised conference to a locally focused human centred design led event with direct outcomes related to key localised needs and pressures. Some concern was voiced by national partners over the possibility of this being ‘another’ conference added to a busy calendar; however, it was agreed that the specificity of the theme and TRP’s excellence made it a viable possibility. Concerns were also raised about the cost and time implications of travel to and from Plymouth for an event. If this were to become a national event, then likeminded theatres such as Derby Theatre (UK’s only ‘learning theatre’), Contact Manchester (a multi-arts venue that, ‘Invests in the next generation, so everyone can hear what the future has to say) and existing key partners including Stuff and Nonsense, Theatre Iolo, York Theatre Royal and Birmingham Royal Ballet are all ideally placed to provide delegates as well as present at such an event. If the event were purely focused on Plymouth, then Nudge Community Builders, Barbican Theatre, About Face and Plymouth City Council alongside HE and FE institutes would be ideal partners. 
Our recommendation is once again to take an ‘inside/out’ approach by starting with a locally focused event built around education, learning and participation in the arts. This will allow for wider cultural partners including The Box and the Real Ideas Organisation to also take part. Presentations on recent success and programmes of work alongside any relevant academic research (this would have the added benefit for HE institutions to share learning and positive impact on their Research Evaluation Framework and Knowledge Exchange Framework) and presentation of new strategies and data by Plymouth City Council and Local Health providers. Outcomes could include:
	•	Greater understanding of the role of the arts in wider agendas
	•	Networking and future work opportunities for freelance practitioners across a wider range of artforms
	•	Opportunity to share the ‘TRP Way’ pedagogical approach.
	•	Testing opportunity of the format/time/location for future Southwest and/or National Conference/Event
Recommended immediate next steps.
	•	Decide internally a timescale and breadth of ambition for the first event.
	•	Discuss concept at PCEP board meeting and engender support.
	•	Set a feasible date and work backwards to define key milestones and partnerships.

3.4	Rationalisation of programme for a more sustainable future
At the commencement of our study there were around 40 projects either currently ongoing or recently completed by the E&L team. This was seen as both a success and a challenge in terms of workload and stretching of the resources. There was an agreement by most internal consultees that a more streamlined programme of work would benefit the organisation and participants alike, however it was equally agreed that none of the 40 or so projects or programmes were obvious candidates for ceasing. Our conversations with external partners focused on the ‘need’ and ‘opportunity’ for developing and refining the programme. It was also clear that there is not one place or one organisation that holds a comprehensive needs analysis for Plymouth that is up to date and live. This in turn makes it hard for TRP and indeed all cultural organisations in the city to know where best to prioritise activity. The developing CEP seems the most likely and appropriate vehicle for bringing together data and shaping future collaborative efforts across organisations to address need. This could lead to success stories much like those in Derby (c. £4m generated in external funding for partnership work around YP led by consortia including cultural organisations such as Derby Theatre) and Wolverhampton (c. £1m for Wolverhampton: City of Youth Culture) but with the specific needs of the Plymouth community at its heart. We also identified a challenge for TRP in the lack of opportunities to refer participants onto other external organisations and their programmes of work. 
Our recommendation is to create and foster greater understanding and agreement of collective challenges in Plymouth across cultural partners (the CEP is an ideal place to start this) to allow for a spread of activity that allows cultural organisations to ‘refer’ participants more frequently and accurately to other cultural partners. The view of what ‘culture’ is can also be discussed and we would advocate for as wide a view as possible to bring into the conversation on an ongoing basis partner such as Plymouth Argyle Community Trust and Prime Skatepark and indeed anyone working directly with participants most at need through a range of cultural, creative, sporting and community engaged activities. Internally TRP can use this report and the conversations that have started as a springboard for internal check and challenge about the ‘what next’ for the programme of the E&L team. The newly appointed Associate Directors and Artists for Change play a key role in shaping this alongside the Senior Leadership Team and the E & L Team and freelance practitioners that are closest to the programme of work. There is a clear weight of passion for the continuation in some form of programmes of work that are specifically targeted at;
	•	Disabled Young People and Young Adults.
	•	Early Years Foundation Stage children from the most challenged communities
	•	Young People from across Plymouth who want to develop a range of skills for future careers in Theatre.
	•	Adults that are isolated or lonely.
However, this is not an exclusive list, but a reflection of the conversations undertaken and themes most commonly expressed. 
Recommended immediate next steps
	•	Create a task and finish group to rationalise the programme (this group may have potential crossover with ‘TRP Way’ working group) 
	•	Ensure this is aligned with relevant internal and external strategies.
	•	Define key target groups/demographics.

3.5	Using Outreach, Co-creation and Talent Development as a lever for artistic success
TRP is a ‘theatrical powerhouse’ that provides inspiration throughout the year. It also has one of the strongest pedagogies in the UK when considering its work through Education and Learning. With this in mind and through conversations internally and externally we have assessed that TRP is in an ideal position to not only uncover talent (‘Talent is everywhere, opportunity is not’) but also to shape and support that talent for future success. This is currently happening organically but through a more codified approach and cogent pathway we believe this can be enhanced even further. 
The UK’s screen and performance sectors provide many examples of working class talent which has emerged from comparable programmes, such as:
	•	Jack O’Connell (The Television Workshop, Nottingham - ‘We look for the individuality and magic in every young person we train.’) 
	•	Daniel Kaluuya (Anna Sher Theatre, London - ‘Improvisation is at the core of its teaching, combining a community spirit with excellence and professionalism.’) 
	•	Milk Presents (In Good Company/Departure Lounge/Derby Theatre - ‘Starting from what artists actually want rather than assumptions about what they need.’)
We believe a fully defined and comprehensive talent pipeline for theatre makers at TRP (building on the success of the LAB Company) could yield the next Oscar/Olivier/Bafta/Stage award winners from Plymouth. 
Plymouth organisations and individuals we spoke to talked passionately about their city and expressed a desire to see more authentically ‘Plymouth’ stories on stage. When coupled with the talent pipeline approach, this could yield  another ‘Standing at the Sky’s Edge’ or similar production which resonates beyond the community with which it is developed in its expression of universally impactful, contemporary themes with much wider reach to national and global audiences. 
Our recommendation is to create a visual/textual representation of the talent pipeline at TRP. From early years provision through to professional training. At first an internal exercise to fully define the ‘steps’ available for YP to develop their skills but then a guiding tool around gaps that need filling. For example where is the regular opportunity for small developing theatre companies to further enhance their practice (e.g. https://derbytheatre.co.uk/about-us/what-we-do/project-in-good-company/) and what would this look like at TRP. How might the best of new writing and performance be showcased through TRP? We believe there is an opportunity (and indeed the appropriate venues in Plymouth) for TRP to lead on small scale independent theatre festival that showcases the best of talent from the region and beyond, also providing a platform for discussion and creative workshops for company and individual development (examples include the Voila Festival https://www.thecockpit.org.uk/voila_europe_2022 and Departure Lounge https://derbytheatre.co.uk/festival/departure-lounge-2023/).

Recommended immediate next steps
	•	Define which parts of the E&L programme allow for talent development.
	•	Codify and present a ‘talent pipeline’ as is, that shows the opportunity and identifies the gaps.
	•	Consider how someone can go from a ‘standing start’ to be an Oscar/Bafta/Olivier award winner through the support of the TRP programme (including access as an audience member to high quality shows).
	•	Show how this work crosses over with the overall artistic ambition of TRP.

	•	Other Findings
4.1	Quick wins
	•	Strength of internal knowledge. The level and quality of the knowledge and experience of internal staff members presents an opportunity for greater sharing as a way of driving engagement. By utilising an ‘inbound marketing’ approach, TRP could create guides/”how to”’s/masterclasses accessible online which provide a long term resource and a way to engage more audiences. 
	•	Paid-for activities. Some participants and carers suggested they would ‘pay more’ for some activities or indeed pay for currently free activities. This isn’t possible for all participants but by providing more opportunities to donate/give/pay what you want or create tiered payment and bursary schemes TRP could increase income that could be reinvested.
	•	Plymouth Culture as Lead on PCEP development. It was identified that the PCEP is in need of further development and that Plymouth Culture were ideally placed to lead this work. A stronger CEP will enhance all of the recommendations above and provide a platform for collaborative working and joint fundraising. As evidenced by other successful CEP’s throughout the UK.
	•	Greater use of comms to amplify the outcomes from programmes of work. It was identified that the Marketing and Communications team at TRP, although not unwilling, are sometimes not able to externally communicate the success of the E&L programme. There is an opportunity to incorporate into the workplan of the comms team a more regular and enhanced programme of case studies/personal stories/success of programmes communications that support the E&L team.
	•	Increasing term lengths to 12/14 weeks from 10x3 Week Blocks. Some participants, carers and education professionals identified that there was not always the time to do everything in 10 week blocks of activity. By increasing this a greater range of opportunities and more time for reflection could be incorporated. 
	•	Mapping of cultural activity and connections through Understory | onion-collective (onioncollective.co.uk) 
	•	Collective Audience mapping through Audience Agency with all Plymouth NPO’s, define shared objectives across multiple partners. Data collected by all NPO’s and collated and assessed by the Audience Agency is rich in detail and creates opportunities for looking at the wider opportunities across a place. By establishing (if not already in place) a Cultural Marketing Group across the NPO organisations and having ‘data’ as a regular agenda point all partners can gain from collective approaches to audience development. The strength and reach of the E&L team provides a powerful opportunity to reach into communities and support any shared objectives assessed. 



4.2	Structural Issues 
Audiences
Changed expectations and behaviours in wake of pandemic, including:
	•	Increased anxiety about attending venues (particularly among core, older audiences)
	•	Cost of living crisis
	•	Substitution of other (online) activity for theatre going
	•	Disruption to attempts to inculcate theatre-going habits amongst young adults
	•	Changes to ticket buying frequency and timing
TRP’s audience is not congruent with administrative boundaries – what is the role of TRP in audience development and wider efforts to enhance cultural engagement in East Cornwall, other Devon local authorities?
The consultation has explored the extent to which Children, Young People and Families are disproportionately affected by any of the above (in an adverse sense) and what other opportunities might present themselves (in a positive sense)?
Internal
During the period of this study TRP was undertaking internal structural change and recruitment of key artistic team members (Associate Directors and Artists for Change) and how these roles knitted with the E&L team was not clear. It is understood though that a clear crossover in working will be in place and clearly each staff member is supportive of this. These new artistic roles will play a key role in many of the headline opportunities identified and developing close working practice and internal meeting and reporting structures will be key to future success in this area. Under the leadership and guidance of the CEO, Executive Director and Senior Leadership Team ‘good governance’ around the refinement and development of the E&L programme will need to be created to ensure wider teams and board members are kept up to date with developments. This will also support any internal and external communications about future E&L activity and help to ‘close the loop’ on wider understanding of the role of education, learning and participation in the overall success of TRP.
4.3	Wider Stakeholder Issues 
Stakeholders – local
Incoming Labour administration – Like all LA’s Plymouth City Council currently face unprecedented financial challenges. However the current administration are confident they have the plans and resources to navigate this. They are also committed to improving the lives of most at risk members of their society and believe that TRP can contribute significantly to this agenda. Plymouth Growth Board was referenced multiple times as a ‘way in’ to wider discussions around new funding opportunities and support for TRP’s ambitions. CEO James Blackman is ideally placed work with relevant internal staff at PCC to develop presentations and papers to present at this board for approval and wider support.
Royal Navy – Many of the Plymouth based partners we spoke to referred to the Navy’s major influence not only on the city’s economy but on behaviours and expectations of families, schools and training providers. It was also felt that they are ‘not always present’ when key citywide strategic discussions are happening. Fostering a long-term relationship through multiple ways in to the organisation was seen as the best approach and TRP has existing work with forces families to start to build a wider relationship if needed.
Private sector employers – skills and employment structures in Plymouth are tailored to the needs of larger employers in naval/maritime supply chain. The pressure that neighbouring and nearby areas seem to feel to provide evidence of enhanced quality of life as a key element in attraction and retention of talent seems not to weigh so heavily on Plymouth businesses. Is that perception real, or is further work needed between cultural, public and private sector to reflect the role of culture in Plymouth’s economy?
Schools – already suffering serious overstretch in terms of resource and responsibilities as well as being on the frontline for some of the behavioural issues, especially absenteeism, they can’t be the backstop for all Plymouth’s social problems – how can TRP work with them to deliver cultural entitlement in a way that starts to reverse worsening trends?
Plymouth cultural partners have been engaged in attempts to convene joint activities and build a cultural prospectus for the city which is more than the sum of its parts since start of Mayflower 400 project, interrupted and disrupted by pandemic. The exact start date of those endeavours could be traced back to start of planning for The Box and the Plymouth Horizon project in 2016. That is a long time (arguably too long) to go without a clear settlement and shared plan. Signs of collaboration fatigue have (understandably) started to creep in, with some organisations increasingly focused on their own critical path at the expense of joint working. Concerns were repeatedly expressed through consultation that perceived differences in status (reflecting a series of ‘parent-child’ relationships between cultural organisations of different scales) continue to hinder development of a new shared prospectus. 
Stakeholders – regional
‘Cross-border’ issues around resourcing audience development and ‘ownership’ of TRP’s programme. Not clear who has ownership of strategic relationships with any of those local authorities within the corporate structure (accountability and responsibility potentially in conflict) nor what the expectation is that Associates might assume accountability, responsibility or both in the medium term.
ACE SW takes a positive view of James’ recent conversations with other cultural partners across the region: can this be a starting point for a wider regional E&L strategy that creates a pipeline of opportunity for communities?
Does Plymouth CC and/or ACE have a strategy to ensure that existing investment can be sustained and increased post 2026?
Stakeholders – national
The E&L team have formed strong and long lasting partnerships with a number of key national creative partners. All of these provide a stable base for further conversations and indeed ‘positive challenge’ around what is and isn’t feasible in terms of collaborative delivery of E&L outputs. All partners shared a desire to have shared objectives in NPO agreements but also recognised the significant challenge in making this happen due to resource and timing. 
Other questions to consider in more detail:
	•	Even if TRP is robust, are there structural challenges to that ‘circuit’ of producing and receiving houses which could undermine the current system?
	•	TRP shows potential to play a larger role in national conversation about development of craft and making skills (set design, painting, lighting etc) – who will take responsibility for developing that with DCMS ALBs and sector organisations (including Cultural and Creative Skills, Royal Opera House, Production Park) with shared/competing interest in growing talent supply for production services?
