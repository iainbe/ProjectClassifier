Executive summary
Key Findings and Themes
University of Liverpool Heritage CPD: Positioning for Future Success
The University of Liverpool's commitment to enhancing and expanding its Continuing Professional Development (CPD) offerings in the field of Heritage is a strategic imperative in today’s rapidly evolving educational landscape. This report presents a comprehensive analysis and strategic recommendations for the further development and differentiation of Heritage CPD courses, consultancy and professional services. The recommendations also connect to the wider strategic ambition of the Heritage Research Institute and widening participation in higher education. 
Key Findings:
The Heritage sector is experiencing significant growth, underscored by the increasing importance of digital heritage, environmental sustainability, and effective marketing communications.
Our research identifies a marketplace crowded with generic CPD offerings. University of Liverpool has wide expertise across different departments in areas of heritage and there is a wide range of offers from universities throughout England and Scotland. 
Further analysis highlighted that, with few exceptions, Liverpool’s and other universities’ CPD offers are undifferentiated, poorly collated and marketed.
There is an evident demand for specialised courses tailored to emerging needs, including:
Archiving and Digital Information Management
Institutional approaches to reconciling ‘Difficult’ Heritage in areas such as ESG, reputation and risk management, supply chain management and Marketing and Communications 
Digital Heritage
Application of sustainability practices developed through a conservation approach in both heritage and commercial development
Architectural imaging and mixed reality
Consultation identified considerable expertise within and beyond HSS and enthusiasm to develop and grow the University’s offer through CPD. There exists a substantial opportunity for the University to differentiate itself by leveraging its unique strengths and we are confident that there is a high probability of identifying commercial demand.
However, the capacity of academics and support staff to develop and deliver any new courses emerged as a major constraint. Throughout our consultation, there were repeated reference to time and resource constraints on senior academics and the lack of a single, clear path within the University to developing the delivery and business model to support it. 
Comparative Insights:
The University of Liverpool’s offerings are comprehensive but face stiff competition from institutions specialising in niche areas.
Opportunities lie in creating unique, specialised courses that leverage Liverpool’s strengths, such as its research capabilities or partnerships.
Strategic Implications: To remain competitive, the University of Liverpool should consider:
Exploring Transferable Knowledge and Skills: The growing trend towards the integration of digital tools and ethical considerations in heritage studies opens up opportunities for their exploitation in other industry sectors.
Enhancing Technology Integration: Leveraging digital tools and technologies to offer cutting-edge courses, consultancy and professional services.
Building Strategic Partnerships: Collaborating with industry and community organisations for practical and impactful learning experience
Feasibility Analysis for New Heritage CPD Courses
The report analyses the feasibility of launching new Heritage CPD courses, focusing on market demand, potential revenue, cost analysis, and the competitive landscape.
It includes an assessment of the University's strengths and resources, such as its research capabilities and strategic partnerships, and how these can be leveraged to create a compelling CPD offering.
We concluded that there was a clear pathway for a market test of one product – Digital Asset Management – which offered a good ‘fit’ with the University’s distinctive capability around archiving and addressed an identified business need not met by existing offers from either universities or private training firms. This is a growing market in the United States but little on offer in the UK. 
The report sets out a proposed two-part course structure that would build on existing University relationships with senior decision makers in industry and public sector organisations, setting out the need for and benefits of Digital Asset Management and pointing to a longer term engagement with specialisms including archiving as a way to increase the lifetime value of those client relationships. Alongside work with external bodies to identify and target board level executives for an initial stage of the market test, there is an internal requirement for further work with colleagues across Departments, in the Business School and in marketing and administration to establish feasibility.
Strategic Recommendations:
Develop a Half-Day 'Knowledge Centre': A free to attend event for Board Directors of companies and public sector bodies which offers an introductory gateway highlighting the University’s USPs in archiving methodologies and digital asset management and their potential application across a range of commercial and institutional settings.
The Knowledge Centre can act as a ‘doorway’ for other commercial offers:
Launch a Specialised Three-Day Course for CIOs: Focused on digital heritage management, this course will cater to the specific needs of Chief Information Officers, combining theoretical knowledge with practical applications.
Extend 'Closed' Consultancy Offers: By providing tailored consultancy services, the University can address specific organizational challenges, thereby establishing long-term partnerships and continuous learning opportunities.
Explore Degree Apprenticeships: These programmes can bridge the gap between academic knowledge and practical skills, offering a unique blend beneficial to both students and professionals in the heritage sector. They hold out the potential of widening participation to students for whom the perceived costs and risks of a conventional three-year undergraduate degree are unacceptable, and at higher levels of presenting a more certain route into employment post graduation.
The University of Liverpool is well positioned to lead in the Heritage CPD space. Development of the Heritage Research Institute demonstrates a unique commitment to the exploration of cross-disciplinary application of heritage knowledge and skills, in many cases augmented by leading edge technology, across business and civil society. By implementing these strategic recommendations, the University can not only meet the current market demand but also anticipate future trends, thereby ensuring its continued relevance and leadership in this field.
Introduction
Purpose of the Report
This report has been commissioned by the University of Liverpool to undertake a comprehensive evaluation of its Continuing Professional Development (CPD) offerings in the field of heritage studies. The Department of Humanities and Social Sciences wished to identify opportunities for enhancement, diversification, and strategic positioning of its offerings in the current educational and professional landscape. Although this had a focus on market demand, resource allocation, and potential revenue streams, its primary objective was engaging in Knowledge Exchange via consultancy and CPD to support the institutional mission, to complement and enhance research and teaching and help the University meet its strategic objectives.
This endeavour aligns with the University's commitment to maintaining its status as a leader in heritage education and to responding proactively to evolving market needs. In particular, it reflects the multidisciplinary and pan-University ambitions for the new Heritage Research Institute.
The Heritage Research Institute at the University of Liverpool will be a university-level cross-disciplinary institute which will bring together researchers from the diverse disciplines currently engaged with the broad theme of ‘heritage’, as well as identifying new researchers and disciplines. The focus will be to enhance the University’s international reputation and status as a pre-eminent research institution, postgraduate research and training base, and outreach in the field of heritage, its management and interpretation. It will also aim to have closer ties and overlaps with the other University research themes (materials; diseases; health; digital and wellbeing).  
Scope of the Report
The scope of this report encompasses:
Analysis of Current Offerings: Reviewing the existing consultancy, training and professional services in heritage-related areas offered by the University, focusing on content, delivery methods, and market reception.
Competitive Landscape: Conducting a comparative analysis of similar offerings from other leading universities to identify market trends and gaps.
Feasibility Study: Assessing the potential market demand for new commercial offerings and the practicality of introducing new CPD courses or modifying and recombining existing curriculum elements to furnish one. 
Operational Capacity and Available Resource: who would potentially develop and deliver CPD courses, what support would they need and does the University have the systems and resources to manage this activity?
Strategic Recommendations: Providing actionable recommendations for course development, marketing strategies, and potential partnerships.
Methodology
The methodology adopted for this report includes:
Desk Research: Comprehensive review of existing literature, course materials, and market analysis reports to understand the current state of heritage CPD, training and professional service offerings.
Stakeholder Interviews: Engaging with key stakeholders including faculty members, industry experts, and current customers to gain insights into the effectiveness and reception of the current offer in heritage-related areas.
Market Analysis: Drawing on multiple tools and frameworks for analysing market trends, competitor offerings, and potential opportunities.
Best Practice Benchmarking: Drawing on successful examples and case studies from other institutions and industries to inform recommendations.
This report aims to provide the University of Liverpool with a clear, evidence-based pathway to enhance its Heritage CPD offerings, ensuring they remain relevant, competitive, and aligned with the University's strategic goals.
Analysis of Current Heritage CPD Offerings 
Overview of Liverpool’s CPD Heritage Offer
The University of Liverpool currently offers a diverse range of consultancy and professional services in the field of heritage. We found some 50 areas of research across the university which map against the four themes of ‘Difficult’ Heritage, Digital Heritage, Environmental Heritage, and Heritage Marketing and Communications. 
These offerings are characterised by their focus on integrating theoretical knowledge with practical applications. Within each of these themes, there is a spectrum of opportunity between:	
Policies, technologies and processes of heritage management and conservation.	
Heritage futures – ways in which the management and communication of heritage can have wider (often customer-facing) organisational impact.
Strengths
Expert Faculty and Research Excellence: the University boasts a strong faculty with expertise in various heritage-related fields, including photogrammetry, modern slavery and other recognised technical and academic specialisms.
Interdisciplinary Approach: The scope of Liverpool’s current CPD heritage offer includes a range of knowledge, skills and client relationships across architecture, archives, languages, environmental science, planning whose scope and capability extends across and beyond HSS. Courses often combine insights from different disciplines, offering a holistic view of heritage.
Practical Learning: Many courses include fieldwork or case study analyses, providing hands-on experience.
Unique offers: UoL has a USP around archives, both as a discipline in which Liverpool has a distinctive offer (one of only two UK HEIs with a course) and in the experience of working with existing corporate archives (Cunard, Unilever).
Opportunities
Technology Integration: UoL has developed distinctive capacity and excellence in areas such as architectural imaging, digital heritage, mixed reality, DIF, supporting both delivery of heritage projects, MLA research (‘library as laboratory’) and Createch R&D (CAVA) – but this is not managed or promoted as part of an integrated offer. As digital heritage gains prominence, there is an opportunity to further integrate cutting-edge technologies into the curriculum and professional services.
Focus on customer needs: Evidence of engagement with Tate, Cunard, Unilever offers proof of concept that this is a product with measurable ROI, not just relevant to heritage professionals.
Liverpool’s own (dark) heritage: Access to its own collections and to corporate archives (Cunard, Tate, Unilever) has provided notable case studies.
Liverpool as a ‘living lab’ The University’s engagement with communities and institutions in and around Liverpool demonstrates that archives and heritage are ‘living’ things, not static artefacts.
International recognition and reputation: UoL academics are already working in EMEA and China; Assessment & Management Centre partnership with WHO; ICOMOS, UNESCO links.
Technological expertise and capacity: including Quartex, a proprietary system licensed by the University which provides a distinctive combination of a powerful and versatile digital repository that can handle a wide range of digital assets and formats and a content management system which allows the creation of bespoke web-based user environments that enable libraries and archives to powerfully showcase, share and celebrate their archival materials in intuitive and visually engaging ways. Extensively used in North America and a handful of UK institutions, UoL is an early adopter amongst UK universities of its use in managing both its own collections and archives and those of clients.
Institutional capacity: UoL has access to commercial knowledge and industry collaborations
Institutional recognition: this area of work is a rich source of profile raising for both University and client through activities such as publication, industry visits and guest speaking opportunities.
Income generation to support research and teaching. 
Knowledge exchange: UoL has distinctive capability to add to company capacity through KTPs, live student briefs and other mechanisms which do not add to end users’ payroll.
Areas for Improvement
Market Differentiation: Despite the quality of offerings, there is a need for more distinctive courses that set the University apart in a crowded market (e.g., modern slavery).
Coordination of the offer: Consultation identified other offers from within and beyond HSS but very little connection between Departments and researchers. They also highlighted the challenges of interdisciplinary collaboration within the university and the potential benefits of establishing a Heritage Research Institute to conjoin and prioritise between offers. 
Focusing on commercial demand and customer needs: In many cases, University colleagues’ current efforts were focused on expanding existing offers to a market of heritage professionals. That is not a criticism; but the purpose of our study was to identify other areas which offered potential both for commercial exploitation and to address teaching, research and knowledge transfer objectives. Developing those new opportunities places a premium on listening to clients and contextualising their needs in order to effectively showcase and utilise heritage skills. We found limited evidence that this had taken place either within UoL nor at other HEIs with comparable expertise – with the result that the market is populated almost entirely by ‘push’ offers.
Resource constraints: Concerns about resource (time, staff, cash) to develop and deliver external courses are inhibiting innovation. Incentives and recognition need to be aligned with other teaching, research and knowledge exchange activities and reflected in both departmental budgets and staff contracts. Any attempt to implement CPD needs to consider practical support for members of staff looking to develop those offers – insurance, pricing guidance, billing control etc.

Lack of customer focus
As researchers, we are not equipped to say whether the heritage offers of other Universities are good, bad, better or worse than those of UoL. However, nor are most of the non-heritage clients in a position to make that judgement – their purchasing decisions will be made on grounds of the apparent ‘fit’ of the offer with their commercial and professional needs. Our analysis highlighted the fact that, with few exceptions, Liverpool’s and other universities’ CPD offers are undifferentiated, poorly collated and marketed. Other HEI offers share the same shortcoming: they focus on what the academic staff can provide and not on what the customer needs.
This is reflected in the difficulty of finding information about available CPD and consultancy offers. Details tend to be buried within Departmental websites, and their presentation is inconsistent between Departments within the same HEI as well as between institutions. None of the institutions examined had a marketing led and/or customer focused approach to Heritage CPD or consultancy. Only one HEI – Cardiff – collated data about all available offers in one area of its web site; but even this was presented as a ‘catalogue’ rather than engaging with the benefits to clients. (see Appendix 2: Cardiff case study).
Comparative Observations:
Market Trends: A shift towards incorporating advanced technology in heritage studies is noted. This is an opportunity for Liverpool to position the University as a leader in digital heritage by leveraging its technological resources and research capabilities.
Market Saturation: The market is saturated with courses focusing on general heritage management. This presents an opportunity for the University of Liverpool to focus on niche areas.
Digital Heritage: There is a growing trend towards digital heritage courses, an area where the University of Liverpool can leverage its technological resources.
Sustainability: Environmental sustainability in heritage conservation is a relatively untapped area that could be a key differentiator for the University.
Customisation and Flexibility: Most offerings are either short intensive courses or longer modular ones. There is potential for offering flexible, customised courses to cater to diverse professional needs.
This comparative analysis underscores the need for the University of Liverpool to position its Heritage CPD offerings more strategically and in the eyes of a wider range of potential clients. By focusing on unique aspects such as digital information management, sustainability practices, and customised learning experiences, the University can establish itself as a leader in this evolving field.
Areas of potential competitive advantage for UoL
One clear potential advantage for University of Liverpool is its ability to effectively communicate an offer which is interdisciplinary.
The Heritage Research Institute embodies an institutional commitment to both interdisciplinarity and, ‘outreach in the field of heritage, its management and interpretation.’
A second potential advantage lies in the University’s ability to demonstrate that its offer is focused on customer need. 
This aligns with the mission of HRI to, ‘initiate relationships with clients and stakeholders,’ rather than to ‘push’ products and services.
Looking across other institutions with a strong presence in the humanities, it is clear that clients and funders attach importance to the personality and key connections of the academics involved in delivery. For example, Prof Kirsty Fairclough at Manchester Met’s School of Digital Arts (SODA) has built a higher profile for her publications and her institution than UoL’s Institute of Popular Music, even though the latter is more established and can boast both an impressive track record of research and a cadre of recognised popular musicians amongst its teaching staff. Here again, the Heritage Research Institute represents a distinctive platform and showcase for the excellence of UoL’s faculty. Finding academics who are prepared to identify themselves with the Heritage CPD offer – and supporting that in the same way that the Uni might support publication or a research sabbatical, with time and resource – is likely to be a critical success factor, and something which could be a priority for HRI.

Identifying other market opportunities for UoL Heritage expertise
In many cases, University colleagues were focused on expanding those existing offers to a market of heritage professionals. Although this all of these have an appeal to those already working in heritage, we felt that there was a greater opportunity to test commercial demand for services. The main focus of the feasibility study was on areas with capacity to attract new commercial clients from outside the heritage sector. 
These can be summarised as ‘Red Ocean vs Blue Ocean’ strategies.
Red Ocean vs Blue Ocean strategy
The concept of red and blue oceans is widely used to help businesses identify market opportunities. 
Red Oceans: A red ocean is an existing market with identified demand but many competitors. 
It represents an obvious option and the ‘state of the art’ in the market; but requires extensive investment in product development and marketing to develop both ‘features’ and ‘benefits’ which will differentiate your product from that of your competitors. 
Both desk research and consultation provided examples of expertise, skills and client relationships from HSS and other Departments in areas including Photogrammetry, Difficult Heritage, Digital Heritage, Digital Imaging, Environmental Heritage and CSG (e.g., in understanding the legacy of slavery on supply chains).
In many cases, University colleagues’ current efforts were focused on expanding existing offers to a market of heritage professionals. 
We took modern slavery as a case study of a ‘red ocean’ (see below). Given the strength of UoL’s offer and its engagement with clients locally and internationally, we wanted to establish whether modern slavery was an area in which, although well serviced by other provides, the University could establish a competitive advantage.  
Figure 2  Case study of a 'Red Ocean': Modern Slavery
A blue ocean is a market yet to be discovered with few or no competitors. A blue ocean strategy, 
‘Redefines how businesses can look at success and offers a unique approach to growth’
There is a large amount of risk associated with blue ocean initiatives. It is therefore best approached by organisations which can demonstrate a serious commitment to aligning their efforts and prioritising transparency.
Our analysis of potential differentiators, including archives and information management, led us to conclude that that there was a greater opportunity to test commercial demand for services around Digital Asset Management.
Given the uniqueness of UoL’s archives offer, and mindful of the Heritage Research Institute’s commitment to working with partners to delivery greater transparency in the understanding of heritage and application of specialist heritage skills to areas beyond conservation and culture, we felt that this was a good ‘fit’. 
Whilst the quality of other existing offers is not called into question, the strength of existing competition in those markets would mean that any attempt to develop and market those offers would be expensive and call upon human resource for which we weren’t able to identify any spare capacity within either HSS or the wider institution.

Risk Assessment
Market Risks: The primary risk involves the potential fluctuation in market demand, influenced by economic factors and changing professional training priorities.
Competitive Risks: There is a risk of other institutions quickly adopting similar offerings, thus increasing competition.
Mitigation Strategies: Diversifying the course portfolio, constant market monitoring, and establishing robust partnerships can mitigate these risks.
Strategic Recommendations for Expanding Heritage CPD Offerings
Digital Asset Management presents further potential to partner with other professional organisations both in the heritage sector (Digital Preservation Coalition), public sector IT managers (SOCITM) and marketing and communications businesses (IPA) to develop joint offers and marketing to their members.
We have focused the market test options on CIOs, as they are likely to have board-level influence both on digital strategy and procurement of digital systems; but during the course of the research, we also saw potential for a more limited offer with specific relevance to marketing and communications professionals (both client-side and agency).
Whilst there is demand for other products – Modern Slavery, photogrammetry – it isn’t clear that UoL has sufficient competitive advantage over other HEIs, nor the marketing resource to establish itself in a crowded marketplace, to justify focusing on these. (See ‘Red Ocean vs Blue Ocean’, above.)

Identify a clear pathway for market testing of one product – Digital Asset Management – to include a Knowledge Centre style introduction.
Use existing partners and relationships to market and test

Address internal time and financial constraints (potentially by aligning CPD with teaching, KE and other activities),

Expand use of undergraduates, post-graduates and Early Career Researchers to connect to business demand.

Delivering the Market Test
1. Half-Day 'Knowledge Centre'
We set out below ‘Fast Track’ process to develop and test a Digital Asset Management product through a half-day Knowledge Centre.
The Knowledge Centre provides a short ‘taster’ to introduce decision makers to the risks associated with their archive and the benefits of working with University of Liverpool to implement digital archive management. It offers an initial, accessible entry point into the University of Liverpool’s Heritage CPD offerings. It needs to identify a clear product (either assembling existing modules or modifying content from those to form the course offer) which introduces and showcase the depth of the University's expertise in heritage studies and digital asset management.
The Knowledge Centre is:
Focused on senior executives (‘C-Suite’) and significant stakeholders.
Presented as a networking opportunity, or in context of another professional/sector event.
As we are looking to use this to benefit from information, this should be free to attend.
The objectives of this session include:
A call to action for senior executives to realise the value of their archive.
To provide more detailed feedback on which elements of digital archive management should be prioritise in development of short course CPD.
To identify managers within their organisation who could commit more time to development of digital archive management strategy and practice through CPD.
Establish willingness to pay and price elasticity (testing different price points).
Introducing and orienting potential clients to a broader range of CPD courses and opportunities.

Conclusion: The Strategic Importance of Expanded Heritage CPD Offerings
Recap of Strategic Importance
The expansion of the Heritage CPD offerings at the University of Liverpool is a strategic move that aligns with the evolving research and technological capabilities of the heritage sector, the increased awareness amongst commercial and institutional clients of heritage-related risks and the broader educational landscape. The proposed initiatives – from the Knowledge Centre to degree apprenticeships – are designed not only to enhance the University's educational portfolio but also to address critical skill gaps in the heritage sector. By implementing these offerings, the University will reinforce its position as a leader in heritage education, contribute to the professional development of individuals in this field, and foster sustainable partnerships with industry stakeholders. 
Final Thoughts
The introduction of new, specialised Heritage CPD courses at the University of Liverpool is a feasible venture with a promising market demand. While initial costs and competitive risks present challenges, the potential for revenue generation and the strategic alignment with emerging market needs make this a worthwhile investment. Continual assessment and adaptive strategies will be key to ensuring the long-term success and sustainability of these offerings.
The conclusion we draw from the findings is that there is a high probability that a well-designed market test could identify and articulate the need for a Digital Asset Management product across a range of sectors and help translate that into demand for a CPD offer which University of Liverpool is uniquely positioned to deliver. 
However, there is no single, clear path to developing the delivery and business model to support it. 
The factors influencing that conclusion are:
There is no precedent for Heritage CPD (as distinct from ‘closed’ consultancy offers) within the University.
There are benefits to introducing an interdisciplinary approach to design and delivery of digital asset management which goes beyond capacity and skills of HSS and engages other Departments. 
We were not able to identify an existing business model or marketing support pathway for CPD from other Departments, or the Business School, which could be applied to Heritage CPD.
Whilst some of the content which could be delivered as CPD exists in modular form, there is no shared mechanism across Departments to translate this into professional short course development.
Resource constraints – time and resource for CPD would be additional to academics’ current commitments for teaching and research.
Design and implementation of any CPD needs to be done with an awareness of the critical need for continuous adaptation to market trends and feedback in maintaining the relevance and impact of these courses.
