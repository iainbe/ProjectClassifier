SPECIFICATION OF REQUIREMENT 

1. Background

Creative Scotland supports the cultural and creative sectors across all parts of Scotland. We support culture and creativity in Scotland as a development organisation, a funder, an advocate, and as a public body that seeks to influence others to increase opportunity and maximise the impact our resources can offer.  As a Non-Departmental Public Body, we are sponsored by Scottish Government and Scottish Ministers, with funding from both the Scottish Government and the National Lottery. We distribute funding from the Scottish Government and The National Lottery.

Since 2025 we have been working to refresh our long-term Strategy, evolving the current strategic framework that has been in place from 2021. This work includes revising our approach to supporting Scotland’s Creative Industries and reviewing our current open access funding programmes. These projects will be central to our work to respond to the Independent Review of Creative Scotland, commissioned by the Scottish Government and published in November 2025. 

To support these changes, we will be developing a new Performance Management Framework, including the Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) we use to report on our own performance. We have identified an opportunity to better articulate and monitor both the impact of Scotland’s creative and cultural sector and the contribution Creative Scotland’s support makes to this impact. 

Our requirement is for research that examines the potential for new dimensions of impact. These will provide tools for Creative Scotland to understand how and where the work it facilitates contributes to Scotland’s Wellbeing Economy. This work starts with a framing of the creative economy as defined in NESTA’s A Manifesto for the Creative Economy. As such, innovation and the creative workforce are key elements for consideration, in addition to contributions from businesses classified within the creative industries.

Several policy developments are specifically relevant to this work: 

	•	The context of Scotland's National Strategy for Economic Transformation (NSET) which, in part, reorients economic policy toward wellbeing and fair work. The Scottish Government’s Wellbeing Economy measures follow from this grounding and are organised against the Four Capitals (Financial, Social, Natural and Human) at a national and local level. 

	•	Expected changes to Scotland’s National Performance Framework (NPF). The current NPF, including the full list of national indicators, has been archived and the Scottish Government has developed draft NPF proposals for a streamlined framework focused on six outcomes: Connected; Healthy; Secure; Prosperous; Skilled and Sustainable.

	•	The identification of the creative industries as a strategic area for economic focus by the UK Government in the recent Creative Industries Sector Plan - GOV.UK recognising that they ‘play a unique role in both an economic and social sense’.

	•	The Scottish Government’s Public Service Reform agenda that aims to deliver service provision that is preventative, joined up and efficient - in order to better deliver for people. It sets out how we will tackle systemic barriers to change.

	•	A Culture Strategy for Scotland, which recognises that Scotland’s culture and creative industries generate cultural and social value, which includes harnessing the transformational power of culture to deliver on climate change priorities.

	•	The Public Services Reform (Scotland) Act 2010 which instigated the creation of Creative Scotland, and which gives it six purposes including “promoting and supporting industries and other commercial activity the primary focus of which is the application of creative skills.”

This project looks to investigate and make visible the contribution of arts, culture and creative industries within Scotland’s Wellbeing Economy. We are interested in the potential to understand this cultural and social value more granularly within this framework, and the relationship and interplay with economic measures. We are considering the following research questions:

	•	Is the framing of ‘cultural’ value and ‘social’ value sufficient? Should other framings be considered for inclusion, such as ‘wellbeing’; ‘thriving’, ‘social connection’, in the context of Scottish Government policy and developing external models for impact measurement?
	•	What datasets are available that can provide this information already? 
	•	How can they be combined to provide meaningful insights?
	•	What data is missing, and how might this be generated or collected?
	•	What can we see over different time horizons? Are there meaningful patterns that might become visible over longer periods of time? 

We invite bidders to consider the logics of economic policy and cultural policy in tandem and help us find the shared area where intended positive public outcome may exist (see Cultural & Innovation Policies | ekip Engine for a useful discussion) and appear in existing data.

Scotland's creative economy is unique, and these key features may suggest areas for enquiry around its contribution:

	•	a highly educated workforce, supported by strengths in our further and higher educational institutions, but one that has significant entrenched inequalities of access.
	•	significant geographic clusters of production but wider national distribution of delivery and consumption.
	•	businesses in Scotland’s creative industries are predominantly small, micro and nano size.
	•	across the country especially in rural and remote communities are significant local anchors for tourism and sites of community cohesion.
	•	creative economy workers are often self-employed, and potentially vulnerable actors in the economy.
	•	many creative businesses operate inclusive and democratic business models (ICBMs), with any of them are key parts of the human and social infrastructure of their local communities. 
	•	challenges around productivity – Baumol’s cost disease was originally modelled on the performing arts, and the issues have not changed since its original modelling in the 60s. 
	•	creative businesses and people are framers of shared imagination which make a significant contribution to how we are understood externally and how we understand ourselves and the things that matter to us locally, nationally and internationally.
 
The Scottish Government is already monitoring progress against its Wellbeing Economy ambitions. A Wellbeing Economy Monitor is published annually. We note that there are two local impact measures are present in the current Wellbeing Economy Monitor (2025 p48-50): 
	•	12.4 Attendance at cultural events and places of culture and 
	•	12.5 Participation in a cultural activity. 
These are considered as part of the ‘Local wellbeing: human capital’ indicators and we welcome their inclusion. This work should tie these measures to questions noted above and enrich our understanding of their impact.

This research should supplement such existing measures and support Creative Scotland to understand the contribution of the work it supports (through its funding, advocacy and development) to the long-term thriving of Scotland’s Wellbeing Economy. This is proposed in the spirit of supporting the NSET commitment to a Culture of Delivery help identify areas of the creative economy where the Scottish Government might 'double down' on things that are shown to work (NSET p8).


2. Our Requirement 

Creative Scotland requires a researcher(s), organisation or consortium that can develop a framework for identifying and measuring impacts grounded in discourses of wellbeing economics, relating to the cultural and creative sectors in Scotland. There are three core strands to this project:

	•	A review of existing datasets and available literature on modelling the various public value impacts of the creative economy in Scotland at present. This should also include an exploration of alternative approaches to measuring value from other sectors and their relevance as models (e.g. SCAPE’s Social Value in Construction matrix) as relevant.

	•	The development of an objective framework for measuring impacts relating to arts, culture and creativity in Scotland that are relevant across policy areas (e.g. economic, health and wellbeing, environmental, local, cultural etc.)
	•	The framework should align with Creative Scotland’s refreshed strategy (this work is ongoing), and it should also be replicable across the wider the cultural and creative sectors landscape in Scotland (for example, align to the new Scottish Government NPF).

	•	A recommendation of how best to measure against the proposed impact framework (the relevant data that should be gathered, how this should be gathered, and the best methods for collecting and aggregating qualitative information that supports the measurement framework). The first two strands will identify gaps in existing datasets and measures, as well as highlighting existing datasets and measures that can be utilised more effectively.

Updating the conceptual underpinnings of our impact: With the upcoming changes to Creative Scotland’s Strategy, which will include a commitment to developing the creative economy and Scottish Government NPF in mind, there is an opportunity to review the various dimensions of impact and to develop a framework that allows Creative Scotland (and others) to better understand and measure outcomes.

A Review of Existing Datasets and Literature

The first stage of the project is to review available datasets and literature to better understand how existing data and qualitative information can demonstrate the range of impacts from the activities of the creative economy.

Examples of datasets are:
	•	Data related to Creative Scotland funding awards (this can be provided by Creative Scotland). This includes data on the allocation of Creative Scotland awards and annual data from Multi Year Funded Organisations. 
	•	Creative Scotland research and evaluations
	•	Our Creative Voice campaign materials 
	•	National data and monitoring from Scottish Government such as;
	•	The Scottish Household Survey
	•	Scotland's Wellbeing Economy Monitor
	•	Scottish Government Growth Sector Statistics
	•	Anonymised geodemographic cultural audiences’ data in Scotland from The Audience Agency
	•	Regional Economic Strategies:
	•	South of Scotland Regional Economic Strategy
	•	Highland & Islands Regional Economic Strategy
	•	Other Open Source datasets

Models of best practice and impact measurements for the cultural and creative sectors could be drawn upon from other sources, as well as reviewing available literature, for example:
	•	Doughnut Economics
	•	The Common Good Economy (with other UCL Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose such as The Public Value of Arts and Culture (see p24-25 in particular)
	•	Art Cure: The Science of How the Arts Transform Our Health
	•	The Care Economy
	•	Assessing the Economic Impact of the Arts on Health and Healthcare Services in Wales
	•	Centre for Cultural Value's Cultural Indicator Suite
	•	The Community Wealth Building (Scotland) Bill and Creative Scotland's Community Wealth Building Toolkit 
	•	Culture for Climate Scotland’s Environmental reporting update


Development of an objective framework for measuring impacts relating to arts, culture and creativity in Scotland

A framework for measuring the impacts of the creative economy in Scotland is required across the various dimensions of impact, which may include, but are not limited to, wellbeing, social, economic, community, environmental and cultural impacts. We note there are good practice guidelines for measuring social benefits described in The Green Book which may be useful for some areas of impact.

This framework should align to a refreshed strategy for Creative Scotland, centred on creatively ambitious and economically sustainable sectors in Scotland which broaden public engagement with culture as audience members, participants or consumers. It should also consider key policy commitments to:
	•	Equalities, Diversity and Inclusion: Supporting a diverse range of creative people, communities, and activity, promoting an equality of opportunity to create, participate and engage
	•	Sustainable Development: Helping tackle the climate emergency and supporting the growth of sustainable creative businesses across Scotland
	•	Fair Work: Promoting fair pay, conditions, and employment opportunities across the creative sector.
	•	Children and Young People: to realise the potential of arts and culture for children and young people.
	•	Scotland’s Languages: including commitments to Gaelic and Scots under the 2025 Scottish Languages Act

As well as this, the proposed framework for measuring impacts should be replicable across the cultural and creative sectors in Scotland and, where possible, align to The Scottish Government’s refreshed National Performance Framework.


How to Measure Against the Proposed Framework

We understand that we may not currently have all of the relevant data points and information to measure against the new proposed framework. The successful bidder will identify knowledge gaps and recommend how best to close these gaps; data to gather, the questions to be asked and future research requirements, for example. It is not expected at this point that we will have all of the components to measure against the new framework. Instead, the framework should inform the data and information that we currently have and how it can be better utilised, as well as identifying gaps to inform future improvements to measure and evaluate the impacts.
