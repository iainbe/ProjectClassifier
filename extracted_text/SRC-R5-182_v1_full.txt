LLDC Place Narrative Stakeholder Interviews
Juliet Can, Director of Stour Space (Hackney Wick)
Tell me a bit about how Stour Space formed and ended up in your current location 

Stour Space was started in early 2008. Her and the other co-founders were artists and makers in the Fish Island area are were struggling to find their own space. The space had been empty for over a decade and they approached the landlord to take it on and lease it to provide affordable workspace for creatives. It later became clear there was a need for a space to connect the local community around wider changes happening in the area.
What are the main positives expressed about the Park by your main audiences?
In 2009 their location wasn’t a positive, but they were lucky to be by the canal. They were happy to have a large affordable venue to rent and the proximity to the water was valuable in terms being able to ‘get away’ – ‘there’s something about the water that’s very calming’.  At the time they started there there’s wasn’t much infrastructure of access I terms of train service and there were physical barriers in terms of being visible.
Their site acts as a thoroughfare between Fish Island and the Olympic site, and people also walk through Hackney Wick to get to Victoria Park. 
Two minutes to the train station on the canal, which makes it ‘valuable and unique for London – ‘people can sit a relax and feel cut off from the hustle and bustle’
Their corner location provides good nighttime visibility and promoting a sense of community safety and calm
	Over the years our location has become quite pivotal because it’s the pathway to many of the new developments being built in Fish Island, making the connection between that and the Olympic site.
	What makes our location really special is we now can actually see the Olympic village and site.

What are the key phrases you use when selling the Park?
As far as Hackney Wick itself, they usually reference it in relation to Berlin—the similarities in terms of the rough, raw environment and the tension in the area. And they always tend to say it has one of the highest identities of makers and creative in both the UK and Europe
	We always mention regents canal, and we always mention we’re on the periphery/border of three boroughs. We also mention the Olympic Stadium because its also a music venue. And we also mention Victoria Park and Queens Yard because people tend to know those two anchor points.
What do you think are the most important assets of the Park?

The diversity of independent businesses—breweries, café, food makers, and the bars tend to be enterprises themselves 
	In terms of architecture and design, there’s a really interesting tension between old and new that people want to experience. That there will be something really old and historic but also new-ness coming in.’
	Certainly the creative economy here is so vibrant. One of the pulling forces of Hackney Wick and in relation to the Park, in terms of weekend café trade, people come here looking to access creative inspiration—whether they’re coming from Bristol or Liverpool, they come to Hackney Wick and then they go into the Park and to see the legacy there. They don’t’ know what they’ll expect in the park but they often want to carry on their journey from Hackney Wick.
	People also come from the park and stumble upon Hackey WIck and its great with the green space and cycling and then finding the Queen’s Yard and [?] wharf. And small pockets of activity. It’s a great journey for people from Westfield to the park and then to Hackney Wick and its art and street art and canal boats.
What are the main challenges you encountered in promoting the Park?
Tension between developers designing for the area and existing development, and then the relationship with the local authority. ‘That tension can be a challenge as can bringing those different sectors together. How do we all connect and have conversation? And how do we maintain those relationships while having similar visions?’
The canal does create a barrier, even between Fish Island, Hackney Wick, the Park and Here East.
Hackney Wick is vibrant and creative – the challenge now is how that identity is changing and will change, particularly as more residential moves in, where does creativity fit in? 
They’re trying to create a nighttime economy but there is the question of how does that relate to the local residents. The Olympic site as a music venue sometimes has shows going on until 10:30 or 11pm. And the Olympic site and the festivals that happen there—needing to communicate that as a positive and articulate the benefits and opportunities that provides. 
	Hackney Wick is quite diverse. I’m a local resident, and I run an art space and there is a tension between makers and artists who’ve been here for decades and new residents with different identities and expectations.
	Costs have gone up tenfold in the last ten years – space, ongoing costs, sustainability is being impacted so the risk of us being able to stay here is a challenge. How can we maintain our businesses and part of this ecosystem and ensure we can maintain that?
Have you implemented anything that has helped overcome these? If not, do you have any plans to address those barriers or challenges?
They’re involved with the Mayor’s Creative Enterprise Zone programme and are trying to deal with their current issues in a positive way
The other way is working with academic institutions – Queen Mary and Londonborough University and understanding how the area is changing
In the past they’ve been reactive and doing interventions that are almost too late and wanting now to try to be ahead of local problems
They’re trying to have good, positive and open dialogue with LLDC, Tower Hamlets and explaining opportunities with new developers. They’re now part of a cultural interest group –a network of businesses within Hackney Wick and Fish Island
	The cultural economy is so significant for London and to retain, promote and expand that –the arts community and residents and to create something viable that will address a lot of those issues around affordability, policy, and licensing.
Do you think the Park’s offer is clear?
	I would say that it isn’t as clear as it could be. Most people stumble upon the park. Most people have an idea of what is there but there’s much more. I thought I was just going to cycle through and see a couple of things, but there’s so much more. People are pleasantly surprised which can be part of the fun too.
How important is it that the Park’s marketing and place narrative should refer to the heritage of this part of East London - including the Olympics? What do you see as the most important elements of this heritage? What's the role of culture in establishing the identity of this part of London?
	Very important! It’s a no brainer. I went to Brazil three weeks ago and people were talking about the history of things that used to happen here and people who worked in factories in the area. It would give it an even bigger beacon so if we highlight the cultural and creative in tandem with sport. And that will make sense and is part of the objectives of what the park is about. When people think about Beyonce playing in the Olympic Stadium, it’s not a weird thing– it just makes sense and should be highlighted more and it will make the park and Hackney Wick fit together better more as one environment. And with UAL coming in too it just makes sense.
What do you think will be the most significant development in the Park in the next five years?
The major players coming into the area –the V & A and the university—they are both the biggest opportunity and the biggest challenge of new development
They hope to be able to work closely with them. The smaller group and cultural organisations want to feel like they can talk to and collaborate with the new entrants. And about sharing resources and communicating well together. 
Part of why the Creative Enteprise Zone is so important 
What do you hope the Park is known for going forward?
She would like the park and neighbouring areas to be known for innovative enterprise that promotes arts and culture across the board—from Stour Space and local creative enterprises to the V & A. And to be a place where small and medium makers can thrive that’s affordable and to come and experience something they wouldn’t elsewhere.
To be an area that has character, edge and tension and need to maintain that tension. To have vibrant spaces that are open and not managed where there’s room for creativity, experimentation and spontaneity. And she hopes it becomes a place that people feel if they’re a maker or artist they can have a home here.
