Vision
Somerset has a distinct cultural identity, rooted in its landscape and natural heritage. We will celebrate this identity through arts and culture and preserve all that is good about the sector. We will nurture it, to ensure that it grows and enriches the lives of our residents, through our communities and cultural partners.























Foreword
The process and creation of a cultural strategy for Somerset Council will be an essential advocate for the arts and culture in Somerset. From this, it will present an exciting vision for the role of culture within the county, and the communities the Council serves. 
I am delighted to share with you our Cultural Strategy for the new Somerset Council. Culture makes a significant contribution to the new Council vision for Somerset. It places an emphasis on establishing a cultural ecosystem and making Somerset a place where business can flourish within safe, vibrant and healthy communities. Residents will enjoy healthy lifestyles, better economic and social prospects through engagement with arts, culture and Somerset’s unrivalled legacy of natural and built heritage. 
The purpose of this Cultural Strategy is to set out a strategic approach for the supply of arts and culture through all our services. It explores ways in which arts and community organisations can work in partnership with services such as social care, education and planning. As well as supporting the new Council’s mission to build healthy, cohesive communities, it embeds arts and culture within our lives. Providing a high quality of life, supporting people in improving their own physical and mental wellbeing. 
Research has been the basis of development for this strategy. It brings to light the breadth and scale of arts and culture on offer in Somerset, and the depth of feeling that the people and communities within the region have for engagement in cultural activity. We have been given the opportunity to transform arts and culture within Somerset. Additionally, through the Arts Council England’s renewed commitment to investing in arts organisations in Somerset, major capital programmes ensuring redevelopment have occurred. This plan understands the contextual issues of the county and seeks to address the key challenges facing our residents. 
The Cultural Strategy seeks to make the arts and culture more accessible, visible, and relevant to the residents and visitors of Somerset. In actioning the priorities of new Somerset Council, we ensure activity serves Somerset's communities positively. However, this is an ambitious proposal whose success will rely on partnership working and the commitment of all parts of the Council, cultural organisations, and community partners, embodies in a new Cultural Compact for Somerset. 
I am excited to see the direct impact our work will have for our residents, and an increased level of activity for our creative sector.
Executive Summary
The new Somerset Council
Somerset Council’s new vision will preserve and promote the county’s heritage, beauty and traditions that have set it apart from the rest of the country. In order to build a fairer, greener, more flourishing community that listens to its residents, and cares for the most vulnerable. Delivery will be built around four priorities: 
	•	A Greener, More Sustainable Somerset: to preserve the natural beauty and encourage an environmentally sustainable and more resilient Somerset. To encourage the existing community to take more steps in this pursuit and to encourage newer residence who have this ethos to set up business and homes in Somerset. 
	•	A Healthy and Caring Somerset: to preserve the health and wellbeing of the community supporting residents to stay as healthy as possible and encouraging new communities to support the economic benefits to the county. For example, bringing new skills, ways of living and enabling employment to support the provision of social care and cultural prescribing. 
	•	A Fairer Somerset: to preserve and encourage a county that is proud of its diversity. To retain and attract the younger demographic, to play their parts in the arts and grow a more diverse and inclusive county. 
	•	A Flourishing and Resilient Somerset: this is the net result of the above priority and is the ‘action plan’ for the next 5 – 10 years to promote Somerset at a regional, national and international level. Promoting the wealth of opportunities to bring investment and highly skilled, well-paid jobs for Somerset people.

Why does Somerset need a cultural strategy?
The new Somerset Council has identified culture as one of the drivers to bring about economic growth and a better quality of life for all. The cultural strategy gives voice to priorities expressed by residents and the cultural sector through a six-month period of research and consultation undertaken between June and December 2022. It recognises that change is needed in planning for and investment in culture to make best use of existing capacity and resources across all Somerset’s districts.
Whilst enlisting change, the cultural strategy also celebrates and builds upon what each local authority already does well. It rationalises existing council strategies, consolidating existing investment and best practice in arts development, cultural programming, venue management, museums, libraries and heritage, community arts provision, cultural education and social prescribing.
The strategy will promote cultural engagement, raise awareness and promote the role arts and culture can play in enriching our communities, improving quality of life, health and wellbeing. 
It provides recommendations to boost skills and creative employment and nurture existing and new creative businesses.
Somerset’s distinctive and vibrant cultural identity will be promoted to establish and realise Somerset’s potential as a cultural destination. 
Serving to protect, capitalise and unlock potential opportunities for Somerset’s creative sector that unifies place-based priorities of all five existing local authorities, the strategy recognises the key role of cultural provision and public art in placemaking in both urban and rural contexts across the county. This includes both significant investment in regeneration through large schemes including the £23m Glastonbury Town Deal and the £29m redevelopment of the Octagon Theatre and support for the county-wide network of venues ranging from commercial theatres to community halls. 
The strategy addresses historic discrepancies in public funding for the arts which have seen urban areas in other parts of the South West receive eight times the funding per capita of that granted to Somerset. 2023 will see an increase in Arts Council investment in organisations promoting rural touring, heritage and arts development in areas of low social mobility.
The strategy proposes that future decision making for culture and creative industries be brought together under a new Cultural Advisory Board for Somerset [see page 27] which will work with the Council and other partners and funders to ensure that it delivers the outcomes promised. Over time, the Cultural Advisory Board will play a bigger role in development of cultural strategy and  delivery of its recommendations .

Action plan
The strategy includes recommendations for a seven-point action plan, led by the Council and delivered in partnership with arts and cultural organisations, schools and colleges, health & wellbeing providers and the private sector.
	•	Cultural and Creative Industries (CCI) investment and support programme which draws together existing investment across council districts and services to provide sector specialist business support and access to finance. It will balance priorities between retention of funding for existing programmes (including events and touring productions) and nurturing existing and start-up businesses to ensure that the sector delivers increased economic benefits and opportunities through business growth and job creation.
	•	Creative skills and leadership development – the strategy will amplify cultural & creative education and skills at all levels including careers advice through securing continuing funding from Department for Education for the Music Education Hub. We will work with partners in the county’s schools and FE colleges, and in neighbouring Universities to develop a plan to dleiver a right to creative skills education at all ages and increases provision of further and higher education in creative and digital skills.
	•	Collaborative network development to enable artists and organisations to benefit in a timely way from opportunities for development and growth. This may include formalising relationships across private and public sectors within arts and culture through development of an independent Cultural Advisory Board. [See page.27]
	•	Recognising and promoting the diversity of Somerset’s rural and urban cultural offer through of Cultural and Creative Zones (CCZs), building on existing and emerging clusters of creative businesses, working with “anchor” institutions, venues, and local arts organisations with the aim to build capacity for arts and creative industries and link them together with other forms of culture and public services to capture and amplify the economic and social value of the creative and visitor economy. CCZs will reflect existing and emerging strengths in both urban and rural locations, from West Somerset to North Mendip, forming a county-wide “mosaic” of creative districts which link creative production, cultural experiences and visitor economy offers and promotion.
	•	Promote Somerset as a Green and creative destination - working in partnership through local representative bodies and democratic structures (including arts and cultural organisations, community groups, business associations, Chambers of Commerce, national trade bodies and Town Councils) to jointly develop plans for and promotion of arts and culture. This will embed the vision of preserving, celebrating, nurturing, and growing Somerset as a special and distinctive cultural and heritage destination. It will promote a mosaic of creative districts (see Cultural and Creative Zones, above), Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONBs) and heritage sites, local food and produce and visitor economy attractions and events. By grouping attractions and events within a thirty-minute radius of the CCZ hub sites, and promoting local transport plans, it will embed the brand of Somerset as a “30-minute cultural county”. 
	•	Develop a shared framework for intelligence and evaluation for creative and visitor economy investment and performance (audiences, businesses, jobs and Gross Value Added (GVA)). 
	•	Establish a county-wide social prescribing framework and training plan to amplify the role of arts and culture in health wellbeing and promote equal access opportunities, building on learnings from existing programmes within the county (including Yeovil Hospital Thriving Communities) and drawing on best practice from national bodies such as the National Academy for Social Prescribing (NASP).

It sets a direction for how venues and arts organisations can work together to develop and grow audiences.

Somerset has a growing ageing population who are increasingly living in poor health, placing a significant burden on service budgets. The rurality of the county adds to those challenges. There are existing local projects in which culture already plays an important role in health and wellbeing, social prescribing and integrated adult social care – this knowledge will be shared across Somerset and built upon, both to support the ageing population of the county as well as developing routes into creative careers for practitioners.
Arts & Health South West is prioritising mental health, social prescribing and loneliness. The very rural character of parts of the county could factor heavily in developing schemes and working across council services and with other local bodies such as NHS Trusts, ICGs and other wellbeing providers to catalyse a change in rurality and cultural participation and well-being. 
The audience for culture
We received nearly 1,500 responses to an online residents’ survey, which revealed an active and dynamic group of residents who demonstrate an appetite for both venue and non-venue-based activity across arts, culture and heritage. They attend music, performance and visual arts events, visit museums and galleries, and enjoy outdoor arts and street festivals.
The high levels of cultural engagement are even more impressive when the rurality of the county and large travel distances are considered. People are prepared to travel to get their cultural “fix”, with nearly 50% of audiences travelling between 5 and 20 miles and 38% regularly travelling up to 30 miles.  
When asked to consider how arts and culture could be applied to make improvements in Somerset (Figure 1), most respondents felt arts and culture were important in enriching lives with activities, along with making places more attractive. Over 50% strongly agreed that improving health and wellbeing was important, as well as building local pride.
The responses confirmed that Somerset residents understand the importance of arts and culture. It shows an appreciation for its contribution to individual wellbeing, place making and quality of life.

Young people
The future of the creative and cultural sector will rely on a healthy supply of new talent. As outlined in the Creative Industries Policy and Evidence Centre report of September 2021, there are “deep divides and entrenched inequality within the creative sector” and it recognises that class-based exclusions are more pronounced in the creative sector than in any other industrial sector.  
The Fifth Sector, supported by Bridgewater and Taunton College, held a round-table for young people to discuss key issues impacting on their access to and involvement in the creative and cultural sector.  The young people expressed a desire to see:
	•	Creative activities during the summer 
	•	More creative careers advice both within and external to colleges and schools
	•	Better signposting to creative events and activities through social channels
	•	A Somerset creative youth social calendar
	•	An annual creative arts competition for Somerset artists and creatives aged under 25 to include a physical showcase and social gathering
	•	 Access to experienced professionals through mentoring and/or work experience
	•	Short courses in new technologies – something for which many young people would said they would be willing to pay
	•	Business support – including legal and financial advice – to support their development as freelancers 
	•	Prioritisation of future skills and digital technologies in all activity

Culture as a guardian of natural heritage: Somerset’s diverse landscapes are a key feature of its cultural environment: as a creative inspiration, as a cultural destination and as backdrop for outdoor work. 	•	The decline in public transport was one of the key factors cited as a barrier to cultural engagement and participation in Somerset. With younger people – including visitors – more likely to access public transport, this represents an opportunity for transport plans around cultural events to encourage behaviour change around public transport.

Existing cultural assets 
The value of place and placemaking provide the canvas for which an area can grow. Those values are embodied in Somerset’s diverse and rich ecology of urban, rural and natural assets. With no single metropolitan centre, the importance of establishing the potential of a distributed model of sustainability and vibrancy is critical in informing the starting point for a new cultural strategy. Therefore, the strategy takes a wider view of what constitutes a cultural asset, including arts, culture, built and natural heritage, festivals, venues, libraries and museums. We comment in more detail on some of these below. 
Libraries
Somerset’s county library service retains a presence in communities across the county, operating across 19 core sites and 13 community library partnerships. The service has assumed an increased importance in Somerset at a time when many other local authorities have actively divested their library portfolio. Here, libraries play a significant role as both cultural and community hubs, offering a much wider range of services: not just exhibition space and access to computers, but a specialist Performing Arts Library offering resources and advice for music and drama study, recreation and performance, business support and access to health and wellbeing services.
Museums 
There are over 50 museums and heritage organisations/attractions within Somerset, and of these 25 are within the Arts Council England’s Museum Accreditation scheme. Almost every town in Somerset has a museum, almost always entirely run by volunteers, and with collections specifically from their town and surrounding parishes, including nine accredited by Arts Council England.  
All are run by charitable trusts, and largely run by volunteers. Many of these are classed as micro-museums by Museum Development UK (i.e., fewer than 10,000 visits per annum); only two of these are in receipt of regular funding by a local authority (Wells and Mendip Museum receives funding to look after the Wells City Archive and Blake Museum in Bridgwater is funded by the Town Council).  
Several of these museums offer resources and sessions for local schools, and some have developed a strong learning offer, receiving repeat visits from local primary schools. Many offer family activities.
In addition, Southwest Heritage Trust (SWHT) manages Somerset County Council collections and museum sites, as well as the county archives, and 400 acres of historic landscapes owned by the county. It is funded largely by the County Council. SWHT became an ACE NPO in 2023. 
Venues
Somerset enjoys a diverse range of cultural venues, each of which plays an important role as a hub for professional development and community identity:
	•	Commercial theatres including the Octagon Theatre in Yeovil, The McMillan Theatre in Bridgwater and Strode Theatre in Street, which present a mix of commissioned and touring work in theatre, music and dance.
	•	Multi-art form venues including The Brewhouse Theatre and Tacchi-Morris Arts Centre in Taunton and Frome’s Merlin Theatre, with its mix of live performance, film and workshops.
	•	Galleries for visual arts and making ranging from Hauser & Wirth in Bruton, part of a large international franchise offering support to Somerset makers, contemporary visual arts and crafts galleries such as  Black Swan Arts in Frome and East Quay in Watchet whose studio and exhibition space play a major role in local regeneration.
	•	Independent music venues and performing arts centres including The Cheese and Grain, Frome and the David Hall in South Petherton.
Rural Touring venues 
Somerset’s cultural ecology is a distinctive blend of formal and informal structures. Rural touring is an essential ingredient in Somerset’s rich cultural mix. It provides opportunities for audiences of all ages and backgrounds in all parts of the county to experience new professional work at affordable prices. Take Art has operated a rural touring programme in Somerset since 1987; it continuing role as an NPO and ACE’s investment in two new touring theatre companies for Somerset illustrate the importance of the form.
Rural touring is about more than just performances, it plays an important part in the social life of small communities, it involves people of all ages and backgrounds, it can be a catalyst for community development.
(“Only Connect Report” by Francois Matarasso)
Rural touring has both huge reach and impact. It provides development opportunities for thousands of volunteers, hundreds of communities and volunteer promoters. Whilst supporting the preservation of local venues, including community and church halls, it provides the aspiration for members of rural communities to engage as audience and, participants in work of the highest quality. It should therefore be seen as an essential complement to venue-based production companies and valued as a seed bed for talent.
The now established pattern of alternating Open Studios and Art Weeks produced by Somerset Art Works achieves a similar end for visual artists: raising their profile, promoting opportunities for collaboration and attracting significant numbers of visitors and spend to the county - “the 2019 Somerset Art Weeks Festival saw over 80,000 visitors engage with art and creativity across 135 venues throughout Somerset.”
The distribution of touring venues across Somerset is reflected in the presence of “microclusters” of creative businesses in rural areas with no producing venues, such as West Somerset, rural South Somerset and North Mendip. It is a reminder that the creativity of a place stems from its people and can be catalysed by the imaginative combination of a range of assets and resources, not just buildings. 
Community Arts
One of the distinctive features of Somerset’s cultural offer is the prevalence of community arts. Community art is a critical element of Somerset’s cultural ecology. Hundreds of smaller arts and voluntary arts organisations across the county are essential to the preservation, retention and development of Somerset’s creative ecology. 
Participation in community events, as audience, participant or professional facilitator, plays a pivotal role in sustaining and nurturing cultural activity in Somerset and has an influence well beyond its measurable economic impact. For many people, it provides their main engagement with the arts as an audience member; for others, it offers an introduction to the idea of creative employment and a way to develop skills as a volunteer. The social capital and sense of a shared historical and cultural legacy created by community arts finds its expression in forms of artistic expression which reflect Somerset’s distinctive cultural identity, including the Illuminated Carnivals.
The often-highlighted resilience of arts and culture in Somerset over the last decade, a period when economic shocks have been followed by reduced levels of arts funding in an endless downward spiral, can arguably be attributed to the habits and practice of collaboration and making the most of what is there formed through community art.
Festivals and events
The festivals and events sector has potential to meet Somerset’s objectives to create distinctive experiences which build on its unique heritage. Like community arts, festivals and events reside on the boundary of the professional sector and their role in wider society. Somerset holds many festivals and events, ranging from major international attractions such as Glastonbury Festival to the distinctive and community-led Illuminated Carnivals. Together they present the diversity of its communities to wider audiences both from within and beyond the county. 
We will look to address opportunities to sustain and grow professional and community-led events by maintaining investment in the event programme is important to maintain local provision whilst encouraging development of skills in creative leadership, production skills and technical crew to meet existing demand. Sector specialist business support will address the need for festivals and events organisations to develop capacity, including bringing in more established operators to support and mentor local artists and producers. This could increase employment, leverage inward investment and deliver GVA through a mix of the wider indirect and induced benefits (see Glossary, below) in sector employment and through the visitor economy.
Natural and Built Heritage
Landscape and natural heritage are part of Somerset’s distinctive identity and should be a catalyst for attracting arts and heritage visitors. Its diverse mix of rural areas, from Exmoor to the Quantocks through the Somerset Levels to the Mendips and the unique landscape of Cheddar Gorge and the Wookey Hole Caves, with its evidence of human habitation dating back to the Stone Age, is complemented by its of coastal offer.  
The county also has a noteworthy built heritage asset, ranging from ancient monuments such as Glastonbury Abbey and Glastonbury Tor to Wells Cathedral to the expansive gardens of Hestercombe House. 
We will promote all of these natural and built heritage assets in promoting Somerset as a green and cultural destination as well as creating content for local communities.  

Existing arts funding
Local authority funding
The County Council Music Education Hub has been the most significant beneficiary of public funding, receiving £700k per annum from the Department for Education that is managed by Somerset County Council. 
Other local authority support includes:
	•	Somerset West & Taunton Council has regular funding agreements totalling £166,000 per annum to organisations including Taunton Brewhouse, Arts Taunton, Fuse Performance, Hestercombe Gardens Trust and Take Art. In addition, it has provided grant funding to organisations including Actiontrack, Creative Industries Centre CIC, Go Create, Somerset Art Works, Somerset Film, Regal Theatre Minehead, Watchet Live, Dulverton by Starlight, Pride 2022, the Ten Parishes Festival and State of Trust, which delivers The Coleridge Project, which works in schools around Taunton to reeal possibilities of a wider scope in careers in the arts by engaging interest and curiosity in museums as well as revealing vocational pathways into the performing arts as a whole.
	•	South Somerset District Council has regular funding agreements totalling £19,000 with Take Art, Somerset Art Works. Somerset Film and Actiontrack to support work in the district. It also has a small commissioning pot for new work.
	•	Somerset County Council has long-term annual funding agreements totalling £54,000 with libraries in Bishops Lydeard, Bruton, Castle Cary, Nether Stowey, North Petherton, Porlock, Somerton, South Petherton, Street and Watchet.
Other agencies, including Somerset Film, have been successful in getting small project grants for social prescribing  from local authorities.

In 2021, Arts Council England (ACE) assessed every local authority in England to determine which areas would become “priority places” for culture. All four of Somerset’s district authorities demonstrated high levels of need for additional investment; but only South Somerset was granted priority place status. 
As part of local government reorganisation, Somerset Council will assume ownership of the assets and responsibility for the development of the Octagon Theatre. From April 2023, it will become an Arts Council National Portfolio Organisation (NPO) with funding for cultural outreach and education, alongside six other Somerset-based organisations: Contains Art, Somerset Art Works, Somerset Film, South West Heritage Trust, Take Art and Little Bulb Theatre, a touring theatre company new to Somerset which will be working in partnership with the Octagon). This represents an increase of 139% in annual NPO funding to Somerset (from £544,000 to £1.298 million per annum.) 
ACE also contributed £645,000 in funding for 36 projects by Somerset-based artists in the 2021. 26 out of the 36 received awards of £15,000 and below, eight received awards of between £15,000 and £50,000 and two recipients received National Activity awards under £100,000.
Major capital investments
There is a significant public and private sector investment into cultural and heritage infrastructure in Somerset, including:
	•	Glastonbury Town Deal has secured £23m investment from the Towns Fund  Of this, £3m is allocated to Bridgwater Carnival to provide improved facilities, £9.2m for the restoration of the docks, £1.6m to the restoration of Bridgwater Arts Centre and £0.6m to development of improved premises for Somerset Film (“Engine Room 2.0”).  The Life Factory is allocated £3.43m, part of which is about creating a new industry and preserving craft skills around restoration, making and repair, and £1.77m to the Glastonbury Abbey Piazza.  
	•	Glastonbury Festival generates over £35m in benefits to the local economy and is  followed each year by a programme of events at Glastonbury Abbey which reinforce its cultural value to residents.
	•	 A £29m investment in the arts through redevelopment of the Octagon Theatre, backed by new Arts Council England investment to help build audiences and improve outreach through a network of hubs across the South Somerset district.
Visitor economy 
Arts and culture have the potential to play a more significant role in projecting Somerset’s cultural identity and driving growth.
	•	“Attractions/entertainment” (including direct spend on arts and culture) represents 10% of total income from visitors to the county.  The cultural strategy will look to grow this through increased coordination in planning and promotion of Somerset’s offer from both professional companies and community arts. 
	•	Arts and culture can also help Somerset address the marked seasonality of its visitor economy, especially in staying visitors – its “shoulder seasons” (between the summer peak and Christmas) are very low. This suggests that the county is not fully exploiting the opportunity to drive more traffic to its arts and cultural offering outside those peak seasons.
	•	The visitor economy is heavily dependent on day trips (91% of visits and 61% of spend). 94% of staying visitors are from the UK; overseas staying visitors spend more per trip (£307 against an average £195 for UK staying visitors). This reflects the trend observed across the UK whereby majority of staying trips are to visit family and friends and thus reducing the accommodation element of that spend. 
	•	More coordinated planning and promotion of cultural events – identifying a “mosaic” of Creative and Cultural Zones connecting urban centres and rural districts across the county and linking the cultural experience with complementary offers such as food tourism - can help visitors plan itineraries and lead to longer and more lucrative stays.

Creative microclusters
Clustering of businesses is often an indication of increased shared expertise with resulting benefits in productivity and the ability to attract and retain skilled workforce. This is typically observed in larger urban areas, but The Creative Radar report supports the idea that rural creative industries are expanding through a series of “microclusters” of 50 or more creative firms within a defined location. Postcode analysis of Companies House data suggests that nearly all areas of Somerset (with a minimum population of 10,000) demonstrate existing or developing microcluster. 
Evidence of the distribution of county-wide urban and rural creative districts informs the recommendation to create cultural and creative zones as the basis for increasing collaboration and investment in the sector. 
	•	Wells – greatest density of creative businesses and the largest cluster of film, television, video and radio businesses
	•	Frome – more than 50 arts businesses supported by a diverse venue infrastructure
	•	Taunton – the largest single cluster of arts businesses and creative services such as architecture, advertising and marketing
	•	Yeovil – strengths in arts and software (potentially linked to the local aerospace cluster)
	•	Glastonbury and rural South Somerset demonstrate diversified creative clusters with a growing arts sector
	•	Bridgwater, Wiveliscombe and Somerton have software-focused microclusters
	•	Other rural areas, including West Somerset and North Mendip, demonstrate evidence of microclustering as strong as that in larger towns with established cultural venues.

Provision of creative skills 
Education and skills: The importance of creative and cultural education in Somerset 
The cultural and creative sectors demonstrate an increasing need for a mix of technical and creative skills. It is therefore not surprising that the creative economy has a qualification and skills structure far more geared towards high level qualifications. Nationally, 67% of the workforce is equipped with qualifications from higher education, while only 16% have qualifications at level 2 or below (low skills). Somerset reflects this national image and has a highly qualified creative workforce, with 65% educated to degree level and 12% with qualifications at level 2 or lower. 
Analysis of creative skills training across Somerset identifies 158 courses, the overwhelming majority of which are at FE level. There are an additional 535 courses available in a wider Travel to Work Area (including Exeter, Weston-Super-Mare, Bristol, Bath and Bournemouth). 
This suggests that Somerset is relatively well equipped to deliver a range of creative and cultural qualifications but there is room for improvement. 
	•	The lack of undergraduate and postgraduate provision is a barrier to developing, attracting and retaining talent and has implications for diversity, inclusion, social mobility and economic growth.
	•	Continuing Professional Development (CPD) is mostly “hobby-type courses” – e.g., “introduction to” type courses such as the “Painting for Fun” courses at Strode College. Although Taunton and Bridgewater College do have a range of accredited Adult Learning courses around sewing, painting, dressmaking, ceramics etc.
	•	FE and HE are not delivering any CPD for creative businesses – there is an opportunity to extend the range of courses for businesses (for example “Smartphone Photography for your Creative Business” at Bridgewater and Taunton College).
	•	FE is covering all 5 pathways well although there are opportunities to develop more creative/technical fusion courses to align with the future skills needs. 
	•	Creative Originals (craft and maker skills) is well catered for at all levels, and there is good provision of Creative Content skills (audio visual content). 
	•	There is an opportunity to concentrate on Creative Services (consider more digital graphic design and visualisation aligned to future business needs) and Creative Experiences (consider event management courses due to the opportunities that the high preponderance of carnivals and festivals in the region offer).
	•	There is a lack of apprenticeships and T-level provision. This is common throughout England due to a lack of pathways and the funding structures of apprenticeships which are currently difficult to deliver by SMEs in the creative/cultural sectors. New T-levels coming on board in September 2023 could provide better access routes into the sector. 
	•	There are some interesting T-level “transition” courses (i.e.: prep for T-levels) at Yeovil and Strode Colleges. 



Cultural ecology

In 2015, John Holden published The Ecology of Culture (Holden, 2015). Commissioned by the AHRC’s Cultural Value Project, the report explored the ecology of culture: 
“The complex interdependencies that shape the demand for and production of arts and cultural offerings.” 
Holden asserted that, “the ecology of culture could be conceived as three highly interactive spheres: publicly funded culture, commercial culture and homemade culture.”
Holden’s findings and recommendations are particularly relevant to a rural county such as Somerset. Culture can be looked at as an economy (of supply and demand, of workforce and consumption) – and this is typically how it is assessed in metropolitan and urban areas, where the mass effects of clustering are more easily observed and measured. 
Holden found that, 
“Treating culture as an ecology brings the qualitative into consideration as much as the quantitative,” 
and this approach seems particularly resonant with the establishment of the County and the unitary authority. A purely economic analysis risks falling short of presenting the true value of culture to a rural county where non-monetary measures – the importance of social value and the preservation and celebration of natural heritage – make a significant contribution to identity and wellbeing. 
This is even more the case in an era of diminishing financial resources, where a strategic approach needs to properly value collaboration and shared social value. 
In this respect, Somerset’s cultural ecology is more like that of small UK nations than urban metropolitan cities.



Cultural sector survey
An online survey for “cultural practitioners” - artists, makers, musicians, performers, producers, venue managers, heritage organisations – was carried out to inform an overview of the cultural ecology of Somerset.
A national system which promotes competition for funding and resource has had a negative impact on the ability of artists and organisations to build capacity and invest in long term development. But whilst funding is always identified as a primary need, many artists and organisations placed equal importance on collaboration as a way to both share scarce resource and stimulate new ideas.
The strategy identifies and reinforces the ways in which pooling resources – skills development, platforms for production and clearly identified cultural and creative zones to promote collaboration – can add to the benefits of bringing together funding and best practice from the five previous local authorities in the new Somerset Council. 
With so many freelancers in Somerset working within educational or community contexts the opportunity to develop new work in these areas serves both to attract new audiences and help the Council identify new, economical and effective ideas to deliver key services. 
Free text responses to the question, “What would assist you?” included:
	•	“A very strong diverse and inclusive Cultural Strategy Group/Board, which includes an education strand, within the new Unitary Authority which drives action and change and raises the profile of culture and activity across the County. Also, a Youth Cultural Group which is valued and feeds ideas into this.”
	•	“Somerset's arts and cultural sector is in serious need of levelling up between areas of strong provision (and coincidentally? higher engagement) and those areas without it…”
	•	“I hope there can be varied opportunities in Somerset. Growing up, peers and I believed you were only going to be successful and work in the arts industry if you moved to London or big cities.”
	•	Local authorities – including Somerset – continue to face reductions in funding from central Government. Although the new Council has an important role to play in setting strategy and framing the ambition for Somerset’s cultural sector it cannot – and should not – be seen as the sole, or even the main funder for arts and cultural activity. Therefore, the strategy includes a recommendation to seed to development of a Cultural Advisory Board which can, over time, work with partners from across arts, culture, education, public and private sectors to assume greater responsibility for attracting the investment needed by the sector.

Indirect and induced outputs
Investment in arts and culture has both direct and indirect benefits to the economy.

	•	£492.3 million direct benefits from turnover and employment in the sector
	•	£52.6 million indirect benefits from turnover and employment in the supply chain
	•	£71.3m induced benefits from increased spending in the wider economy
Measures of economic impact alone do not reflect the full value of Somerset’s rich and diverse cultural ecology – in particular, the importance of social value and the preservation and celebration of natural heritage – and their significant contribution to identity and wellbeing. The proposal to connect creative industries development and visitor economy promotion through a mosaic of urban and rural Cultural and Creative Zones reflects the complementary values of arts, culture and heritage.

Whilst recognised in programmes such as Hauser & Worth’s Make Gallery, crafts activity in Somerset represents an untold story and an underutilised asset for development and promotion of both the creative and the visitor economy.

Appendix 2: Glossary
Artist: person who creates art or culture: for example, a painter, writer, singer, musician, dancer.
The arts: refers to the wider arts and cultural sectors including arts and or cultural organisations, venues (music, theatre, visual arts, comedy, film etc), artists, producers, and freelancers. It covers the range from those who create content to those who distribute it and show it.
ACE: Arts Council England, an arms-length body of the Department of Digital, Culture, Media & Sport with a duty for development of arts and culture in England.
Community arts: artistic activity that is based in a community setting (which can be geographical communities or communities characterised for example, by ethnicity, gender, age, or sexuality), characterised by interaction or dialogue with the community and often involving a professional artist collaborating with people who may not otherwise engage in the arts.
Creative arts: Using creativity as a means of expressing emotion or conveying an idea. Through the medium of paint, film, theatre, photography or sculpture.
Creative education: provision of education services (both formal as regards courses and curriculum-based learning) and informal such as workshops and drop-in sessions. Can range from any age.
Creative industries: business in the commercial sector who provide creative services such as graphic design, web design, marketing and advertising, games development, and other digital service – also a blanket term to include arts and cultural organisations (also CCI: Cultural and Creative Industries).
Creative practitioners/creatives: people who provide either cultural and/or creative services or produce cultural product.
Creative supply chains: analysis of the business base, workforce and the supply of skills and education is linked by the concept of creative supply chains, made up of the following industries and activities: 
	•	Creative content: businesses which make a living through creation and exploitation of intellectual property in new creative works such as film, television, recorded music, animation, computer games and publishing. 
	•	Creative experiences: sectors in which the primary means of delivery is in person. Including theatre, dance, live music, performance, festivals, events, libraries, museums and galleries. 
	•	Creative originals: creation of original physical artefacts, including fine and visual arts, crafts, “making”, fashion design and photography. 
	•	Creative services: professional services with a significant creative element, including advertising, marketing, architecture, graphic design, cultural education and translation services. 
	•	Digital creative: computer-related services which utilise the creative skills of software development and coding but whose output is typically in other sectors (eg, business software development, computer consultancy, fintech). This excludes both computer games, augmented and virtual reality, which are identified within creative content. Computer security and “hardware” elements of the IT supply chain (telecoms, data centers, supply of computer equipment).  
Culture: can refer to a broader sense of culture as people and how they do things. The consultation process did not dictate a definition of culture, so as not to exclude people who view culture beyond the formal definitions that the cultural sector relates to. In the context of this strategy, its use refers to cultural product; culture that has been produced including but not exclusively, music, theatre, film, visual art, poetry. 
Cultural activity: the act of taking part in culture: going to the theatre, to see a band or orchestra, to watch a film, visit a gallery, attend a Carnival etc.
Cultural organisations: a structure that can range from 1-2 employees or freelancers to many employees who produce and support culture, including the creation of, distribution of, and showcasing of. This can be venues such as theatres, cinemas, music venues, comedy clubs and art galleries but also non-venue-based organisations such as community arts organisations, producers, outdoor arts companies, festival companies and business Some may be formally constituted as charities, CICs, CIOS, limited companies or partnerships and some may be informally convened. Some may be core funded through public bodies such as Arts Council England and others may be funded on a project-by-project basis whilst others might have a model of all of these as well as ticketed income from box office or fees. Can also be referred to as an arts organisation.
Direct economic impact: the direct impacts of cultural and creative industries employment (whether permanent, self-employed or freelance) in terms of increased turnover and earnings.
Indirect economic impact: supply chain linkage effects from arts and culture, eg, the economic impact of consumption of accommodation, food and transport which results from attendance at arts and cultural events.
Induced economic impact the economic impact of increased spending in the local economy from increased personal income which is a result of the direct and indirect benefits.
Natural Heritage: Natural features, geographical and physiological formations. Plants and natural sites of value from the point of view of science, conservation and natural beauty. 
NPO: abbreviation of National Portfolio Organisation. Cultural organisations who received core funding from Arts Council England, usually on a three-year basis.  
Outdoor arts: culture produced in outdoor settings such as Festivals, Carnival, Mela and other work like sculpture or visual art in the public realm. (In towns, rural areas etc).
Producer: a person who helps produce culture for example a theatre producer, a festival producer, or a carnival producer (roles can include project management, fund raising as well as other creative roles).
Theory of Change: A comprehensive description and illustration of how and why a desired change is expected to happen in a particular context. 
World Café: a consultation format which makes use of an informal café set up for participants to explore an issue by discussing it in small groups. Discussions are held in multiple rounds, with participants moving around between rounds.
