EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Kirklees Council commissioned The Fifth Sector to repeat previous analysis of the sector (carried out over a period from 2015 to 2022) to ascertain the impact of changes in the district’s cultural and creative industries over that period.
The research was conducted during the second quarter of 2024 and focused on: 
	•	Scale of the sector – the number of commercial creative businesses (including micro-enterprises) and not-for-profit arts and cultural organisations
	•	Creative employment, both direct and indirect, including freelances, contractors and self-employed creatives
	•	The financial value of the creative industries and music industry
	•	Strengths and shortcomings of the creative industries and the music sector
	•	Opportunities for business growth, employment, and skills development.
Our research reveals two contrasting perspectives on Kirklees' creative industries: one based on national statistics and another derived from in-depth analysis of diverse sources.
Official statistics paint a pessimistic picture, indicating a decline in Kirklees' creative businesses since 2015. However, our comprehensive analysis of various data sources, including ONS data, proprietary databases, and LinkedIn, suggests a more positive trend of growth in creative business numbers, employment, workforce, and output.
Key Findings
	•	Scale of the Sector: The creative sector in Kirklees is larger than reflected in national statistics, both in number of businesses and employment.
	•	Economic Impact: We estimate the GVA from Cultural and Creative Industries in Kirklees in 2022 at £250.9 million, an increase of £75m from 2020.
	•	Employment and Workforce: The creative workforce in Kirklees exceeds 9,400 people, significantly higher than official employment figures suggest.
	•	Business Growth: There were 899 creative business starts between April 2021 and June 2024, with 691 remaining active.
	•	Clustering: Significant creative clusters exist in sectors such as advertising and marketing, music, performing and arts, and film and television.
	•	Opportunities: Kirklees demonstrates potential for growth in areas such as film and television production, music tourism, and creative skills development.
Challenges and Recommendations:
	•	Take the opportunity of a strong commitment to Culture, Heritage and Sport at regional level and an incoming UK Government to address the discrepancy between official statistics and actual sector performance to make a stronger case for investment.
	•	Work with colleges, universities and the private sector to improve creative skills training and development, particularly in digital technologies and AI applications.
	•	Enhance support for creative freelancers and micro-businesses, including through developing the Creative Kirklees network. 
	•	Develop strategies to spread the benefits of the creative sector more evenly across the district. 
	•	Engage with regional initiatives to maximise opportunities in fast-growing markets like digital marketing and film and television.
It is important for the Council, stakeholders and Kirklees businesses and residents to understand both the data, how those discrepancies occur and how evidence of creative people, businesses and assets in the borough can be used to make a case for more investment. 
The starting point for this work aligned with community wealth building: a place-based and asset-led approach which builds on actual and emerging strengths, rather than seeking to identify a ‘deficit’ in need of intervention. This report provides a comprehensive analysis of Kirklees' creative economy, highlighting its strengths, challenges, and potential for future growth. By addressing key issues and leveraging existing assets, Kirklees can strengthen its position as a hub for creative industries and cultural activity.

Comparison of business datasets

Whilst this survey is carried out annually on a consistent basis (and therefore gives us a benchmark for relative changes over time), it is not a census of businesses in Kirklees.
To address concerns that some kinds of businesses – particularly creative microenterprises – are underrepresented in national statistics, we have consistently used Beauhurst, an independent platform which combines data from several sources, including Companies House, to give a fuller picture of the sector’s business population and performance. 
At the time of our analysis in June 2024, Kirklees was home to 1,730 active cultural and creative enterprises – 785 more than recorded in national statistics and an increase of 120 (7.5%) since our last report in 2022.

1.3	CREATIVE WORKFORCE
Restating our findings from previous studies in Kirklees and across England, BRES data tend to overstate the significance of IT, underrepresent employment in sectors including Music, performing and visual arts, and seem out of kilter with evidence of the number of businesses within other sub-sectors (including Advertising and marketing and Film, TV, video, radio and photography). Reasons for this include:
	•	The sampling methodology used to calculate employment by ONS is based on a comparatively tiny proportion of active businesses – fewer than 85,000 out of more than 4 million registered firms in Great Britain. Repeated studies suggest that this leads to an underestimation of the scale of the creative workforce.
	•	BRES data is based on the Standard Industrial Classifications – SIC codes – chosen by the employer, not on the occupation of the employee. Not all employers choose a SIC code which accurately represents the nature of their business, which can in turn lead to further discrepancies in estimates of employment.
	•	BRES counts of employment are based on payroll returns to a workplace location. LinkedIn asks subscribers to identify a location but does not specify that it needs to be their place of employment. Some of those identifying a Kirklees location may therefore be employed (either salaried or freelance) in other parts of West Yorkshire, South Yorkshire, Greater Manchester or further afield.
Innovative approaches to address gaps in workforce data 
To address some of the shortcomings of ONS data, we repeated an analysis of LinkedIn initially carried out in 2022. This confirmed that the creative workforce was made up of both employed, self-employed and freelance workers. It also counts individuals who may be underemployed, in that they may be undertaking some creative work as part of a ‘portfolio’ of other work, as well as the unemployed.
At the time of our analysis in March 2024, Kirklees had a creative workforce in excess if 9,400 people.

Our 2024 study reflected a significant increase amongst LinkedIn subscribers (883 individuals, 50%) in Creative Content occupations, including music. There was also strong growth in Creative Services (464 individuals, 15%) and Creative Originals (261, 20%). The percentage increase was greatest in Creative Experiences, which grew by 54% from a lower base. 
A change in LinkedIn Industry definitions early in 2024 meant that we could not carry out a ‘like for like’ analysis in every case. In particular, the changes removed the previous code for ‘Information Technology and Services’, used in 2022 analysis of a combined ‘Software’ sector, which resulted in a seemingly precipitous fall in Kirklees’ software development workforce. However, this means that the LinkedIn evidence for the combined workforce of 1,512 in Computer Games (included within the Creative Content supply chain), Software Development and Social Networking Platforms much more closely reflects the BRES total of 1,225 for the equivalent DCMS Creative Industries sub-sector of IT, software and computer services than was the case in our previous analysis. 	•	Those working in public-sector managed museums and libraries tend to be less likely to use LinkedIn as a source of employment opportunities
	•	The proportion of artists and creative freelancers using LinkedIn is anecdotally reported as being lower than that for platforms which may bring them more directly into contact with buyers or commissioners, including Instagram, Facebook and Etsy.

 Why do the counts of employment and workforce differ so much? 
Analysis of LinkedIn showed that the creative workforce in Kirklees in 2024 was 5,937 larger than the employment total for 2022 reported in national statistics. The discrepancy between employment totals in BRES and our analysis of LinkedIn can be attributed to:
	•	A continuing trend of growth in creative employment between 2022 (the latest date for which BRES data were available) and the time of our measurement in 2024 – although this was unlikely to make up the difference of more than 2,500 full time and part time employed roles between BRES and LinkedIn.
	•	Some of those identifying as being in full or part time employment may have done so whilst working in a freelance or self-employed capacity – LinkedIn is self-reporting, rather than monitored through payroll records.
	•	It is probable that none of those given their occupation type as freelance and self-employed, and only a small proportion of those identifying as contractors or interns, would have been registered for PAYE. Most would, therefore, have been excluded from the BRES data.

Creative workforce in West Yorkshire
Analysis of the creative workforce across the West Yorkshire Combined Authority region, carried out at the same time as the Kirklees study, identified 87,434 individuals with creative occupations. Whilst a significant proportion of that workforce was likely to be employed within the city ‘core’ of Leeds, it also reflected the presence of a pool of creative talent available to support the growth of creative businesses in Kirklees. 
Data for West Yorkshire reflected significant clustering of activity in advertising and marketing, software development and film and television.
Student creative workforce
LinkedIn provides evidence of a supply of new entrants from the region’s Universities (including Huddersfield) and local colleges: more than 1,600 students in Kirklees were registered on LinkedIn as having a creative occupation, out of a total of 12,939 across West Yorkshire. Adding students to the count brought the total creative workforce in Kirklees to over 11,000 individuals, and that of West Yorkshire to over 100,000.

Clustering
Nesta’s 2016 report into The Geography of Creativity  holds an important lesson for smaller creative districts such as Kirklees:
“Specialising in a smaller number of creative sub-sectors with a high technology component … may be less ‘hip’ than creative cities like Brighton, Liverpool and Glasgow, but our research suggests they make significant economic contributions. In particular, they are associated with larger-sized creative businesses, and potentially higher levels of business productivity.”
Sector analysis and the evidence of relatively poor levels of connectivity and interaction in the creative sector across the district highlight the need for different models of growth:
“Before jumping to the conclusion that stronger specialisation is the way to grow creative businesses and secure productivity gains, however, we need to remember that freelancers give creative businesses the flexibility to scale up and down in response to the vagaries of creative markets and help diffuse knowledge between creative businesses (thereby making important contributions to the creative ecosystem).”
Another risk to the cluster highlighted by Nesta’s research is evidence of undersupply of graduate talent from local HEIs, which, 
“tentatively point[s] to a potential barrier for the continued success of creative conurbations that appear to have less ready access to talent, research and knowledge exchange resources than those in other creative clusters.”
As alluded to in our summary of GVA above, analysis of creative occupations and outputs relative to LinkedIn-derived estimates of creative workforce DCMS Sector Estimates for economic output also gave a clearer impression of the extent of clustering of creative occupations within Kirklees. We observed a location quotient, or LQ, of 1.23 for creative industries and 1.55 for cultural sectors. These are significant because higher levels of agglomeration are a reflection of higher levels of specialisation and physical ‘clustering’ within and across creative industries, which in turn presage higher levels of collaboration and productivity by individuals and businesses within the cluster. 
LQs of 1.0 and above, meaning that the concentration of creative occupations within those sub-sectors was equal or greater to the average for the whole of Great Britain, were identified in:
	•	Crafts – 13.05 (in other words, craft occupations were relatively 13 times more concentrated in Kirklees than the average for Great Britain).
	•	Photography – when analysed as a stand-alone sub-sector of Culture, it had an LQ of 4.39.
	•	Design and designer fashion – 4.25
	•	Advertising and marketing – 2.80
	•	Music, performing and visual arts – 2.43
	•	Film, TV and Music (analysed using DCMS’s Cultural definition of the sub-sector, with Photography removed) – 1.05
	•	Architecture – 1.04
The LQ for any creative sub-sector is skewed by the outsized representation of those businesses in London and the South East, which together represent about 60% of the creative industries in Great Britain. Therefore, for any business outside those regions to register an LQ of 1.0 or above represents a significant level of agglomeration and a clear indication of opportunities for greater specialisation and productivity.




1.5	SUB-SECTOR TREND ANALYSES
Arts
Our updated analysis provided further evidence of the resilience of Kirklees performing and visual arts sector, which demonstrated an incredible ‘rebound’ in both workforce and output following the disruption of multiple lockdowns in 2020-21. It will remain important to maintain a dynamic cultural offer to ensure that Kirklees can retain and build upon the advantages offered by the diversity and dynamism of its creative artists and producers.
Advertising and marketing
Analysis of the creative services supply chain, including advertising, marketing, graphic design, digital agencies and architecture, demonstrated the emergence of this as a significant supply chain within Kirklees,, influenced in part by growth and in part by increasing specialisation within digital marketing drawing on development of both new product and software in new service offers.
Design and designer fashion
The 2016 study of Kirklees creative economy highlighted the strengths of design and this has been reinforced by our subsequent studies, which showed continued growth of the design workforce to 1,160 individuals and GVA of £53.5m. This growth has been supported by Wilson’s Republic, which continues to promote the borough’s design businesses and offers both networking, peer support and supply chain linkages within design and with other creative sectors. The influence of the University’s design courses has been a significant factor in the attraction and retention of design talent.
Film and television
BFI data shows that combined spend by film and high-end television production (HETV) 2023 reached £4.23 billion, 32% down on the record levels observed in 2022 as production ‘caught up’ with gaps in scheduling left by successive lockdowns, but almost level with pre-pandemic levels. Lacking significant production and postproduction infrastructure, Kirklees’ remains dependent on the ability of its creative producers to leverage opportunities and investment for new IP development. We have observed fluctuating levels of commissioning for crew and production services across the UK film and HETV sector which may leave Kirklees’ based freelances exposed to sudden changes in demand resulting in a ‘feast or famine’ environment.
Architecture
Although still characterised as a sector of micro and small enterprises, architecture practices tend to be larger than equivalent businesses in other creative sectors. A focus on identifying and growing existing practices to ensure that they continue to be able to specialise and attract talent could be further developed with support from the University of Huddersfield and greater engagement of sub-regional and wider sector networks .
Megatrends
Our wider research has identified two key ‘megatrends’ which will have a significant impact on the development of creative industries in Kirklees, as much as in other places: 
	•	The future of agency and client relationships
	•	AI and the future of creative work
	•	Future of agency and client relationships: Kirklees' advertising and marketing sector has shown significant recent growth; but may need to adapt to changing client expectations and the rise of in-housing. In context of the increased opportunities presented by the growth of digital marketing, software development and film and HETV across West Yorkshire, this could involve developing more specialised services or focusing on areas where they can maintain influence on creative development. 
	•	AI and the future of creative work: The impact of AI on Kirklees' creative workforce could be substantial. Our mapping of skills supply highlighted a relative lack of supply of programs in creative digital skills and support for workers transitioning to new roles. Kirklees does not currently boast any advantages in digital infrastructure which could be leveraged to explore innovative applications of AI in creative production – this may necessitate more collaborative relationships both between creative and tech businesses in Kirklees and more widely to leverage the high levels of investment in tech in the city cores of Leeds and Manchester.
To fully benefit from these trends, Kirklees should focus on:
	•	Developing specialised skills and services that complement rather than compete with in-house client capabilities.
	•	Investing in AI-related education and training programs to prepare businesses, the existing workforce and potential new entrants for future challenges and opportunities.
	•	Information, advice and guidance to prepare both graduates and those currently employed in sectors which may be at more risk of disruption from digital technologies for the opportunities in digital and creative.
	•	Leveraging existing strengths in music and performance to explore new formats and distribution channels enabled by these technologies.
By proactively addressing these megatrends, Kirklees can position its creative industries for future growth and competitiveness in the evolving digital landscape.

SECTION 2: MUSIC IN KIRKLEES
In addition to its economic output, music plays a critical role in engaging people in cultural activity and production. Kirklees Year of Music 2023 demonstrated strengths in both professional and amateur sectors, and in the use of music in other activity, including festivals, teaching and therapeutic settings. In addition to its direct contribution, music contributes to the value of nearly every other creative sector and the same elements of the production supply chain also serve live events, festivals, television and film. 
Kirklees contains some part of nearly every element of the professional music supply chain, including both live and recorded music, labels, promoters, talent management, venues, recording and rehearsal spaces and production services such as staging, lighting and sound. KYOM23 also demonstrated a strong community base of bands, choirs and non-commercial performance venues including churches and community centres. In 2022 we estimated that there were upward of 120 places across Kirklees at which live music was enjoyed in some form at some point in the year – findings which need to be updated using the evaluation of KYOM23.
Scale and significance of Kirklees’ music economy

	•	Musicians - £28.7m

The 2022 report also included an estimated £21m for music tourism, based on an 8% apportionment of music tourist spend for Yorkshire and the Humber in proportion to Kirklees’ population. SECTION 3:  STRENGTHS AND SHORTCOMINGS
The outcomes of the research are in some cases contradictory and low levels of published data on the economic output of freelance workers make it difficult to gain a complete understanding of the productivity of Kirklees’ creative sectors, and its comparative standing with neighbouring districts with higher levels of creative employment.
Strengths and shortcomings
The overall picture is of a growing business base, with increases in creative employment and a healthy and thriving cultural ecology at grassroots level across the district. However, the evidence suggests that Kirklees creative businesses are smaller than both the national and city region average, which may have implications for their resilience, productivity and capacity to adapt to changing market opportunities. This is reinforced by the lack of investment in R&D amongst those businesses, despite the efforts of University of Huddersfield to promote this. Benefits of the creative sector are not evenly spread; towns in the east of the district including Batley and Dewsbury have far lower numbers of businesses, although this is somewhat redressed by employment within Dewsbury’s textiles manufacturing sector.
Opportunities
In our 2022 report, we stated:
‘By taking a more active role in supporting skills development and investment in high growth sectors including film and high end television, it could quickly start to address the productivity gap between itself and its larger metropolitan neighbours, Leeds and Manchester. Those commercial opportunities contain a pathway to increase the diversity and inclusion in employment and leadership of the cultural and creative sectors, in turn presenting new pathways to employment for some of Kirklees’ most excluded and socio-economically deprived communities. In the process, this would help cement the gains made by the Council’s consistent investment in cultural events over more than a decade, strengthening Kirklees’ narrative as a borough with a strong and distinctive cultural offer, in particular around music, and a great place for creative practitioners to base themselves and their businesses.’
In revisiting the evidence for the sector in 2024, we found nothing to contradict this analysis. However, we would add that the accelerating pace of adoption of digital technologies, in particular Generative AI, represents an as-yet unmet opportunity to a whole range of creative producers in Kirklees, including those in arts and culture.
Strengths
The findings highlight some key strengths:
	•	The sector is larger than reflected in national statistics, both in number of businesses and in employment terms.
	•	Sector output is conservatively estimated at £250 million – a 83% increase since the last study of this kind, undertaken in 2022. We have seen growth in both workforce and productivity over the last two years.
	•	The overall growth of creative employment in Kirklees seems not to have been stalled by the impact of Covid-19, although the picture for creative businesses in mixed, with a growth in startups not yet reflected in BRES data for registered companies. We observed that impact and rate of recovery were not evenly distributed across creative sectors.
	•	Kirklees benefits both from a large and active community of creative freelances and a much larger workforce in its immediate hinterland, which stretches across large parts of West and South Yorkshire and into the Eastern edge of Greater Manchester. In particular, clustering of digital marketing, software development and film and TV production in Leeds’ city ‘core’ has resulted in dramatic increases in both the scale and productivity of the creative workforce across West Yorkshire.
	•	The impact of creative businesses and employment is felt more widely across the district than has previously been observed, with significant activity in places such as Cleckheaton, Mirfield and Holme Valley as well as. In and around Huddersfield town centre.
	•	Kirklees is home to some significant creative assets – cultural institutions such as the Lawrence Batley Theatre are complemented by the large and activist presence of the University of Huddersfield and an eclectic mix of commercial and community venues for arts, culture and live performance in all genres.
	•	The borough is home to internationally significant music festivals including Huddersfield Contemporary Music Festival, Cleckheaton Folk Festival and Marsden Jazz Festival.
Shortcomings
Evidence of wider benefit from the sector does not extend to areas such as Batley and Dewsbury.
The inconsistency of evidence around the supply of software development skills and the prevalence of cross-sector collaboration in production and R&D suggested that Kirklees’ creative sector, including arts and cultural practitioners, may be less able to respond quickly and effectively to threats and opportunities presented by the advance of digital technologies, particularly in applications of Gen AI.
At a strategic level, the borough has not identified how it might take a leadership role in any of the initiatives to support culture being promoted by West Yorkshire’s elected mayor, herself a native of Batley and actress and screenwriter by trade.
Opportunities
There are some clear opportunities for Kirklees to strengthen its narrative, attract more investment to the sector and provide new opportunities for skills development and employment in the creative sector which would help “level up” its most deprived communities. These include:
	•	Ensure that the Council and the borough’s content and production services businesses are fully engaged with regional initiatives in film and television, which is a fast-growing market with shortages of skills and talent which could be addressed by Kirklees residents.
	•	Use evidence from the evaluation of Kirklees Year of Music 2023 to continue to build capacity amongst and emphasise the supply chain opportunities between Kirklees-based musicians, music enterprises and production services businesses, as well as the opportunities to promote the brough as a destination for music tourism.
	•	Engage local education providers, particularly at FE level, to address gaps in the provision of creative skills training and ensure sure that students can pursue creative skills pathways in specific disciplines from entry to higher level without having to move away from Kirklees.
	•	Improve provision of training in a range of creative technologies which are already a minimum requirement for entry into many creative industries occupations. Analysis of industry ‘megatrends’ highlighted how provision of ‘future skills’ around digital production and application of AI were increasingly essential determinants of the success of initiatives to help improve business planning and workforce development in all sub-sectors of the large cohort of creative microbusinesses in Kirklees, not just those involved in creation of digital content.

Appendix 1: Methodologies used to calculate GVA of Kirklees’ creative economy
The estimate for 2020 GVA output given in our equivalent report in 2022 was £176m. The discrepancy (a reduction in reported GVA of £20m) is accounted for by availability of more up-to-date and verified statistics for industrial output and productivity, recalculated by ONS to take account of the impact of lockdowns on economic activity and available on a narrower set of industry definitions (e.g., 2-digit SIC codes which offered a higher level of accuracy than the SIC 07 Section codes used to produce our earlier estimate.) The estimated GVA for 2020 in our 2022 report also included significant contributions from arts and culture (£50 million) and music and music tourism (£36 million) for which no equivalent data were available to us for 2022 output when we came to do this report owing to changes in methodology – e.g., MusicUK has not updated its ‘Wish You Were Here’ analysis of the economic contribution of music tourism since 2020.
Revisions to the original report
For our 2024 revision of the report, We applied a multi-method approach over the period January-May 2024. As well as drawing upon more narrowly defined and updated definitions of output and productivity from ONS and DCMS Sector Estimates to arrive at estimates of GVA which were both more accurate and more easily repeatable than those used in 2022, by combining evidence of creative workforce from LinkedIn with those official statistics we we able to offer a clearer sense of the potential for growth in workforce and productivity.
Situational analysis and interrogation of ‘megatrends’ in the sector and wider economy were used to derive scenarios for the future prospects of the cluster.
Clustering
There are several barriers to defining clustering of digital and creative industries at the city region level. 
The first is the national statistical evidence base. As Section I (above) demonstrates, national figures underestimate Kirklees’ business and employment base in the cultural and creative industries, often by a significant margin. This means that the standard measure of geographical agglomeration of the sectors which make up the creative economy - ‘LQ’, or location quotient - will also understate the extent and economic impact of clustering in absolute terms and relative both to the national picture for the sector and to the wider LCR economy. To provide more accurate estimates, we have constructed a different set of measures of LQ, drawing on a comparison of LinkedIn data and DCMS Sector Estimates, to inform our analysis of clustering and output. 
Data on GVA
Data on GVA were derived from multi-method analysis of a range of sources including:
	•	BRES data on employment for 2022 (which allowed us to estimate the clustering of creative employment, reflected in a Location Quotient or LQ)
	•	April 2024 release of ONS Subregional data on economic output per industry Section or sub-section (see Table 19)
	•	ONS Subregional Productivity June 2024 release (which provided data up to and including 2022, allowing us to compare data for 2022 with our previous report based on 2020 data)
	•	DCMS Economic Estimates for Creative Industries and Cultural sectors. We applied these conservatively, basing our estimates on 2022 data – the last year for which returns had been consolidated at time of our analysis, and consistent with the ONS datasets for wider industry outputs and regional productivity – rather than using the latest data available (up to the first quarter of 2024).

Appendix 3: Education and skills
Skills gaps for creative freelances and microbusinesses
We carried out an assessment of skills needs for creative freelances and microbusiness on behalf of WYCA as part of the development of a new skills and business support training programme. This suggested that current skills gaps within West Yorkshire fell into three broad categories:
Freelance development support  
While some current freelance development activities existed, our research showed they were often not tailored to sectoral needs or targeted at freelancers within a specific district. An exception was Wakefield's Xcellerator programme, originally aimed at Wakefield creatives, with some places later opened to WY creatives; and the Creative Bubbles programme, which has extended its reach from Kirklees to include other West Yorkshire businesses.
Creative freelancers and micro-businesses tended to wear many hats for different income streams and were not attracted to the current business support model. Support therefore needed to be more tailored to the sector – flexible and preferably delivered by respected and valued practitioners. We noted that the freelance community was not defined by recent graduates but included many, often highly experienced, individuals who may have been working in the sector over a number of years and who were often multi-skilled across a range of sub-sectors.
Support for leaders and managers
The review of skills needs documentation showed gaps in any form of development support for leaders and/or managers within the creative workforce. The West Yorkshire Consortium of Colleges acknowledged in their report (Let's Talk Real Skills: Creative sector) that leadership skills represented a significant skills gap in the sector, both currently and going forward. 
The same gap was highlighted in the Bradford Producing Hub report. A creative leadership program run by Creative Wakefield in 2022, developed in conjunction with Leeds University Business School, attracted significant interest from the sector but was confined to Wakefield leaders only.
The importance of having the right leaders in place to move Bradford's cultural ecology forward and position it as an energiser for change was seen as vital. The JCNA and the Workforce report for the Bradford 2025 bid both identified the need to invest in creating the next generation of leaders in the arts and cultural sector to maintain momentum and culture's position in Bradford's offer post-2025 (BPH Report, June 2023).
The importance of this was further highlighted within BPH's 'Culture is Our Place' strategy, which commited to drawing 50% of Bradford's creative workforce, audiences, and cultural leadership from people currently underrepresented in the culture and creative industries sector.
New and future skills for the sector
Our review of data showed gaps emerging around the application of digital skills related to new technologies. Interviewees drawn from employers, colleges and universities reported a gap in terms of possessing the technical skills required to create or perform, but there was also an increasingly evident issue of creatives not having the skills required to respond to changes in demands of work that necessitated a more digital approach to production.
Skills needs
Freelance development support
The needs expressed were around a wide range of business support – from business development, marketing yourself and your business, financial planning and fundraising, pitching, etc., relating to business support skills such as financial management and strategic planning. This included sole traders who perceived that they lacked the necessary skills to grow their business, and employers who felt that their staff had some skills in this area but did not have a broad range of business areas. Fundraising skills were also one of the key skills needs reported in our research.
Support for leaders and managers
The review of skills needs documentation highlighted the need for leadership development across many sub-sectors – dance, music, production, etc. The speed of change within the sector, ever-evolving consumer trends, and disruptive technologies demanded sector-specific support. There was a need for more distributed models of leadership which gave individuals at all levels of an organisation the autonomy to make decisions and contribute to the effective operation of the business. Furthermore, business leaders needed to develop the skills to manage the digital change process.
WYCA plans to complement the sector-specific training in skills and business support with a network of sector "champions" across the region for the development of future activity, with contracting to be concluded in July 2024 – that may contribute to meeting the needs identified above.
New and future skills for the sector
The creative industries have long used a range of technologies to create, distribute and attract audiences and clients. However, the rapid pace of recent technological changes continued to disrupt traditional methods. Immersive technologies (XR), Artificial Intelligence (AI), cloud technologies, and 5G all impact the day-to-day working of individuals and businesses within the sector. With increasing overlap between the digital and creative industries at all levels and within all sub-sectors – music, dance, theatre, advertising, production – new technologies and approaches were not only in demand but seen as vital for the future growth of micro-businesses and job opportunities for freelancers.
