
Resilient Cave Worlds: Deep-time heritage as living laboratories for virtual cultural evolution
Part B - Research and innovation action
1. Excellence
1.1 Objectives and ambition
1.1.1 Project objectives
The Resilient Cave Worlds project establishes Palaeolithic cave art sites as living laboratories for understanding how cultures evolve within virtual environments. These sites, representing humanity's first immersive storytelling spaces where firelight animated painted figures and acoustics shaped ritual experience, provide a unique lens for examining cultural transmission and community formation in constructed realities.
Primary objective: To develop and validate frameworks for virtual cultural evolution through systematic investigation of deep-time heritage sites as active spaces where past and present converge to generate new forms of cultural expression.
Specific objectives:
To establish robust technical infrastructure enabling massive multi-user virtual heritage experiences that maintain archaeological authenticity whilst supporting emergent cultural practices.
To advance archaeological methodology through VR-enabled research that reveals new insights about cave art creation, use, and meaning through immersive analysis and experimental archaeology.
To activate cultural institutions and communities as co-creators in virtual heritage, developing sustainable models for implementation across diverse contexts.
To demonstrate how virtual environments can serve as spaces for meaningful cultural evolution, where collective practices emerge through the interaction of ancient heritage and contemporary expression.
To develop ethical frameworks ensuring respectful representation of indigenous knowledge systems and equitable access to virtual heritage experiences.
1.1.2 Ambition beyond state of the art
Current virtual heritage initiatives primarily create static reconstructions for individual viewing. This project fundamentally reconceptualises heritage sites as dynamic laboratories where cultural practices evolve through collective interaction. We move beyond documentation to generation, creating spaces where new cultural expressions emerge from the dialogue between deep-time heritage and contemporary creativity.
The innovation lies in treating cave art sites not as artefacts to be preserved but as active methodologies to be understood and applied. By examining how Palaeolithic communities used caves as technologies for cultural transmission, combining visual narrative, acoustic design, and embodied ritual, we develop principles directly applicable to designing virtual worlds that support authentic cultural evolution.
This represents an exponential increase in temporal scope compared to existing digital heritage projects, which typically focus on recent centuries. By focusing on the 40,000-year vista of European rock art, we expose the deep, indexical, porous, and interconnected roots of European culture, demonstrating its ancient fluidity across geographical boundaries. This offers a powerful counter-narrative to contemporary fragmentation.
1.2 Methodology
1.2.1 Overall methodology and approach
The methodology employs three interconnected living laboratories structured around a foundational technical infrastructure, with archaeological innovation and cultural activation working iteratively to generate new knowledge and applications. This approach recognises that understanding virtual cultural evolution requires simultaneous advancement across technical, archaeological, and cultural domains.
Lab 1 - Technical infrastructure and social VR
Lab 1 establishes the technical foundation that enables all project activities. Building on Froehlich's extensive experience with social VR systems and the proven capabilities of the VR4More platform developed by Kulik, this laboratory creates what we term "Resilient Cave Worlds": immersive environments that can support large-scale collaborative exploration while maintaining the intimate, contemplative qualities essential to heritage experience. 
The technical innovation centres on solving the fundamental challenge of scale in heritage VR. Traditional approaches struggle to accommodate the throughput demands of major heritage institutions. Museo Altamira, for example, receives 300,000 annual visitors, requiring solutions that can handle massive concurrent usage whilst maintaining quality of experience. Our approach enables parallel instances supporting groups of 20 to 30 users each, with the capability to scale to thousands of simultaneous participants across multiple virtual cave environments.
The VR4More platform provides the technical backbone through its proven multi-user capabilities and cross-device compatibility. The platform's efficient data streaming architecture enables detailed digital twins to be accessed across devices, from high-end VR headsets to smartphones and tablets. This democratises access by removing hardware barriers that typically limit virtual heritage experiences to well-resourced institutions or individuals. 
Integration of embodied AI agents represents a significant advancement in heritage interpretation. These agents serve as knowledgeable guides capable of natural conversation about cave art, archaeological context, and cultural significance. However, their implementation requires careful consideration of what Froehlich terms "acceptable social interaction", ensuring that AI responses remain culturally appropriate and factually grounded. The agents are configured with specific knowledge bases developed in collaboration with our archaeological partners, avoiding the hallucination problems common in general-purpose language models.
Environmental simulation capabilities extend far beyond basic lighting effects to include acoustic modelling, humidity simulation, and temporal variations that reflect seasonal and historical changes in cave conditions. This responds to insights from our archaeological partners about the importance of understanding caves as dynamic environments that have changed significantly over millennia. Users can experience how natural light penetration varies throughout the year, how acoustic properties affect the perception of cave art, and how environmental conditions may have influenced prehistoric usage patterns.
The platform development follows an iterative approach with regular testing cycles involving both researchers and public users. Initial prototypes focus on single-cave experiences with limited user numbers, progressively scaling to support multiple interconnected cave environments with hundreds of simultaneous users. Each iteration incorporates feedback from archaeological researchers about accuracy and research utility, from cultural practitioners about creative possibilities, and from public users about engagement and accessibility.
Labs 2 and 3 form part of an iterative research cycle which works together with the core integrative framework (WP4: Histories to futures – detailed below). This is a critical theoretical framework that ensures technical innovations and creative experiments remain grounded in rigorous analysis of the philosophical, ethical, and cultural implications of immersive heritage representation.
Lab 2 - Archaeological innovation and knowledge generation
Building on the technical infrastructure, Lab 2 transforms archaeological methodology by establishing VR replicas as legitimate research-generating tools that extend interpretation beyond traditional fieldwork limitations. Led by Ruiz-Redondo, whose field experience spans decorated caves across six countries including Altamira, La Pasiega, and Chauvet, this laboratory demonstrates how immersive technologies can reveal new insights about prehistoric human behaviour and cultural practices that are impossible to obtain through conventional archaeological methods.
The core innovation lies in what we term "embodied archaeological exploration", enabling archaeologists to experience cave environments as dynamic, lived spaces rather than static repositories of artefacts. Using the social VR platform developed in Lab 1, archaeologists conduct collaborative research sessions within virtual cave environments, testing hypotheses about spatial usage, lighting conditions, and social organisation that cannot be explored in fragile physical sites.
The methodology draws on extensive prior digitisation work. Fatas and Bayarri's comprehensive 3D documentation of Altamira's Polychrome Hall using georeferenced GPR and photogrammetry provides unprecedented detail about cave structure and art placement. Their analysis reveals how environmental factors, including water infiltration patterns and geological instabilities, have affected cave art preservation over millennia. This data forms the foundation for creating virtual environments that accurately represent not just current conditions but also probable prehistoric states.
Ruiz-Redondo's visibility analysis and audience studies provide crucial insights into how caves functioned as social spaces. His research demonstrates that art placement often corresponds to acoustic hotspots and areas of maximum visibility from gathering points, suggesting deliberate design for collective experiences. In virtual environments, these hypotheses can be tested systematically by simulating different group configurations and lighting conditions.
Temporal reconstruction capabilities allow researchers to experience caves as they appeared during different periods of prehistoric occupation. This includes removing modern interventions such as walkways and lighting systems, reconstructing original floor levels based on excavation data, and modelling how natural light penetration and acoustic properties may have differed when cave entrances had different configurations. The ability to "rewind" caves to their prehistoric states provides unprecedented opportunities for understanding how these spaces were originally experienced and used.
Experimental archaeology within virtual environments enables researchers to conduct lighting experiments using virtual torches and fires based on archaeological evidence of prehistoric lighting technologies. Acoustic experiments test how sound propagates through cave spaces and how this might have influenced ritual activities. Movement pattern analysis examines how groups would have navigated through caves and gathered in specific chambers. These experiments generate quantitative data about prehistoric cave usage that can be compared with archaeological evidence from excavations and surface analysis.
Cross-cultural comparative analysis leverages partnerships with heritage institutions across Europe. The Archaeological Museum of Istria provides access to Romuald's Cave, offering insights into Adriatic coastal cave usage. Museo Altamira contributes decades of research on one of Europe's most significant cave art sites. By examining how different cultural groups utilised cave environments, patterns emerge in art placement, spatial organisation, and environmental modification that may reflect broader cultural practices across prehistoric Europe.
The integration of AI-assisted pattern recognition, developed in collaboration with the technical team, enables identification of previously overlooked connections between images. Machine learning algorithms trained on archaeological data can identify stylistic similarities, compositional patterns, and potential symbolic relationships that human observers might miss. This computational archaeology approach complements traditional visual analysis while maintaining archaeological rigour through human validation of all AI-generated insights.
Lab 3 - Cultural activation
Lab 3 bridges creative practice with sustainable sector transformation through a carefully structured progression from artistic experimentation to sector-wide deployment. Led by Jones, whose expertise in blockchain applications for cultural assets and post-digital library infrastructures provides essential foundations for new models of cultural value creation, this laboratory demonstrates how virtual heritage environments can serve as generative spaces for contemporary cultural production.
The laboratory operates through three distinct but interconnected phases across the project timeline. Year 1 focuses on creative commissions and experimental work that reframes VR caves as "post-museum spaces" for narrative, aesthetic, and theoretical experimentation. Contemporary artists are commissioned to create new works that explore the relationship between ancient and contemporary culture, using the virtual cave environments as creative laboratories. These commissions, developed in partnership with AND Festival, explore how immersive heritage spaces can inspire new forms of artistic expression that wouldn't be possible in physical caves or traditional gallery settings.
The integration of [Paul] Coulton's expertise in creative experimentation in games and UX ensures that artistic commissions engage meaningfully with the interactive possibilities of virtual environments rather than simply transposing traditional art forms into digital spaces. This collaboration explores how game design principles can enhance heritage experiences while maintaining cultural authenticity and respect for archaeological evidence.
Blockchain and NFT experimentation builds on Jones's research into "Artists Rethinking the Blockchain" to explore new models for digital provenance and cultural asset ownership. Drawing inspiration from [Nicole] Wilson's work on Ötzi's tattoos, which Fontana identifies as an example of how blockchain technology can ensure the "survival in the contemporary age" for cultural information, the laboratory pilots systems for tracking the provenance of virtual heritage assets and enabling new forms of creator compensation.
Year 2 transitions to IP/platform exploration and industry integration, building on creative experiments to develop sustainable business models for virtual heritage experiences. This phase involves intensive collaboration with Sony Digital Imaging to test commercial applications of the technologies and methodologies developed in the project. The partnership includes multiple creative production sessions at Sony's Pinewood facility, where artists and heritage professionals work with Sony's advanced virtual production tools, including the Verona LED wall technology, to create high-quality immersive content.
Industry integration testing involves partnerships with creative industry organisations, particularly CEPI (the European Audiovisual Production association), to explore how virtual heritage technologies can be integrated into existing cultural production workflows. This includes testing new licensing models, exploring co-production opportunities, and developing standards for virtual heritage content that can be adopted across the European cultural sector.
Year 3 focuses on museum and festival activation, translating experimental work into practical applications that can be adopted by heritage institutions across Europe. The deployment strategy recognises that different institutions have vastly different technical capabilities and visitor throughput requirements. The Archaeological Museum of Istria, with its regional focus and limited resources, requires different solutions from Museo Altamira, which must manage hundreds of thousands of annual visitors.
Festival integration leverages connections with major European cultural events to demonstrate the public engagement potential of virtual heritage experiences. Collaborations with LEV Festival and other major European cultural events showcase how virtual heritage can enhance traditional festival programming while reaching new audiences who might not typically engage with archaeological content.
The "Virtual-Ready" playbook development represents the culmination of all laboratory activities, synthesising insights into practical guidance that can be adopted by cultural institutions across Europe. The playbook is structured as a modular resource that institutions can adapt based on their specific needs, technical capabilities, and audience requirements.
WP4: Histories to futures
Underpinning all three laboratories is a critical theoretical framework that ensures technical innovations and creative experiments remain grounded in rigorous analysis of the philosophical, ethical, and cultural implications of immersive heritage representation. Led by Pinotti, whose work on the "paleocybernetic" concept directly links Upper Palaeolithic cave art to contemporary VR, this framework ensures that our approach goes beyond technical achievement to address fundamental questions about cultural representation and evolution.
The theoretical framework centres on what Pinotti and Fontana term the "paleocybernetic" perspective, which recognises both ancient cave art and modern VR as "environmentalised images" that dissolve traditional boundaries between viewer and viewed. This perspective challenges conventional approaches to heritage representation by acknowledging the fundamental impossibility of authentic reconstruction while exploring the productive possibilities that emerge from this limitation.
Fontana's critical analysis of "paradoxical hyperrealism" examines how digital reconstructions create representations where "the referent is not there, or rather it exists as an aggregation of data, some inferred by comparison." This analysis explores how users navigate the tension between the apparent authenticity of immersive experiences and their awareness of the technological mediation involved. Understanding this tension is crucial for developing heritage representations that are both engaging and intellectually honest.
The framework explicitly confronts how "Paleoart" has historically "confronted prehistoric humanity," acknowledging its "racist and colonialist biases" (Fontana) to ensure a new, ethically-informed practice. This critical perspective informs all project activities, from technical development to artistic commissioning, ensuring that our representations promote what Fontana describes as a "new sensibility" and "aspiration for equality" in cultural representation.
Interdisciplinary seminars bring together archaeologists, media theorists, philosophers, and artists to examine the implications of immersive heritage representation. These seminars address fundamental questions about the nature of historical authenticity, the ethics of representing cultures that cannot consent to their representation, and the political dimensions of heritage interpretation. The seminars are structured around key theoretical concepts including posthumanism, new materialism, and media archaeology, providing intellectual frameworks for understanding how virtual heritage experiences shape contemporary cultural understanding.
1.2.2 Research questions and hypotheses
The project addresses three primary research questions that emerge from the intersection of archaeology, technology, and cultural practice:
RQ1: How did Palaeolithic communities use caves as immersive technologies for cultural transmission, and what can this teach us about designing virtual worlds that support cultural evolution?
This question drives the archaeological research in Lab 2, using VR not just to document but to understand the sophisticated ways ancient humans created meaning through spatial experience. We hypothesise that caves functioned as carefully designed immersive environments where visual, acoustic, and embodied elements combined to transmit cultural knowledge across generations. The research tests this through:
	•	Systematic analysis of art placement in relation to acoustic properties and sight lines
	•	Experimental recreation of prehistoric lighting conditions and their effects on image perception
	•	Comparative studies across multiple cave sites to identify common patterns in spatial organisation
	•	Investigation of how movement through cave spaces structured narrative and ritual experiences
RQ2: What technical and social infrastructures are necessary to support authentic cultural expression in massive multi-user virtual heritage environments?
This question guides Lab 1's development of scalable platforms that balance archaeological accuracy with space for contemporary expression. We hypothesise that successful virtual heritage requires not just photorealistic reproduction but carefully designed affordances for collective meaning-making. The research explores:
Technical architectures that can support hundreds of simultaneous users without compromising experience quality
AI moderation systems that understand cultural context and can distinguish between respectful reinterpretation and inappropriate behaviour
Interface designs that enable both expert archaeological research and public engagement
Cross-device compatibility strategies that democratise access without sacrificing functionality
RQ3: How can virtual heritage experiences activate cultural institutions and communities as co-creators rather than passive consumers of heritage?
This question shapes Lab 3's engagement strategies, moving beyond traditional museum display paradigms. We hypothesise that virtual heritage becomes meaningful when communities can actively shape and contribute to evolving cultural narratives within these spaces. The research investigates:
Creative methodologies that enable contemporary artists to engage meaningfully with ancient heritage
Business models that make virtual heritage sustainable for institutions with varying resources
Pedagogical frameworks that use virtual heritage for active learning rather than passive consumption
Community engagement protocols that respect indigenous knowledge while enabling new interpretations
1.2.3 Interdisciplinary integration
The project requires deep integration across disciplines, achieved through structured mechanisms that ensure knowledge flows between domains. This integration is not merely additive but transformative, generating new interdisciplinary knowledge that none of the constituent disciplines could produce independently.
Methodological integration
Archaeological expertise grounds all technical development in empirical evidence about past human behaviour. Every technical feature implemented in the VR platform must serve a research purpose identified by archaeologists or enhance understanding of prehistoric cave use. Computer science innovation makes possible new forms of archaeological investigation and cultural experience that extend far beyond traditional fieldwork limitations. Anthropological perspectives ensure virtual environments support authentic cultural practice, drawing on ethnographic studies of how communities engage with heritage spaces. Museum studies expertise translates research insights into practical implementation strategies that acknowledge the diverse realities of cultural institutions.
Integration occurs through structured mechanisms designed to prevent disciplinary silos:
Quarterly cross-laboratory workshops where teams share findings and identify synergies
Joint supervision of doctoral researchers ensuring next-generation scholars develop truly interdisciplinary perspectives
Integrated deliverables requiring input from multiple laboratories, preventing isolated development
Shared data infrastructure ensuring all teams access common resources while respecting disciplinary methodologies
The Virtual Agora as integration mechanism
The Virtual Agora concept, developed by Robinson and Cassidy through their Pleito Cave prototype, serves as both metaphor and method for interdisciplinary integration. Just as ancient agoras were spaces for exchange of goods, ideas, and cultural practices, our virtual agoras become spaces where disciplines meet to create understanding that none could achieve alone. 
The Pleito Cave prototype demonstrates this integration in practice. Archaeological data about Native American rock art provides the foundation, but the virtual environment enables new forms of collaborative interpretation that wouldn't be possible at the physical site. Computer scientists develop novel visualisation techniques that reveal patterns invisible to the naked eye. Anthropologists observe how different communities interact with the virtual space, generating insights about cultural engagement with heritage. Museum professionals test different interpretation strategies, identifying what works for diverse audiences.
This integration extends to the partnership with Sony Digital Imaging, which brings industrial-grade virtual production capabilities to heritage research. Sony's Verona LED wall technology and advanced pre-visualisation tools enable rapid prototyping of immersive experiences, allowing researchers to test different approaches to heritage representation before committing to full development. The partnership ensures that academic research benefits from cutting-edge commercial technologies while contributing to the development of new applications for virtual production in cultural contexts.
Ethical integration
Ethical considerations are not confined to a single work package but integrated throughout all project activities. Fontana's analysis of historical biases in paleoart informs technical development, ensuring that avatar systems and AI agents avoid perpetuating stereotypes. Archaeological research protocols incorporate indigenous perspectives on appropriate representation of sacred sites. Creative commissions are evaluated not just for artistic merit but for cultural sensitivity and respect for heritage values. The ethics framework recognises that working with 40,000 years of human cultural expression requires exceptional care in representation. We cannot ask Palaeolithic artists for consent to digitise and reinterpret their work. This places a special responsibility on the project to ensure that our representations honour the cultural significance of these sites while acknowledging the impossibility of authentic reconstruction.
Temporal integration
The project's unique temporal scope, spanning 40,000 years of human cultural expression, requires integration across vastly different time scales. Archaeological time, measured in millennia, must be reconciled with the rapid pace of technological change and the immediate needs of contemporary cultural institutions. This temporal integration is achieved through:
Layered virtual environments that can represent multiple time periods simultaneously
Narrative frameworks that connect ancient and contemporary cultural practices
Predictive models that use patterns from deep history to anticipate future cultural evolution
Sustainable technical architectures that can evolve with changing technology while preserving core heritage content
1.2.4 Gender and inclusiveness
The gender dimension is fully integrated into our research and innovation content, particularly within the archaeological innovation and cultural activation laboratories. The project explicitly investigates how gender roles, expressions, and power dynamics evolve differently in virtual environments compared to traditional spaces, drawing comparisons from the archaeological record where possible. 
Archaeological research examines gendered representations in cave art and their interpretation in virtual spaces. This includes analysing how different genders may have participated in cave art creation and ritual activities, challenging traditional assumptions that have often excluded women from narratives about prehistoric cultural production. Virtual reconstructions enable testing of hypotheses about gendered use of cave spaces, examining whether certain areas were associated with specific gender roles or activities. 
Technical development ensures that avatar systems and interaction mechanisms avoid perpetuating gender stereotypes. This includes providing diverse avatar options that don't enforce binary gender categories, designing interaction mechanisms that don't favour typically masculine or feminine interaction styles, and ensuring that AI agents use inclusive language and avoid gendered assumptions about user interests or capabilities.
Cultural activation explicitly seeks gender balance in artistic commissions and ensures that creative works explore diverse perspectives on heritage and identity. The project examines how immersive heritage experiences, designed with gender-inclusive principles, contribute to wellbeing and sense of purpose for diverse user groups, including those who may feel marginalised in traditional cultural spaces.
Accessibility considerations ensure that virtual heritage experiences don't exclude users with disabilities. This includes developing alternative interaction mechanisms for users with mobility limitations, providing audio descriptions and haptic feedback for users with visual impairments, and ensuring that content is cognitively accessible for users with learning differences.
1.2.5 Open science practices
Open science practices are fundamental to the project's methodology, enhancing transparency, reproducibility, and societal engagement. All research outputs, including peer-reviewed articles, reports, and policy briefs, are made openly accessible via institutional repositories or dedicated platforms. This ensures that insights generated through public funding remain publicly available.
A detailed Data Management Plan, developed by Month 6, ensures all research data follows FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). This includes:
Archaeological data: 3D scans, photogrammetry data, and excavation records deposited in established archaeological repositories with standardised metadata
Technical specifications: Platform architectures, code repositories, and API documentation made available through open-source licenses where commercial exploitation is not compromised
User research data: Anonymised ethnographic data, user studies, and engagement metrics made available for future research while protecting participant privacy
Creative outputs: Documentation of artistic processes and selected creative works made available under appropriate Creative Commons licenses
The project recognises that some data requires restricted access to protect cultural heritage or respect indigenous knowledge systems. Protocols developed in consultation with heritage communities govern access to sensitive information, balancing open science principles with cultural responsibilities. Living Labs inherently involve citizens and end-users in the co-creation of content and experiences, directly embodying open science principles of engaging diverse knowledge actors. Public participation in archaeological interpretation, artistic creation, and platform testing ensures that the project benefits from diverse perspectives and generates outcomes relevant to broad communities.
