# Strategic review of culture in Southampton
## Proposal from The Fifth Sector

**Submitted to:** Carolyn Abel, Director of Culture & Tourism, Southampton City Council  
**Date:** May 2026  
**Budget:** Not to exceed £25,000 (inclusive of expenses and VAT)  
**Deadline:** Final report by 4 September 2026

---

## What this review is actually for

Southampton Forward has existed in four distinct forms since 2010: as a fundraising vehicle, as a sector convening body, as a City of Culture bid vehicle, and as a legacy delivery organisation. Each iteration has reflected what the city needed at that moment. The legacy programme is now complete, the founding CEO has departed, and all staff bar one have left. The founding partners, when asked to recommit, reduced their contributions to the point where the council's share moved from 23% to 46% of total funding. Trustees were within days of winding the organisation up in March 2026.

The council stepped in — rightly — to fund a strategic review and preserve the organisation's legal existence and funder track record while that review takes place. But what is being asked is not how to improve Southampton Forward. It is what shape a cultural development function for Southampton and the wider sub-region should take, given that the social, economic and political realities of 2026 are substantially different from those of 2010 or even 2023. That includes being honest about what did not work in the legacy period, and why, without turning the review into an inquest.

The review also needs to produce something practical on day one of whatever comes next: a direction-of-travel playbook that gives any new or reconstituted entity a starting framework for vision, mission and purpose, not just a governance structure.

---

## Our reading of the brief

Several things shape our approach that a standard reading of the written brief would miss.

**The organisation failed to fundraise at the scale originally intended.** As a fundraising vehicle in its early years, it raised enough to cover staff costs and no more. That is not a minor operational shortfall; it is a structural question about what kind of organisation Southampton needs and whether an independent trust is the right vehicle for the development function at all.

**The legacy programme delivered well against its agreed objectives but generated frustration.** The impact report covers £5m of programme delivery across grassroots, capacity-building, governance and strategic capital work. There is genuine achievement in that record. There is also, as Carolyn has noted, significant anger in some quarters — some of it legitimate, some of it ego and miffedness — rooted in a perception that Southampton Forward sought to control and lead rather than distribute and convene. Understanding which of those is the common sentiment matters, because the next model has to be designed around the latter.

**The governance structure created conflicts of interest that were never resolved.** Founding partners were simultaneously funders, board trustees and decision-makers about the organisation's future. The council officer most responsible for culture was an observer, not a trustee. The chair of the board is chief executive of one of the founding partner organisations. These are not incidental design flaws; they shaped what Southampton Forward could and could not do. The review needs to name them clearly enough that any new model addresses them.

**The sub-regional picture is live, not theoretical.** Carolyn has already begun conversations with Eastleigh, Test Valley and New Forest. The four organisations that collaborated on MCA work — Southampton Forward, Hampshire Cultural Trust, Portsmouth Creates and Island Collective — are very different from each other, and not all of them are genuinely playing a development agency role, whatever they say. The review should map this honestly and shape the sub-regional dimension of any new model around what is real rather than what is asserted.

**The political ground has shifted in ways the brief did not anticipate.** Southampton City Council is now in no-overall-control minority Labour administration, with Reform UK holding 8 of 51 seats. The new council leader's position on culture funding, and whether she joins the Southampton Forward board, is being confirmed as this proposal is written. The council officer responsible for culture is in the middle of a restructure that may expand her remit to include economic development. Any future model has to be financially resilient in a budget environment where culture competes for resource in a hung council with a fiscally hostile opposition group.

**The HSCCA is real but distant.** The Hampshire and Solent Combined County Authority has no cultural remit in its initial devolved powers and no elected mayor until 2028. Planning around it as an imminent source of strategic leadership or investment is premature. What is live is the question of how Southampton's cultural voice is represented at that table before a mayor exists.

## Approach

The review runs in five strands over twelve weeks. They are partly sequential, partly parallel. The logic is: understand before generating options; generate options before recommending.

### Strand 1 — Desk research and evidence review (weeks 1–3)

We begin not with data but with reading. The starting point is Southampton Forward's own impact report (2023–25), read not as a validation exercise but as a primary source: what did the programme actually deliver, what did it not deliver, and what does the gap between the two tell us about organisational design, governance and relationships? The impact report was produced by Southampton Forward itself and is not an independent evaluation. The review will provide that independent read.

Alongside this we will review the original brief and founding documents for Southampton Forward, ACE's Place Partnership grant conditions, any existing evaluation evidence from the legacy programme, and Carolyn Abel's working notes and stakeholder list. Charles Freeman, whose knowledge of Southampton's cultural landscape spans decades and who coordinates Creative Network South, will provide an initial orientation to the institutional history and current relationship dynamics that no document can fully capture.

This strand also covers published evidence on the relationship between cultural infrastructure, health outcomes, economic development and place identity — not as desk filler but because the playbook the council has asked for needs to be grounded in what actually works, rather than in the assumptions that shaped Southampton Forward's founding mission.

### Strand 2 — Comparative model analysis (weeks 2–4)

We will examine a minimum of five models from elsewhere — not to find one to copy, but to understand what conditions each model requires to function and what trade-offs each involves. The selection will include:

**Liverpool** — a council-embedded cultural development function operating alongside a membership organisation (Cultural Liverpool). Carolyn's finance director has cited this as a possible direction. We will be specific about what the Liverpool model actually delivers, what it costs to run, and whether the conditions that make it work in Liverpool exist in Southampton.

**Plymouth** — a leaner, more strategic model built explicitly on distributed leadership rather than centralised control. Carolyn named this herself as a reference point, and the alignment with her own philosophy of how cultural development should work makes it the most important comparator to understand in depth.

**Wakefield** — a compact, council-adjacent model. We have worked there and can bring direct knowledge.

**[Two further comparators TBC]** — at least one of these will be an example where a similar organisation was wound up or significantly restructured, because the review should be honest about that as a realistic outcome and what it means in practice.

For each model we will assess: governance structure; funding base and financial resilience; relationship to the local authority; what the development function actually does versus what a membership or sector convening function does; and how it handles sub-regional relationships.

### Strand 3 — Consultation (weeks 2–7)

The stakeholder list Carolyn Abel has provided runs to approximately 150 individuals across 12 or more groupings. The scale of this is significant. We will not try to speak to everyone individually; the proposal below sets out how we will tier the consultation to get the right depth in the right places without burning the budget on process.

**Individual interviews — approximately 25 people**

These are the conversations where depth matters more than breadth: the Southampton Forward trustees (individually, not as a group, because the conflicts of interest make group sessions unproductive); the founding partner leads (Laura Read, Michael Ockwell, the university representatives, Tim Keeping and Stephen Manion at GO! Southampton, Peter Taylor at Paris Smith); the ACE relationship managers (Ginny Graham, Phil Gibby, and Stuart McLeod individually, since ACE's view of what happens next may be the single most consequential external factor); Claire Whitaker as outgoing CEO, if she is willing; and the interim CEO Claire Geveaux.

On the political side: the leaders of all five council groups (Labour, Conservative, Reform UK, Green, Lib Dem). The Reform UK conversation will not be comfortable. It is necessary.

**Group sessions — approximately 8 sessions**

Working through the groupings Carolyn has already mapped: the Pulse network (cultural sector leaders, chaired by Michael Ockwell); the Festivals and Events group; the Heritage sector; Cultural Connectors; council officers (Growth & Prosperity directorate and Enabling Services); the sub-regional partners group (Portsmouth Creates, Hampshire Cultural Trust, Island Collective and the local authority officers Carolyn has already been speaking to); and GO! Southampton alongside the Destination Management Partnership (even though tourism is out of scope, the DMP's relationship to any new model needs to be understood).

Charles Freeman will lead or co-facilitate the creative industries and heritage sessions, where his network relationships are most directly relevant.

**Survey**

A short structured survey — 10 to 12 questions — deployed to the wider stakeholder population and to artists. This gives us something more than anecdote when presenting findings, and allows people who will not be in group sessions to contribute.

**What we will not do:** conduct a retrospective audit of the legacy programme's spending or governance decisions. The review is forward-looking. We will surface what did not work in order to design against it, not to apportion blame.

### Strand 4 — Options development and financial modelling (weeks 5–9)

We will develop a minimum of four organisational scenarios. These will span the full realistic range and will not exclude the option of not continuing Southampton Forward in any form. Each scenario will be tested against the same set of criteria:

- Financial viability, including transition costs, reserve rebuild requirements and VAT position
- Political resilience under current NOC conditions (drawing on the Brighton, Bristol and Edinburgh evidence — the economic argument, not political goodwill, is what has protected cultural investment under NOC)
- Governance clarity, specifically addressing the conflicts of interest that weakened the previous model
- Capacity to deliver the development function the city actually needs (not the one the original brief described)
- Sub-regional fit: how does the model sit alongside Hampshire Cultural Trust, Portsmouth Creates and Island Collective without either duplicating or subordinating them?
- Relationship to the HSCCA: can the model credibly represent Southampton's cultural voice at the combined authority table from 2026, and position the city well when investment decisions become available from 2028?

Jon Bains will bring analytical capacity to the financial and economic modelling, and will help construct the economic case for cultural investment in Southampton specifically — the argument that will need to be made and remade in each council budget cycle under NOC conditions.

### Strand 5 — Playbook and report (weeks 8–12)

The final report will have two parts.

The first is the organisational options analysis: a clear account of the evidence, what we found in consultation, and a recommended model with the reasoning behind it. This will be written for a non-specialist reader and structured so that the council, the founding partners and ACE can all use it.

The second is the direction-of-travel playbook: a working framework for vision, mission and purpose that reflects 2026 realities, not 2010 or 2023 ones. This is not a strategy document in full — that is the job of whoever leads the new or reconstituted entity. It is the starting framework that makes it possible to recruit the right person, approach the right funders, and convene the right partners without having to start from scratch.

We will share a draft with Carolyn Abel and the board chair at week 10, present findings to the council, board and founding partners in week 11, and deliver the final report by 4 September 2026.

## A note on independence and approach

We are aware there is significant frustration in parts of the sector about the legacy period, and that some founding partners feel the programme did not deliver what they expected. We will not ignore this. We will not, however, conduct an inquest. The review's job is to understand what did not work clearly enough that the next model is designed against it, not to arbitrate who was responsible.

We are also aware of the structural tension Carolyn has named directly: founding partners who are simultaneously funders, trustees and decision-makers about the organisation's future are in an impossible position. Any model we recommend will need to resolve that tension, not work around it.

We will follow the evidence. We will be willing to say, if the evidence points that way, that Southampton Forward in its current form should not continue. We will also be willing to say the case for a reconstituted body is strong. What we will not do is fit the evidence to a preferred answer.

We know Southampton and we know this landscape. We are ready to begin within a week of appointment.
