Introduction
1.1 Context
Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park and its surrounding neighbourhoods, including
Stratford, Hackney Wick and Fish Island, are entering a period of
unprecedented growth and change, creating a new model for what a truly
mixed-use new urban district can mean in the 21st Century.
The immediate post-Games period saw development of an events and visitor
strategy to establish the Park as an amenity for local communities and a
destination for the population of London.
Development of commercial space during that period – both the repurposing of
the International Broadcast Centre as a home for Here East and Loughborough
University London, and the development of IQL – mean that the Park and Its
surrounding areas are already attracting a mix of global institutions and sociallyconscious employers, alongside innovative startups and media brands such as
BT Sport.
In parallel, the repurposing of the Athletes’ Village as East Village, offering new
models for rental to make it more accessible to those entering the housing
market, has been accompanied by development of new neighbourhoods
offering high-quality housing offers for rental and sale, including both ‘affordable’
and luxury homes.
The next phase of development is a new cultural and education district (CED),
East Bank, that will bring academic staff, researchers, cultural workers, some
10,000 students and hundreds of thousands of visitors each year to the Park.
East Bank will be spread across three sites – UCL East (University College
London’s new campus), Stratford Waterfront (including BBC’s new music
studios, Sadler’s Wells, University of the Arts London’s relocation of the six
London College of Fashion sites into a single new building and the V&A,
including a partnership with the Smithsonian Institution) and Here East (the
V&A's new Collection and Research Centre, and an existing space for UCL).’

–

Curation of a world-class cultural programme for Londoners and for
visitors from across the globe (arts & culture)

–

Creation of unrivalled educational opportunities for Londoners of all
backgrounds (education & opportunity)

–

Stimulation of job growth and the spin-off of new enterprises
(employment & enterprise)

–

Research and innovation that serves London, its citizens and the British
economy (research & innovation)

A new place narrative is needed to take into account existing and future strands
of employment, housing, education, retail, sports, heritage, cultural production
and consumption, alongside the visitor economy and leisure offer of The Park.
1. The Park will increasingly be a place where people live and work. The place
narrative needs to articulate the relationship between these communities and
existing and developing residential neighbourhoods in the vision for the Park,
whilst signalling opportunities for further investment.
2. The place narrative will be one element of a brand strategy for the Park that
provides a framework for its use in a range of different contexts, in
partnership with its diverse stakeholders and investors.
3. The development of East Bank will complement existing workspace and
innovation provision at Here East and IQL. As the focus of London’s creative
industries moves eastward, and into the Thames Estuary, the Park will
become a pivotal location in the production and consumption of culture,
4. The CED, and the combination of international cultural institutions within it,
will also catalyse the opportunity for the Park’s role as a destination for
visitors from London, the UK and overseas.

1.2 Approach
This document brings together evidence from a consultation exercise carried
out between May and July of 2018 consisting of:
–

Two workshops with the project working group on 29 May and 26 June
2018, looking to explore shared understanding of place messaging,
shared audiences, key markets and opportunities.

–

A review of key markets for Live, Work, Study, Play and Culture.

–

Comprehensive document review of materials provided by LLDC and
partners and collation of related external publicly available documents.

–

A review of external policy and strategy documents

–

An international benchmarking exercise carried out reviewing projects
around the globe for an analysis of Place Narrative based on six areas:
Open Spaces, Historic Preservation, Public Art, Street Life,
Programming (events and community engagement) and Architecture

–

A set of interviews with a diverse group of stakeholders carried out in
person at LLDC's offices, in their own premises and over the telephone.

The messages and proof points evidenced in this document will change over
the next five years. The perception of clients, business occupants, residents,
students, workers and visitors to the Park and surrounding areas will evolve as
new offers come on stream. Even in the brief period of our consultation, four of
the partners have either rebranded or begun marketing new offers. So, this
evidence review is just that – a snapshot of the current context.
Something more is needed to cohere these messages into a place narrative.
We have used a mix of methods, recombined from examples of successful
placemaking, branding and place marketing approaches across the globe to
create a flexible framework that links themes and key markets. Along with our
review of evidence provided by partners, stakeholders, interviewees and other
third party sources, we believe that these form a toolkit that can be used by
existing and new partners to evolve the place narrative and messaging over the
next three to five years. The sources of those methods, our use of them and the
derivation of the place narrative will be drawn out in more detail in an executive
summary that complements this evidence review.

The contents of this evidence review have in turn been used to:
–

Locate the Park within London and its surrounding area

–

Examine how the Park itself is currently referenced – or not - by current
stakeholders

–

Assess both opportunities and challenges presented by both current
and future development

–

Identify key themes that help link up partner messages

–

Establish ‘proof points’ to ensure that any narrative is at once authentic,
rooted in reality and aspirational

–

Analyse key markets

2.4 Play
The scale of the Park (560 acres) and the diversity of its offer and that of its
surrounding areas make it, “a park like no other”. Distinctive attractions – such
as the ArcelorMittal Orbit – within reach of a huge catchment of day visitors
across London and the South East.
There is still a strong perception of the sporting opportunities within the Park –
both watching and participation. This is part of the Olympic legacy and the
continuing daily use of the venues within the Park. Increasing use of London
Stadium for major concerts reinforces the identity of the Park as a destination.
Visitors to the Park also report very good perceptions of community events as
well as sporting and cultural events.
Source: Arkenford

The leisure offer ranges from the existing, independent spirit of the canalside
cafes and studios of Hackney Wick, to the mainstream retail and dining offers
within Westfield Stratford City, to the cutting edge, upmarket developments by
Manhattan Loft Company and HH Living. This very much builds on the sense
that this is a creative and unconventional place, identifying a sense of adventure
for an audience of young adults (25-40) who are attracted to individual
experiences and a sense of doing things differently.

2.5 Culture

Number of attractions visited - Nine out of ten cultural tourists visited at least
one attraction while in London. Overseas and younger visitors went to the most
attractions.
Popular attractions - West End shows hold particular appeal for domestic
tourists and older visitors, while London’s historic attractions are most sought
after by overseas tourists.
Booking trends - Almost a third of visitors surveyed had not purchased any
attraction tickets during their visit, although UK visitors are more likely to
purchase tickets than those visiting London from overseas.

Reasons to visit
The predominant reason for leisure visits to London is the city’s cultural
experience. Its considerable offering of museums, galleries, exhibitions and
attractions represented a significant draw for 39% of leisure visitors surveyed.
This is more than double that of the second most popular driver, London's
architecture and iconic landmarks, which were attractive to almost a fifth of
leisure visitors.

Since the financial crisis, tourism has been the third
largest source of jobs and growth for the capital.

Analysis of current London visitor numbers provides some insight into the
importance of culture as a driver for the visitor economy on and around the
Park, with nine out of ten leisure tourists visiting at least one cultural
attraction during their visit to London.

5.1 London Plan
The London Plan is the key document that determines priorities for the future
sustainable development and growth of London. In its current form it reflects a
core aspiration to rebalance opportunity and outcomes for citizens across the
city – in particular, emphasising both the need to reduce the disparity in income
and life expectancy between West and East London and identifying the capacity
for growth of housing and employment opportunities in both inner and outer
East London.
In that context, the pivotal importance of LLDC’s strategic role, the development
of Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park and the regeneration of neighbouring
communities becomes clear.

Live
Housing development is a fundamental component of the London plan which
sets ambitious targets for the next ten years of 21,610 new units from LLDC,
13,300 from Hackney and 35,110 from Tower Hamlets between now and
2028/9. According to the Plan, to meet these goals, the average amount of
housing delivered in the capital will need to double during this period. ‘The
Mayor recognises that development of this scale will require not just an increase
in the number of homes approved but also a fundamental transformation in how
new homes are delivered.’
Attention to architectural innovation and environmental sustainability and
adherence to ‘good growth’ principles (see sidebar) should be a thread across
all new development.
Affordable housing in particular is mandated in the Plan (Policy H5), with a
strategic target of half of all new homes brought online classified as affordable
(for context ‘the London SHMA has identified that 65 per cent of London’s need
is for affordable housing’). Categories of affordable housing identified include
low cost rented homes (social rent / London affordable rent) and intermediate
products (London Living Rent and London Shared ownership).
Meeting this target is ‘critical to enabling London to meet the housing needs of
its workforce and maintain the function and resilience of the city’. The Plan also
makes a point of noting that ‘the delivery of overall housing targets should not
be relied on as a reason for reducing affordable housing delivery or other policy
requirements’.

Good Growth by Design
Mayor Sadiq Khan has introduced the Good Growth by Design
initiative to ensure that as London enters a period of unprecedented
new building and infrastructure development that it is equitable and
inclusive, maintains local character, and enhances rather than
undermines community cohesion.
The £70 million Good Growth Fund, which will run between 2017 and
2021, will support activity that unites the public and private sector.
This includes a new social enterprise, Public Practice, which will
embed designers and planners in local councils, and the appointment
of 50 ‘design advocates’ who will assess challenges and set
standards for London’s built environment.
‘Good Growth by Design is a call to everyone engaged in London’s

Londoners. We know that good architecture and planning can make a
real difference between the places that work – socially and
environmentally as well as economically – and those that age
gracelessly, as expensive eyesores and failed urban districts.’

Opportunity Areas
The London Plan identifies a set of Opportunity Areas (OAs) which are large

8.1 Thematic findings from interviews
Terminology
There is a lack of consistency in the terms and signifiers used by both
stakeholders and in the media—which in turn leads to an inconsistent narrative
in the wider public. Across the materials presented in this document you will find
over a dozen different ways that the Park, its location, and districts wherein are
referred to. These include Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, Olympic Bark, E20,
East London, East Village, Stratford, Stratford Waterfront, East Bank,
Canalside, Stratford International, Former Athlete’s Village, and others.

Legacy
While there is generally high use of and positive association of the Park with the
legacy of the Olympic Games and its role as both a milestone and catalyst for
regeneration, for some stakeholders the Olympic message is starting to fade
and lose some its former relevance, particularly with new and younger
audiences.

Heritage
While the association with Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park and Stratford is one
of new-ness, both Stratford and the Park have a rich heritage and history of
industry, connectivity, and creativity which should continue to be researched
and integrated in the story of the area. From an industrial perspective, much of
the history of London can be wrapped up in the Park (although some of its uses
have been less appealing, i.e. the tanning and chemicals production).

Modernity:
As new projects come on stream and the Park becomes more populated – with
new residents, businesses and a wider cross-section of visitors, it is starting to
establish itself as a modern, buzzing and cool destination, particularly
benefitting from its proximity to Hackney Wick and certainly to be enhanced by
the addition of over 5,000 fashion and design students from around the world
with the upcoming move of London College of Fashion to Queen Elizabeth
Olympic Park (further detailed in Section 5: Study, above).

A new creative and innovation hub

Affordability

That it is quite literally more than the sum of its parts is a key USP of Queen
Elizabeth Olympic Park, and one that hasn’t been communicated or leveraged
to its full advantage. For businesses (particularly those operating in the tech and
innovation space), academic and cultural institutions in development, and new
residents, the ability to be part of something exciting, new, and larger than any
one project or initiative is a key consideration in their decision to locate within
the park, particularly between now and 2023 as Here East, IQL, East Bank, the
Fashion Quarter and others continue to develop, the Park has the potential to
evolve into London’s most dynamic hub for cross-disciplinary collaboration.

The Park is generally seen as an area of London that provides good value for
money, in terms of both rates and rents. The provides an opportunity to attract
companies across all sectors who might anticipate being priced out of more
central areas of the city due to major new developments like Crossrail.

Navigation and connectivity
This presents both an opportunity and a challenge for Queen Elizabeth Olympic
Park. The Park is incredibly well connected, not only through Stratford’s multimodal transport interchange but also with both with the new Hackney Wick
Overground station and Stratford International, which offers a frequent service
with a transit time of 7 minutes to King’s Cross St. Pancras.
It’s clear, then, that this is one of London’s most well-connected transit hubs; but
the perception of its distance from ‘central’ London, and uncertainty about the
best way in which to reference it within the context of East London, continue to
undermine attempts to establish a clear, shared message about the location of
the Park and its surrounding areas.
In addition, the Park itself is still viewed by many as inaccessible. Wayfinding
both to and within the Park can be illegible and confusing, particularly to first
time visitors. And it’s a big site: Here East has had to set up a private bus
service to address the distance to the closest accessible transport links.

Community engagement
Community engagement and wellbeing sits at the heart of LLDC’s mission and
is reflected in both the aims and messaging of stakeholders in and around the
Park. For both current and upcoming projects, the aspiration to be inclusive and
sustainable and to promote wider health and wellbeing is clearly reflected.

Overcoming and reshaping existing perceptions
Both Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park and Stratford grapple with a lukewarm
public and media narrative, but with the success of projects like King’s Cross
people are becoming more open to what transformative regeneration projects
look and feel like when experienced in person.

For people who aren’t necessarily familiar with London,
Here East or Stratford, [the tie to the Olympics] is a
prominent story people understand.
For the Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park story the
Olympic connection is bedrock stuff and the clue is in
the name, no matter what the Park morphs into.

Keeping pace with new development
Even for active stakeholders of the Park, keeping on top of all the new
development activity and timescales for each poses a challenge.

The development of a wider destination offer [is] taking
place. It’s better if we all work together on the message
we want to send out.

8.2 Interview findings: key markets
Live
While ‘Live’ was one of the areas least discussed and noted in stakeholder
interviews, the impact that changes in the residential landscape of the Park and
surrounding area will have on its demographic and feel will be significant.
One interviewee noted that while there will be an influx of students with new
educational campuses opening over the coming years, many of them won’t be
able to afford to live in or near the Park.

Work
The businesses within the Park view proximity and opportunity to engage with
the new academic campuses coming into the area as a key USP, and in some
cases, a factor in their decision to locate within the Park. They see the potential
this brings to drive innovation within their companies and to have a new pool of
talent to draw from. For example, Ford articulated the potential to collaborate
with Loughborough University London around research and engineering. This
also extends to the benefit these companies view from co-locating with other
companies across sectors within their individual projects. There was some
(though less) reference to the potential to collaborate more widely with
institutions across the Park and surrounding areas.
Attracting and retaining talent is a big issue for tenants and the opportunities of
being able to recruit directly from several universities in close proximity is a
significant factor for their relocation here. One interviewee asserted that most
businesses on the Park will benefit more from the tertiary education and
knowledge economy offer than from the consumer experience.
Here East and IQL are seeking to strategically curate an interesting mix of
sectors and businesses of different scales and maturity, to ensure that they are
appealing both to prospective and existing tenants.
