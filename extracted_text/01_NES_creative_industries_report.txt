Preface: Two studies, twelve years apart
In the winter of 2012-13, Francois Matarasso travelled across Aberdeenshire speaking to artists, makers, musicians, community organisers and cultural practitioners. He published his findings in 2014 as The Pinning Stones: Culture and Community in Aberdeenshire. It is a precise and honest assessment of a regional creative culture. Its central argument was structural rather than celebratory: that Aberdeenshire's cultural life was rich, dispersed, self-reliant, largely invisible to policy, and, like the small stones set between the larger granite blocks of a traditional rubble wall, essential to holding everything together. Remove the pinning stones, he wrote, and the mortar cracks faster. The wall looks fine until it doesn't.
Twelve years later, this research arrives at a different place by a different route and finds, in several important respects, the same thing. And something more.
The methodology is entirely different. Where Matarasso walked and talked, this analysis counted: LinkedIn profiles, company registrations, four tiers of workforce modelling, gross value added calculations, skills comparisons and place-based counterfactuals. Where Matarasso offered a humanist portrait, this report offers an economic audit. The core finding about scale and visibility is structurally identical to his: the region holds a creative economy that is significantly larger, more economically significant, and more strategically important than any official dataset records, and it is hidden in plain view.
But this research finds something Matarasso's lens could not have seen in 2014: a separate convergence opportunity. Technical firms with transferable creative-digital capability and creative firms with energy-sector client relationships operate alongside one another, but often unaware of each other. A strategic focus on turning that coexistence into effective cooperation could unlock opportunities to grow the region's creative economy in ways that reinforce a just transition from dependence on fossil fuels. The report puts forward ways to market test and exploit those nascent synergies.
The report casts new light on these opportunities, as well as those presented by the city's creative core and the distinctive strengths of cultural life in the region's urban and coastal districts. It seeks to close the visibility gap that Matarasso first documented; and in doing so, it looks to demonstrate how the creative economy can be identified as a critical success factor in both Regional Economic Strategy and the Local Outcomes Improvement Plan.

Part one: What this document is and who it is for
1.1 Scope and purpose
This document presents the findings of a comprehensive assessment of the creative economy of Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire. It is addressed at once to Regional Economic Partnership members and local authority decision-makers; to practitioners and organisations working across the creative, cultural and digital sectors; and to any reader with an interest in how North East Scotland positions itself for sustained economic growth during a period of rapid structural transition.
It draws on original analysis combining official government data, real-time company intelligence and workforce profiling with stakeholder consultation and comparative research. It makes the case for strategic investment grounded in evidence, identifies specific funding opportunities that are available now, and charts the growth pathways available at each level of a creative economy that is larger, more productive and more strategically distinctive than any previous account has recognised.
1.2 Articulating the region's creative economy
North East Scotland contains three geographically dispersed and commercially disconnected creative communities:
	•	City-centre creative, marketing and content production agencies hold narrative capability, client management experience and commissioning relationships.
	•	Rural and coastal creative practitioners operate across Aberdeenshire and the north-east coast, often outside registered business structures.
	•	Technical firms with transferable creative-digital capability sit across the four convergence geographies identified in Part Four: city centre; a corridor running from Westhill to Dyce; south Aberdeen and Stonehaven; and Inverurie and Kintore.
Each community has capability that matters to the region's future creative economy. The evidence shows that the base creative economy is larger than official datasets record: 10,490 workers, 7,715 FTE, £453 million in direct GVA and £675 million in total economic impact. It does not yet prove that these communities are already selling into the same markets, or that demand for new forms of creative experience is large enough to support a new growth programme; it identifies potential growth that requires market testing.
1.3 Strategic significance of creative industries in North East Scotland
The evidence gathered here points to the distinctive role that the creative economy of North East Scotland can play in sustaining communities through the energy transition, economically, socially, and culturally, in ways that no other region in the United Kingdom is positioned to deliver.
The significance of the creative economy exists across four dimensions:
	•	The region has distinctive strengths: it does not need to compete with the Central Belt or London in conventional creative industries terms. What the region can offer, and what no other UK city or region can match, is the combination of industrial-scale simulation capability, authentic energy domain knowledge, and the rural-coastal cultural identity that makes technical storytelling about the energy transition credible to international clients and communities.
	•	Recognising the gross value added contribution made by creative industries and how to increase that is significant: but if the question is about sustaining communities through transition, strategy for the sector needs to recognise that the Local Outcomes Improvement Plan outcome domains are equally important.
	•	The evidence contradicts a false binary choice between investment in digital sectors and creative industries, and argues that the right approach is a sequenced strategy that addresses different populations at different timescales.
	•	Addressing those combined opportunities calls for a change to current institutional governance: no existing body is currently configured to provide solutions that respond to the evidence presented in this research, and the answer is a new mandate rather than a new agency.
How this document is organised

Part two: Scale and significance of the creative industries in North East Scotland
2.1 What this analysis measures, and how
National statistics are designed to count employment within registered companies. The structural and methodological limitations of applying those measures to analysis of the creative industries are well known and documented. In particular, national statistics typically fail to count creative capability that exists outside registered creative sector employers, or technical capability within non-creative sector firms that has a creative origin and a creative application. In a regional economy structured like that of North East Scotland, both of these omissions are significant.
This research applies a methodology that works across three distinct layers of evidence: official employment and company registration data, real-time firmographic classification derived from company website analysis, and LinkedIn workforce profiling treated as a large-scale occupational survey. These are reconciled through a sequence of analytical steps designed to avoid both double-counting and systematic exclusion. The result is a more complete structural picture than official statistics alone can provide, identifying firms and workers not captured by previous analyses. The differences in scale and composition that follow reflect what becomes visible when the frame of analysis is widened to match the actual shape of the economy being studied. It also reflects Throsby and Bakhshi's recommendations that analysis should reflect three different but complementary types of value: economic value (gross value added, employment, exports); non-market cultural value (cultural goods' intrinsic and social worth); and symbolic and heritage value (longer-run identity and heritage impacts).
2.2 Official baseline
Standard approaches to measuring creative industries rely on two official data sources: the Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES), which counts formally employed workers in registered businesses; and the Inter-Departmental Business Register (IDBR), which records companies by their declared Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code. Both systematically undercount the creative economy in Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire.
Employees
BRES data for Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire, filtered against creative industries SIC codes and adjusted to remove general IT consultancy, produces a count of 4,965 full and part-time employees.
Of that total, the IT consultancy sub-sector (SIC 62.02) accounts for between 1,400 and 1,600 full and part-time jobs and an estimated £150 million in gross value added. Detailed analysis of sector intelligence and stakeholder consultation confirm that this activity functions primarily as enterprise support for the whole economy, in particular for the energy sector. This part of the workforce is already recognised in Opportunity North East's mapping of the digital sector in North East Scotland; including them in our assessment of creative industries would inflate the creative economy data while misrepresenting its character. As the comparison in Table 1 shows, it has also been a volatile employment sector in recent years; for those reasons, and to avoid double counting, we exclude this section of the workforce from the resulting total of around 3,300 employees who form the basis of our assessment of the strategic creative core (see Part Three, below).

2.3 Real-time data sources
Real-time workforce data
BRES data forms the floor of the workforce analysis: the population whose economic activity is formally registered, reliably counted and analytically secure. It captures the region's publicly funded cultural institutions, its registered design and architecture practices, and its other commercial creative firms; but it misses the large population of self-employed and freelance practitioners who are neither employed by a firm nor registered as an employer. In a sector where over 90% of registered creative enterprises employ fewer than ten people, and where many practitioners operate through portfolio working, part-time activity and informal contracts, this is a methodological shortcoming rather than a marginal omission.
We address this through analysis of LinkedIn profiles, treating the platform as a large pre-filled workforce survey rather than a social media tool, to identify more than 8,000 unique profiles of residents of North East Scotland working in creative industries, with a further 4,000 IT services and IT consultancy occupations. Excluding the IT services and consultancy component, comparing LinkedIn data with BRES provides evidence of more than 1,500 additional employees and 3,280 self-employed and freelance workers not captured in official employment statistics.

Real-time company data
IDBR measurement of business populations suffers from similar limitations to those of BRES. We used The Data City Industry Engine to validate data from IDBR, and to distinguish active regional operations from holding companies, dormant registrations, and businesses registered in the region but operating primarily elsewhere.
The Data City platform analyses company website content and other proprietary data sources to assign Real-Time Industrial Classifications to companies: descriptions of what firms actually do rather than how they are officially classified. This reveals a differently configured population of active creative businesses from that shown in official statistics, particularly in the Createch sub-sector (creative technology: firms applying creative methods to digital and technological products and services). All company identifications were subject to human validation to confirm operational presence in the region and to exclude subsidiaries with no substantive local operation.
Analysis of the Industry Engine provided evidence of over 1,700 companies registered under at least one of the DCMS creative industries SIC codes with either a registered office or commercial activity undertaken in North East Scotland; further analysis and validation using The Data City's Real-Time Industry Classifications and manual validation of the company's activities reduced this to a list of around 1,200 active companies, from which a further 280 companies were excluded as being primarily computer consultancy businesses. A detailed description of the methodology used can be found at Appendix B.
Full data tables and classification methodology supporting this analysis are set out in Appendices A and B.
