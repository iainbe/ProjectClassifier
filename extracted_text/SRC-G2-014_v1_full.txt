 Executive Summary  
Background 
Liverpool City Region Growth Platform (‘the Growth Platform’) commissioned 
this study from BOP Consulting, working with City Curator as data partners, in 
November 2019. The purpose of the research was to:  
 Reduce reliance on national reports to provide the narrative for 
investment marketing  
 Improve on the existing evidence base of companies and employment 
in Liverpool City Region (LCR) 
 Identify market niches or ‘smart’ specialisms in immersive tech, content, 
and markets in LCR  
During the course of the study, the covid-19 emergency imposed an urgent and 
essential additional stage of the work: to identify a plan for the recovery and re-
establishment of the immersive sector and ecosystem. This is set out as the first 
stage of the implementation strategy set out below. 
Market opportunity 
Comparing industry and investment reports into the potential growth of 
immersive technologies and content suggests a global market size of 
(conservatively) $100bn by 2022. 
Some trends emerge from our analysis of these secondary sources: 
 Augmented Reality (AR) will dominate; as well as being more portable 
and having a lighter technology overhead, which allows it to be 
integrated within more consumer devices, the rollout of 5G will also 
drive the greater availability of mobile AR applications and services 
 Games is already, and will remain, by far the largest immersive creative 
content sub-sector, representing one-third of market revenues  China will lead the market; but lacks experience in producing 
international standard content companies. For example, using awards 
for new content to benchmark international markets, companies based 
in the US won 54% of awards. 8% of productions awarded since 2017 
were (co)produced by companies based in the UK. 
 Players in Liverpool City Region have big ambitions: 
“We want to be the ARM of Spatial Audio” 
The research also identifies barriers to growth 
 Demand: although some reports suggest that growth will be faster in 
other industrial sectors (including health, manufacturing and education) 
as well as in other entertainment applications, these are at a much 
earlier stage of maturity in their use of immersive tech than the games 
market, and therefore projections are less certain 
 Supply: The supply chain for immersive content remains fragmented on 
a number of levels: absence of consolidation in consumer hardware and 
exhibition leading to segmented distribution; skills shortages and 
competition with other industry sectors in key areas of content and 
technology; lack of integration with existing use-cases beyond games 
 Regulatory environment: Greater awareness of personal data 
protection, and consumer concerns around safety of 5G, may inhibit 
take up of hardware and content 
 Technology: Market is lacking a “hero device” AR headset

suggest it can develop as a sustainable, high productivity sector. There are 114 
LCR-based companies, of whom some 70% are based in Liverpool, plus a

The ecosystem contains companies of varying sizes and degrees of

both in employment and turnover, than the average for digital and creative 
industries; more profitable; and growing strongly. LCR is home to some of the

Irrespective of the sector from which the companies are drawn, LCR’s 
immersive ecosystem operates across four main supply chains: 
 Experience: creation of content (original IP or commissioned work) for 
live events, digitally distributed entertainment, exhibitions, or 
installations 
 Applications: content and platforms in AR/VR for social media, 
marketing, education, health, and other industrial applications, whether 
in the form of original IP or ‘work for hire’ 
 Originals: creating original IP that combines an immersive application 
and a physical product 
 Tech: R&D-focused businesses looking to develop IP in technology and 
tools for the creation, authoring, editing, processing and distribution of 
immersive content, products, and hardware  
Assets 
As well as commercial businesses, LCR’s immersive ecosystem contains a 
significant number of other organisations who make a significant contribution 
through either commissioning of content, collaborative R&D, education and 
skills development or provision of facilities and venues. These are drawn from a 
range of sectors including culture, education and skills, health, digital 
infrastructure, research and business support, and retail. 
Cultural organisations such as National Museums Liverpool, whose 2019 
Terracotta Warriors  exhibition made extensive use of AR content commissioned 
from Draw & Code, and delivered by Adlib, play an important role in 
commissioning new content and stimulating the development of new 
technologies and applications for display and curation of museums and 
galleries. Culture Liverpool, through its stewardship of St George’s Hall, acts as

resource for teaching, experimentation, and collaborative R&D.

Investment and business support 
Surveys and interviews consistently identify the shortage of ‘strategic investors’ 
– individuals and institutions with experience and specialist knowledge of the 
sector, willing to provide ‘patient capital’ – as a constraint on growth :  We need patient investors who can see the potential - 
this is a core technology - with various vertical market 
opportunities 
There is also frustration at the lack of specialist business support and 
proliferation of unconnected schemes through which businesses can access 
finance for research and growth. For small research-led organisations, 
‘business support is a necessary evil’, taking time and resource away from core 
development activity and forcing them back on their own connections and 
‘sweat equity’: 
 In the absence of financial backing - time and 
determination are a founder’s greatest assets 
Challenges 
Survey findings make it clear that there are challenges around competition for 
experienced immersive talent, both  between XR companies in LCR and with 
end-user businesses in other sectors (who may be able to pay better wages and 
offer better conditions of employment than start-ups and SMEs with limited 
capital resources.)  
Whilst Liverpool is growing fast, Manchester is already operating at scale, 
presenting an additional source of competition for the same talent pool. Slightly 
further away, but still within a 90-minute commute, Yorkshire is putting 
significant investment into development of its XR and screen industries. 
In this context, the lack of a ‘tech voice’ for the city region – in the way that Phil 
Redmond has been seen as a figurehead for film and television, for example – 
is seen as a significant gap. LCR has initiated a successful, sector-led music 
board; Salford has created the post of ‘Chief Digital Officer’ to draw attention to 
its ambition for MediaCityUK. LCR should explore whether one or both routes 
could help secure additional investment.

 Benchmarking 
Although not as large as the cluster in Greater Manchester, less mature than 
the community of content and R&D led businesses in Bristol and Bath and 
lacking the levels of public, private and inward investment that have stoked 
development in Newcastle Gateshead, Liverpool City Region is comparable to 
all these benchmark cities and has similar size of workforce and percentage 
penetrations when compared against the wider creative community. It has 
strengths in software development, built upon the legacy of its games sector, 
and a reputation as a hotbed of cultural and creative talent; although the 
opportunities to commercialise that capability are limited by the absence of a 
significant national media commissioning body in the city region. 
The immersive sector recognises the value of Liverpool’s cultural legacy and 
global reputation in framing opportunities for growth: 
 Entertainment is a big part of the Liverpool City Region 
story (football, music, film) and it would be great if the 
creative tech sector were recognised as a worthy area 
to get behind. It not only supports the entertainment 
sector; it also taps into all aspects of our lives - and 
offers a wide range of inspiring job opportunities. 
Conclusions, priorities, and recommendations 
Our interim findings suggest that Liverpool City Region has a fast-growing 
immersive sector. It has a strong meetup ecosystem, and strengths in IT and 
software, particularly in comparison to UK competitors such as Bristol, 
Manchester, and Newcastle, although the perception that it is strongest in 
creative talent is possibly overstated. Lack of strategic investors is a weakness; 
and there are clear opportunities for greater coordination of public sector 
support for skills, business, and investor development - and to align its 
objectives to Local Industrial Strategy and Cultural Strategies.  LCR’s immersive sector has grown ‘organically’ with limited sector support, few 
strategic investors, and no big conference to draw attention to itself– but other 
city regions are more coordinated in their response: 
‒ Bristol has a cohesive and well-established immersive sector, built on a 
legacy of collaborative R&D experimentation at Pervasive Media Lab 
and access to BBC commissioners, with the added potential of new 
digital commissioning from Channel 4 
‒ Manchester has invested in diverse production assets (MediaCityUK, 
Sharp/Space Projects) and has a coordinated angel investor forum (GC 
Angels) focused on immersive  
‒ Newcastle Gateshead has hosted the Thinking Digital conference for a 
decade, and secured AHRC funding for the ‘North East FUSE’ business 
support project to link the region’s digital and creative sector to 
opportunities for collaborative R&D inputs from its universities. Including 
a  new University IT/Cloud centre  
‒ Leeds City Region has forged strong strategic links between screen and 
XR sectors, catalysed by AHRC investment in XR-focused collaborative 
R&D facilities in Leeds and York 
All stages of engagement with the immersive community identified a shared 
vision around collective and collaborative development, with a strong sense of 
‘place’ and Liverpool’s unique combination of local identity and global reputation 
at its core. LCR’s immersive tech ecosystem has potential to be a positive 
narrative for innovation in the city region and even further afield: 
 If all components of the immersive ecosystem across 
LCR were packaged as a single front end, it could offer 
LCR a soft power - cultural and political capital.

 1. Introduction 
1.1 Background to the brief 
Liverpool City Region Growth Platform (‘the Growth Platform’) commissioned 
this study from BOP Consulting, working with City Curator as data partners, in 
November 2019. The purpose of the research was to:  
 Reduce reliance on national reports to provide the narrative for 
investment marketing  
 Improve on the existing evidence base of companies and employment 
in Liverpool City Region (LCR) 
 Identify market niches or ‘smart’ specialisms in immersive tech, content, 
and markets in LCR  
The impact was envisaged to extend to supporting future (capital) bids for 
Strategic Investment Fund (SIF) monies from the Liverpool City Region 
Combined Authority (LCRCA) and clarifying the need to make provision of 
specialist information, advice and guidance through the Growth Platform.  
The Growth Platform recognised that business cases for new funding would be 
strengthened were the research to identify both current sources of and gaps in 
private equity and venture capital funding available to LCR businesses; and to 
highlight those companies which are ‘growth ready’ and seeking investment.  
The consultants were asked to establish whether the findings suggested options 
for scalable ‘High Performance Opportunities’ around Liverpool’s immersive 
sector, and to make recommendations on that basis. 
During the course of the study, the covid-19 emergency imposed an urgent and 
essential additional stage of the work: to identify a plan for the recovery and re-
establishment of the immersive sector and ecosystem. This is set out as the first 
stage of the implementation strategy set out in Chapter 11, below. 
 1.2 Definitions of immersive technology 
Immersive experiences consist of both a perceptual  (input to the user) and 
interactive  (output from the user) element in order to stimulate one or more 
human senses (sight, sound, touch, taste and smell) to create a ‘realistic’ 
environment for the user that blurs the line between the physical world and the 
simulated or digital world.  
Most immersive content focuses on visual, auditory, or tactile sensations – 
although there are experiments (including in Liverpool) in olfactory solutions.  
1.2.1 Augmented, virtual and mixed reality 
The most commonly used definitions of immersive experiences are: 
 Augmented reality (AR) , which overlays computer generated virtual 
objects (sights, sounds, smells) on the real-world environment 
 Virtual reality (VR) , which immerses users in a fully artificial, digitally 
created environment 
 Mixed reality (MR) , in which both real and computer-generated objects 
are ‘blended’ to create a realistic environment, through which the user 
can navigate and interact with both real and virtual objects 
 Extended reality (XR) , which is an umbrella term used to describe 
immersive technologies that can merge physical and ‘virtual’ worlds 
1.3 Components of immersive technologies 
The development, creation, recording, distribution, and experience of immersive 
reality brings together three different types of technology.  
1.3.1 Interaction 
These technologies provide the user with the ability to interact and 
communicate with the virtual environment. They encompass a range of human-
computer interaction techniques: 

[PAGE 11]
 
8 
  Computer vision 
 Gesture recognition 
 Speech processing 
 Artificial Intelligence (AI) that uses machine learning to measure 
perception in order to replicate and reflect human behaviour – eg, 
Affectiva2, which combines deep learning architectures, computer vision 
and speech processing 
1.3.2 Hardware 
There are many types of hardware that provide a physical interface for the user 
to experience, navigate and control AR and VR experiences, including 
 Head-mounted displays (HMDs) like the Oculus Rift, HTC Vive and 
PlayStation VR, as well as mobile-based headsets like Samsung Gear 
 VR controllers – hand-held haptic devices which incorporate tactile and 
movement sensors 
 360° projection spaces – eg, Igloo domes3, Bristol’s Data Dome (a 
stereo 3D hemispherical screen with 4K resolution)4 
1.3.3 Software 
Developers use a range of software and middleware to manage the creation, 
authoring, editing, rendering and online distribution of immersive content. These 
include Unity VR and Unreal Engine: the most commonly used ‘middleware’ 
technologies, these ‘real time engines’, evolved from games engines, help 
developers manage workflow by combining authoring tools (such as animation, 
lighting, rendering and materials), an editor and a high-optimisation rendering

Immersive industries not only include AR and VR, but also haptics, spatial 
audio, and voice user interfaces (VUI). The haptic technology market is

2.3 Benefits and applications of immersive tech 
Current benefits and business applications of immersive technologies are varied 
and include:  
‒ new markets and applications for gaming and entertainment 
‒ applications in development and training 
‒ accelerated product and service development for businesses 
Further opportunities will unfold with the development of hardware, creation of 
more sophisticated and engaging software, and the rolling out of improved 
connectivity infrastructure (5G).  
Projections anticipate that the games industry will be the one with the largest 
potential (34% of the total revenues projected for 2025), followed by Healthcare 
(15%) and Engineering (15%). 
2.4 Trends 
2.4.1 Trends: AR set to dominate  
Sources reflect a consensus that AR as a technology, development platform 
and content type will dominate the industry. Projected AR revenues for 2022 
and 2023 are between double and nine times those projected for VR. This is 
due to the following factors:  

[PAGE 14]
 
11 
 ‒ AR is more portable and has a lighter technology overhead, and it is 
already integrating into all the main social media platforms after the 
success of AR features in Snapchat 
‒ The rollout of 5G will also drive the greater availability of portable AR 
applications and services 
‒ VR remains expensive and while standalone VR headsets continue to 
be developed, most headsets remain tethered to supporting hardware 
‒ Distribution platforms for AR have already converged to the iOS app 
store and Android’s Google Play Store, whereas VR distribution 
remains segmented and split across different platforms 
‒ Investment trends are shifting away from VR, with money going to 
computer vision and other AR-related technologies  
2.4.2 Trends: Games will be by far the largest 
immersive creative content sector 
The research suggests we may be at an inflection point for software sales, with 
a spike representing an almost tenfold increase in software sales between 2019 
and 2020. The projected increase is attributed to the introduction of new 
hardware—Oculus Quest and Oculus Rift S—as well as the launch of several 
exclusive hit games.  
‒ Software will overtake hardware in share of industry revenues over the 
next three years.

2.5 Barriers to growth 
2.5.1 Barriers: Demand side 
‒ Lack of demand and revenues beyond gaming remain uncertain. This 
means that many developers and producers are waiting for greater 
adoption before entering the market and advertisers are also unwilling 
as yet to invest in creating expensive bespoke immersive content  
‒ VR content is growing particularly slowly, with the distribution of 
immersive live streaming events and entertainment segmented across a 
range of proprietary systems, often tied into high end games consoles 
2.5.2 Barriers: Supply side 
‒ The distribution of live event experiences for VR or AR remains very 
segmented for consumers due to competing platforms 
‒ Lack of development talent for quality immersive experiences overlays 
more general skills shortages in developers more generally 
‒ There is a lack of integration with existing everyday use-cases – but 
increasing the use of immersive experiences as integrated features 
within mainstream apps (e.g. Google Maps) may be integral to driving non-games related use and associated revenues for immersive 
producers 
2.5.3 Barriers: Regulatory 
‒ Greater awareness of the need for better person data protection and 
related regulations: as awareness grows and government regulations 
tighten it will become more difficult to access, use, and port users 
personal-data cross borders and across different legal jurisdictions 
‒ Although the biggest market and global investor, China’s regulatory 
barriers are a problem for international immersive producers wishing to 
access the market 
2.5.4 Barriers: Technology 
‒ The consumer market currently lacks a “hero device” AR headset, 
though hopes are high for Apple to deliver one imminently that will bring 
AR headsets into the mainstream. Apple’s iPhone X and 11 ranges 
already have extensive AR capabilities 
‒ Limited battery power still hampers immersive development as AR and 
VR applications and devices have relatively high-power consumption 
needs.

Although a number of UK cities can claim particular digital assets or 
specialisms in immersive content production, there is at present no centre 
dedicated to immersive technologies outside London in the UK that would 
present a direct comparator or benchmark for economic impact for LCR. 
The benchmarking exercise focused on comparing Curator analysis of 
LinkedIn data on the existing immersive workforce in LCR with competitor 
cities and city regions of Bristol, Manchester, and Newcastle.  
It also looked at the social dimension of ecosystem development, using 
data from Meetup.com on membership and attendance of networking 
events as a proxy for cluster growth and engagement. 
 
Benchmarking and trends analysis took into account scenarios for growth of 
immersive content and tech over the next three to five years. 
BOP and Curator’s sector analysis for Innovate UK has already provided an 
initial analysis of the relative standing of Liverpool City Region’s immersive 
content sector against 22 other UK geographies. It reveals that: 
‒ London enjoys a considerable ‘head start’, as home to nearly 50% of 
the UK’s creative industries-focused immersive content sector and 
several national centres 
‒ Liverpool City Region has an immersive content sector on a par with 
several other core cities, but is smaller than comparator regions such as 
Manchester and Bristol  
In addition to a comparison of the scale of the workforce, the benchmarking 
exercise sought to establish the relative strengths of LCR by other qualitative 
measures, for example, whether the presence of key technology and/or 
research organisations is likely to influence growth within any geography or how the number of immersive businesses correlates to the total population of 
creative businesses and the total business population in each region. 
5.1 Comparator city regions 
5.1.1 Bristol, Manchester, and Newcastle 
The benchmark criteria were based upon selecting cities (or city regions) with 
similar population and a level of promoted immersive activity. The cities were 
also assessed on the basis that they had a comparable creative community, 
with significant representation within media production and games 
development. Consideration was given to the wider sectors within these cities 
that may be influenced by immersive technologies such as architecture and 
construction, manufacturing, and cultural industries. Finally, selection of cities 
was based upon their prevalence within the inward investment market, with 
Manchester and Bristol performing well as a destination both for international 
and UK expansion. Newcastle is less prominent in this market and seen as a 
second-tier destination. 
Liverpool City Region has its own strengths, particularly in software 
development, built upon the legacy of its games sector; whilst the immersive 
sectors in Manchester, Bristol, and Newcastle each have a discernible business 
community and set of activities. They are all represented as hotspots in 
ImmerseUK and InnovateUK’s Immersive economy in UK report. 
The findings bear out the report’s conclusions that Liverpool is comparable to all 
these benchmark city regions and has similar size of workforce and percentage 
penetrations when compared against the wider creative community.  
Greater Manchester is clearly the largest city region on the list in terms of both 
company numbers and workforce; although the data does show that as a 
percentage of the wider creative community Liverpool’s workforce shows a 
greater level of highly skilled workforce.  
In most factors Liverpool comes second to Manchester, and top in some 
technical specialisms; and is overall a close comparator to Bristol. Newcastle is

This chapter summarises findings from interviews conducted between January

6.1 Business support 
Responses to questions about business support coalesced into two strands: 
 Support for a peer-to-peer and collective approach to practical, project- 
based development to encourage organisations (XR producers, 
universities, cultural organisations, and other industry partners) to work 
together to ensure that all parts of the ecosystem ‘evolve together’. This 
is contrasted with an approach that seems to prioritise the ‘scalability’ of 
individual firms at the expense of wider sector development objectives. 
 Concern that lack of specialist knowledge of XR amongst those 
designing and delivering support meant that funded programmes 
tended to look too narrowly at development of immersive as part of 
Digital and Creative Industries, missing opportunities for collaborative 
and cross-sector working across all industry sectors.  
 Interviewees suggested a stepped approach to reconcile these needs: 
 Educating businesses across the economy on the potential of XR (eg 
through seminars and online case studies and ‘white papers’) 
 Directing business support funding into collaborative R&D&I projects 
with end user companies that could potentially draw upon the input of 
diverse specialisms from businesses and research institutions   Using the projects and collaborations that result to help XR content 
producers and technologists build their businesses and strengthen 
supply chains across the Liverpool City Region 
Key chapter insights 
 Consensus around the need to develop partnerships and work 
together to ensure all parts of the ecosystem are effectively using 
existing creative technology in order to  ‘evolve together’  
 Sector development  needs practical support and capacity building 
– including aligning Growth Platform business support and investor 
development offers to the needs of immersive companies 
 Attracting and retaining  experienced and senior talent – both 
software developers and business development – is an immediate 
priority; demand exceeds supply 
 Strategic investment is needed to help organisations develop and 
commercialise new IP  and reduce their dependency both on client 
work and live experiences

Even prior to the covid-19 emergency, the survey and interview findings 
reflected the difficulty that companies face in attracting and retaining 
experienced developers and business development people. Liverpool has a 
more limited pool of skilled digital people than places such as Manchester or 
Leeds; and is in competition with both those cities for available talent. If this is 
not addressed, it will constrain not only the growth of existing companies but 
also the scope to attract new investors.  
Whilst the pipeline of talent from the universities is felt to be a relative strength, 
the present market uncertainties mean that there is a risk that companies will 
not be able to take on and develop graduate talent to the levels required to 
maintain and grow the immersive sector in LCR – threatening both graduate 
retention and long term sector development. 
6.4 Signalling LCR’s ambitions for XR 
There was a shared view that Liverpool lacked the kind of flagship facilities and 
events that had proved effective in attracting investment and talent to competitor 
cities such as Bristol, Manchester, and Newcastle. Interviewees identified three 
ways to address this: 
 An Institute of Creative Technology, convened by universities and 
cultural institutions (eg, FACT) to promote collaborative R&D and joint 
project working, along the lines of Bristol’s Pervasive Media Studio 
 A large-scale immersive entertainment venue, building on Liverpool’s 
legacy in music, sport, and games, to make clear the fit between XR 
and the city region’s cultural, tourism and retail offer 
 Using Sci-Tech Daresbury and its role as a national centre to convene a

LCR has an emerging immersive ecosystem with some characteristics that

 7.3 Strengths 
LCR boasts a small, but fast-growing immersive sector, with some distinctive

In this context, the lack of a ‘tech voice’ for the city region – in the way that Phil 
Redmond has been seen as a figurehead for film and television, for example –

its ambition for MediaCityUK. LCR should explore whether one or both routes 
could help secure additional investment.

R&D and for growth. The existing path to finance imposes time commitments

investment  or ‘masses of paperwork’) and is not responsive to the needs of 
small, development-led organisations.

The final section considers what threats could stall and reverse the growth of 
the immersive sector in Liverpool City Region and its ambitions, including what 
threats the identified weaknesses could expose it to. 
Financial constraints around the ability of private, public, and cultural sector 
bodies to invest in immersive tech and content require that any plan to develop 
and grow the sector brings together substantial funding from multiple 
sources  in order to transform their use. At the moment, much of the available 
funding is made available on a project basis, where the combined intervention 
lacks the scale needed to present a distinctive and high-quality offer from LCR 
that would in turn attract new investment. 
The failure of Liverpool (and the North West region) to attract funding from 
AHRC for an R&D-focused creative industries cluster has accentuated the 
investment gap in XR between LCR and competitors in Yorkshire, West of 
England and South Wales, adding to the historic problem of a concentration in 
public and private sector investment in London and the South East. Public and 
private sectors need to urgently develop shared mechanisms for the advocacy 
of XR activity in LCR that can help attract more investment. 
Whilst cultural organisations are expressing greater interest in developing new 
forms of XR to engage and grow their audiences, a shift given renewed stimulus 
by closure of venues in response to the covid-19 emergency, a key threat lies in  
the lack of capacity to sustain the momentum of increasing partnership 
work once funding – including the short terms stimulus of extended Arts Council 
funding for National Portfolio Organisations - for these and similar programmes 
ends. LCR will always face competition from other cities . Substantial efforts 
will need to be made to convince audiences and investors of the comparative 
merit of LCR’s offer. This means both understanding what audiences and 
investors want  and communicating the offer successfully . Given the 
competitive pressures from other regions outlined above, it is likely that LCR will 
need to demonstrate an intervention at scale if XR is to play a significant role in 
helping Liverpool re-establish, renew, and grow its cultural and visitor economy  There is also a real risk that levels of public funding for culture, already 
expected to decline as changing demographics place additional strains on 
government and local authority services in health and social care, will be further 
undermined by financial retrenchment once the immediate crisis has passed. To 
do so will require actors across the board to recognise the value of joint activity 
and communication, and their ongoing willingness and ability to engage. 
Related to this is the threat of key stakeholders not recognising existing 
opportunities and losing the chance to band-waggon on to those .  
More than these individual threats, however, the biggest threat to the immersive 
supply chain is in not seizing the moment: 
 Not capitalising on the opportunity we currently have - to 
be seen as hotbed of innovation in this space. 
Hesitation and lack of action will mean we miss our 
moment to showcase the region’s talent and other 
clusters will be created elsewhere.

It is not clear at time of writing (April 2020) what the impact of the covid-19 
emergency has been, will continue to be or whether the effects have been felt 
consistently across the immersive sector. The most recent conversations with 
immersive sector businesses and organisations suggest that: 
 Funded projects, particularly in collaborative R&D, are experiencing 
delays and require the agreement of funders to revise timescales 
 New product release timetables (for both online and physical products) 
have been postponed 
 There has been volatility in demand for ‘work for hire’, with demand for 
promotional campaigns and applications utilising XR from some sectors 
(including construction) seeing an increase whilst other consumer 
product sectors have seen a fall 
 Some employers have been able to make use of ‘furlough’ provisions; 
but worry that these will be too short term to address continuing volatility 
over the next 6-18 months (especially as many ‘work for hire’ projects 
are delivered over a 4-5 week timescale), meaning that there is a risk of 
a second and subsequent waves of companies having to lay off staff 
 That in turn risks losing capacity and skills built up over several years, 
which may be harder to get back once demand returns to its pre-crisis 
trend, leading to a shrinking of the sector, a relative weakening of the 
competitive advantages inherent in its current workforce and a long-
term loss of opportunities for inclusive growth in a high value 
employment sector within LCR 
 Immersive companies are subject to the same difficulties in accessing 
the CBILS (business interruption loan) scheme and the same low levels 
of acceptance as other businesses  Many smaller businesses will be sub-letting or in shared workspace 
where business rates are included within rental payments, and 
therefore not eligible for the business rates relief grant 
 Owner-directors who pay themselves through a mixture of salary and 
dividends are unable to access either employment-based reliefs or 
support for self-employed people 
 Freelance workers who entered the workforce in the last eighteen 
months, or who have become self-employed in that time, are similarly 
unable to access either employment-based reliefs or support for the 
self-employed at this time; given the high levels of dependency on 
freelance workers highlighted in the survey, this could have a lasting

www.bop.co.uk 51  Appendix 1 - Methodology 
Immersive content and technologies encompass a range of sectors, job titles, 
skills, and markets which, because of their novelty and variety, cannot be 
summarised through analysis of Standard Industrial Classifications (SIC codes) 
and Standard Occupational Classifications (SOC codes) alone. To address 
these complexities, this study took a three-stage approach to the mapping of 
companies, assets, and investors. It employed a combination of primary and 
secondary sources, including a bespoke database of companies identified by 
Artificial Intelligence (AI) web search, to provide a knowledge base that was 
then analysed for evidence of immersive activity.  
The methodology for this study built upon previous sector analysis of UK 
immersive content workforce and companies developed for Innovate UK as part 
of a three-year evaluation of the Audience of the Future (AotF) project. It 
extended that analysis to include agencies, producers and technology providers 
in adjacent markets and end users in other industrial sectors - including 
advanced manufacturing, education, health, construction, and logistics  - to 
provide an updated and more comprehensive picture of immersive employment 
and businesses within the Liverpool City Region. 
 
Output Approach  
Benchmarking and 
horizon scanning Taken in two parts: initial desk-based  market 
assessment and horizon scanning  to establish 
market context for immersive activity in LCR; and, 
following sector mapping, a further stage of 
benchmarking  the scale and attributes of the 
immersive sector in LCR against comparator cities  
Mapping of sector 
supply chains and 
capacity An initial phase of desk-based research, using a 
methodology developed and refined through 
immersive sector analysis for Digital Catapult and 
Innovate UK, to map the scale and significance  of 
immersive supply chains and workforce within LCR, 
complemented by primary research  with industry and other stakeholders to establish specialisms, facilities 
and opportunities to grow the sector. 
Gap analysis Analysis of findings to establish a clear picture of the 
immersive ecosystem in the city region, accompanied 
by a gap analysis to highlight weaknesses that could 
be addressed by further intervention or investment 
Recommendations A report summarising the findings and 
recommendations in a clear and jargon-free way, 
providing a document that can inform future research, 
policy development or investment decisions 
 
Three-stage approach 
Stage 1 - identify 
BOP and Curator have developed and refined an approach to mapping 
immersive sectors through secondary research that draws on multiple sources   
 Company data: Companies House and other proprietary sources of 
financial data, parsed via DueDil  
 Workforce: LinkedIn and Office of National Statistics (ONS) data, via 
online access to the Business Register and Employment survey (BRES) 
 Private sector investment: Crunchbase  
 Research and innovation: (business and academic): analysis of UK 
Research & Innovation Grants and Research Database (GRAND) and 
Arts Council England investment 
 Business community and ecosystem:  analysis of social media including 
Meetup groups, Twitter, Facebook, and Instagram; trade organisations 
and directories 
These data build upon a bespoke database of companies identified by a n 
Artificial Intelligence (AI) web search to give evidence of:

Core to the mapping of the skilled immersive workforce in LCR was an in-
depth analysis of LinkedIn data. LinkedIn was used as a huge sample 
frame into which a large proportion of the workforce  - 26 million UK users 
to date - has opted to share information about the industry they work in, 
their skillsets, job roles and experience. This mitigates the risk of low 
response rates in other forms of sampling – including ONS data and online 
surveys – to provide a more comprehensive and up-to-date record of 
industry activity, skills, experience, and workforce trends. 
This method:  
 Can help define workforce experience 
 Allows for key word search against individuals’ skillsets  
 Identifies the top regional employers across the immersive value 
chain. 
 
Stage 2 – assure 
Keyword search was used to assure our initial findings.  This stage allows us to ensure that the scale of the immersive cluster was not 
overstated or understated – e.g. by removing  ‘false positives’, dissolved 
companies etc. This process: 
 Identifies all the businesses that could fall within scope 
 Confirms that the businesses identified are active 
 Confirms that the company is undertaking immersive activity 
 Provides evidence of addresses of business premises (i.e., is the 
business operating within LCR) 
 Of the businesses that are no longer active, seeks to identify any that 
have been acquired or merged (itself an indicator of potential growth 
ambition) rather than just closed 
 Checks whether the businesses that are dormant or have been 
dissolved are continuing to trade under a different name or as part of 
another group. 
Web search also provided insight into other kinds of immersive activities, e.g.: 
 Other kinds of ‘non-business’ activity (e.g., projects put together by 
individuals or other ‘special purpose vehicles’, including in cultural 
organisations or the public sector, rather than under the auspices of a 
registered business 
 Evidence of investments in research, innovation and development not 
identified thorough analysis of public and private sector investment 
databases (Gateway to Research and Crunchbase) 
 
Stage 3 – Synthesise 
Bringing together the mapping with the desk research, findings of an online 
survey, interviews with industry and public sector bodies and case studies of 
organisations within LCR to produce the final report.
