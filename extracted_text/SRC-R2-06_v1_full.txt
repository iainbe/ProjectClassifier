www.bop.co.uk 1  Executive summary  
West Midlands Combined Authority (WMCA) is one of three city regions to have 
agreed a Sector Deal for Creative Industries. This includes funding for a 
creative business scale-up programme, to boost growth and productivity in the 
sector across all parts of the region. 
WMCA needed to establish which creative businesses in the region would most 
likely be eligible for, and derive most benefit from, this programme. The 
Common Application Gateway (CAG) criteria established for the programme by 
DCMS – turnover growth of 10% and a minimum of 8 employees (a mixture of 
four permanent and four freelance workers) – represent a high bar for the 
majority of West Midlands creative businesses. A more detailed and specialist 
account of the creative sector was needed to unearth the latent scale-up 
potential of creative businesses across the city region. 
BOP had previously carried out a mapping of the creative economy in Greater 
Birmingham & Solihull in 2017/18, using Business Register and Employment 
Survey (BRES) data analysed by standard DCMS Creative Industries sector 
definitions to compile a dataset of creative businesses. This was analysed for 
the strengths and opportunities both within the creative industries sub-sectors 
defined by DCMS1 and across four supply chains defined by product type:  
‒ Creative Content: eg, television, film, digital media, software and 
applications, computer games, publishing, recorded music 
‒ Creative Experiences: eg, live performance, venues, museums, 
galleries and libraries 
‒ Creative Originals: eg, visual arts, fashion, crafts, product design 
‒ Creative Services: eg, advertising and marketing, architecture crafts, 
graphic design, design consultancy, computer services 
 
 
1 Advertising and marketing; architecture; craft; design; film, television, video, radio and photography; IT, software 
and computer services; publishing; museums, galleries and libraries; and music, performing and visual arts. For this study, BOP joined with City Curator to pursue an innovative and multi-
method approach to mapping creative businesses and workforce to provide 
both a fine-grained understanding of creative businesses’ ‘scale-up potential’ 
and contact, financial and employment details of individual creative businesses.  
Initial mapping of the scale and significance of the sector in September 2019 
identified some 14,900 businesses registered at Companies House under one 
of the DCMS Creative Industries subsectors with an office in one of the WMCA 
constituent and non-constituent member and partner areas.  
Initial analysis of this cohort to establish details of employment, turnover, 
profitability and assets resulted in a ‘longlist’ of 2,336 businesses for further 
analysis by a range of methods including AI web search, social media analysis, 
records of public grants and proprietary investment databases. The method 
used LinkedIn data to provide more up to date information on company size, 
location and activities. This resulted in a shortlist of 645 companies, including 49 
‘non-creative industries businesses’ (ie, those not in a DCMS CI SIC code), 
uncovered through keyword search, whose products or services either clearly 
formed part of a creative industries supply chain or made extensive use of 
creative and immersive content and technologies .  
As suspected at the outset, very few of the shortlist – 26 companies – met both 
the CAG criteria, with a further 386 meeting one or other criterion. We applied 
other measures of private sector investment value, public investment, social 
media and recruitment activity to establish a level of confidence in the scale-up 
potential of the shortlisted companies. This indicated that there were 168 West 
Midlands creative businesses with a very strong likelihood of scale-up potential, 
and a further 253 which we think could benefit from the programme. 
We organised our findings into a colour-coded dashboard (provided as an 
accompanying document to this report) which also sets out further details of 
company founders, employment numbers and contact details. The result is a 
comprehensive toolkit to assist the programme director in marketing and 
recruitment.

1.2 Common application gateway criteria 
We tested all 14,903 eligible creative businesses against DCMS’s ‘common 
application gateway’ (CAG) criteria for the creative business scale-up 
programme:  
‒ Turnover growth equal to or greater than 10% in the last year (see 1.3, 
below) 
‒ Eight or more employees (see 1.4, below) 
Only a tiny minority of company records - ten companies, of which two were part 
of the same group – contained data which indicated that the business met both 
CAG criteria. Below we explain why this information is not available in most 
cases, and the steps we took to try to mitigate that. 1.3 Measuring turnover growth 
Most companies are below the £10.2 million per annum turnover threshold at 
which they have to submit full accounts to Companies House. This means that 
most companies submit accounts in one of two forms: 
Micro-entity accounts 
Companies may submit ‘micro-entity accounts’ if they meet two of the following 
criteria: 
‒ Turnover is no more than £632,000 
‒ Balance sheet total is no more than £316,000 
‒ Average number of employees is no more than 10  
The minimum level of disclosure for micro-entity accounts consist of: 
‒ Simple balance sheet and footnotes 
‒ Signature of a director and their name printed on the balance sheet. 
‒ Statement on the balance sheet above the director’s signature that the 
accounts have been prepared 
‒ Simple profit and loss account 
Whilst companies that can be defined as micro-entities are potentially in scope 
of the scale-up programme, theses limited reporting requirements mean that it is 
very unlikely that the public record will contain details of turnover or turnover 
growth. 
Abridged accounts 
Companies may submit abridged accounts whose: 
‒ Turnover is no more than £10.2 million 
‒ Balance sheet total is no more than £5.1 million 
‒ Average number of employees is no more than 50

www.bop.co.uk 3  Abridged accounts include abbreviated versions of the company balance sheet 
and profit and loss account. Again, this can mean that the public record does 
not contain details of turnover; but we are more likely to able to find evidence of 
turnover or growth, either year-on-year or on a Compound Annual Growth Rate 
(CAGR) basis, for these companies. 
1.4 Measuring employment 
DCMS had already indicated that there was some flexibility around the definition 
of ‘employees’. To reflect the known prevalence of freelance and contract 
working, the common application gateway criteria could be interpreted to mean 
businesses with four permanent and four freelance employees in the last year. 
However, in most cases it was not possible to determine an exact number of 
employees from publicly available data, nor a split of permanent and freelance 
workers. Our main sources of data for employment numbers were: 
‒ Team pages on the company web site – although many companies do 
not have either a web site or a team page 
‒ Information about the company and its employees from LinkedIn.  
LinkedIn now has 25 million UK users and provides a much larger and more 
accurate sample of employment than BRES data. LinkedIn also generates an 
estimate of employee numbers based on the number of subscribers with links to 
the company, for which the lowest band is companies with 2-10 employees. 
This led us to determine that we would carry out further analysis on all 
companies for which we could find evidence of at least two employees. This 
‘permissive’ approach allowed us to include more businesses in our initial 
longlist for further detailed analysis.

2. Selection of the shortlist 
2.1 In-depth analysis of the longlist 
Our initial analysis has resulted in a cohort of 2,336 businesses – 2,082 aged 
between two and ten years, 66 younger than two years and 188 between 11 
and 15 years old. We carried out further detailed analysis of their scale-up 
potential to arrive at a ‘scale-up score’ for each company. 
These analyses included: 
‒ AI web search  that looked for additional evidence of growth factors, eg, 
by searching for team, project and client pages on the company web 
site. This: 
o Provided more information about company size, products and 
services) 
o Helped us categorise businesses into product or services 
companies, and allocate them to one of four creative supply 
chains (or none) 
o Gave insight into the scope of their activity for further (manual) 
analysis 
o Eliminated those companies without a web site 
‒ Crunchbase , to establish evidence of private investment within the last 
three years 
‒ LinkedIn  data, to provide more accurate estimates of total employment, 
including freelancers, and details of directors active in the business 
‒ UK Research & Innovation’s Grants for R&D (GRAND)  database, for 
evidence of successful applications for public funding 
‒ Recruitment  web sites, to identify companies actively hiring

2.2 Eligibility 
‘Eligible’ businesses are those which met both the Common Application 
Gateway criteria established by (DCMS) for admission to the creative business 
scale-up programme. From the shortlist, only 26 companies met both criteria. 
2.3 Confidence (scale-up criteria) 
The research methodology was designed to address the risk (crystallised 
through analysis) that too few West Midlands creative businesses would meet 
the CAG criteria. The combination of sources detailed at 2.1, above, was used 
to determine a further four scale-up criteria: 
‒ Private Sector Investment Value 
‒ Public Investment 
‒ Social Media Test  
‒ Recruitment

The following tables summarise our analysis of the 645 businesses using the 
scale-up scoring methodology described in our proposal.  
The scale-up criteria were very useful in discerning the differences between the 
619 businesses that did not meet both of the CAG criteria, and yielded the 
following results: 
‒ None of the shortlisted businesses met all four scale-up criteria 
(outlined at 2.3 above.)  
‒ 168 businesses met two or three criteria (scored ‘Very Good’) 
‒ A further 253 businesses met at least one criterion (scored ‘Good’) 
‒ 224 businesses met none of the investment or recruitment tests and 
had either no or partial social media coverage (scored ‘Low’.) 
For the purposes of interpretation, we have assumed that companies scoring 
‘Very Good’ or ‘Good’ have some scale-up potential. We have not analysed the

We further analysed of the results using the methodology established in our 
2017/18 mapping to the creative economy of the Greater Birmingham & Solihull 
LEP area3. This recombined SIC codes of our shortlisted companies into 
descriptions of creative supply chains established in our previous study.  
The 645 shortlisted companies divided into the following creative supply chain 
groupings: 
‒ Creative Content: 325 (50%) 
‒ Creative Experiences: 50 (8%) 
‒ Creative Originals 57 (9%) 
‒ Creative Services: 183 (28%) 
A further 30 businesses (5% of the total shortlist) were included because of their 
use of creative and immersive technologies in non-creative applications, 
although they did not fit the definition of any of the four creative supply chains.
