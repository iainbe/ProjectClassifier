Executive summary
1.1	Research brief
The Arts, Culture and Leisure Service at Wakefield Council commissioned this research as part of the development of a new creative skills development programme for locally rooted Cultural, Creative & Digital Industries (CCDI).
This is a collaborative initiative within Wakefield Council led by the Arts, Culture and Leisure Service together with the Economic Growth & Skills Service. The Council has developed a comprehensive Employment and Skills Strategy for Wakefield district - including identifying the city as part of an international network of Learning Cities – and is currently undertaking a review of existing provision across its Arts, Culture and Leisure Service. The new creative skills programme will add value through the creation of a targeted and sector-specific programme.  
The research provides insight into the following areas:
	•	The current skills offer for progression into CCDI across the learning spectrum 
	•	Entry points for progression into CCDI at every stage of life and career development
	•	Gaps of the current offer and opportunities for development
	•	The level of accessibility of the current offer
	•	Skills pathways for training / career development in CCDI
	•	The perspective of industry on the quality, relevance and reach of the current offer
	•	The perspective of training providers on the quality, relevance and reach of the current offer
The brief called for: an audit of current provision and evidence of skills pathways for progression into CCDI via training or employment from entry level to career development, as well as transfer into CCDI from other sectors, informed by the analysis of current provision. 
We consulted with local CCDI employers to gather insight into the current landscape and with training providers to gather insight into the quality, relevance and reach of the current offer. We looked into the popularity of courses and pathways with learners; demand from employers; and progression into further learning or employment. We also looked into the key drivers behind the current offer, how it is being develop and, the support needed to maximise the reach and impact of creative skills training.
1.2	Skills pathways
Most arts and creative courses, and many digital courses, are not directly vocational. Unlike professions such as accountancy, law or medicine, there are few roles within CCDI which require formal certification or completion of an accredited qualification as a precondition of employment. (Exceptions include the need for Health & Safety and electrical engineering qualifications in parts of the production services sector.) In addition, the accelerating pace of digital transformation across CCDI means that professionals are constantly acquiring new skills, either self-taught or through CPD, to keep pace with technological changes. 
Mapping skills pathways in CCDI, therefore, is an art and not a science. Our aim was to produce a typology of skills which was easy to understand and linked training and qualifications to occupations in a way which would make sense to students, providers, employers and careers advisers alike. It was also important that the framework should be ‘future proof’ and adaptable to future changes in technology and the continuous innovation in job roles and descriptions which is taking place across the industry. To do that, we mapped skills pathways against five digital and creative sector supply chains, each of them made up of a series of LinkedIn industry codes:
	•	Creative originals: arts & crafts, fine art, apparel & fashion
	•	Creative services: marketing and advertising, architecture & planning, graphic design, product design, design services
	•	Creative experiences: music, performing arts, museums and institutions, libraries 
	•	Creative content: computer games, mobile games, publishing, motion pictures & film, broadcast media, animation, photography
	•	Digital technology: information technology and services, computer & network security, computer software, computer networking
Basing our analysis on these five skills pathways helped us to see strengths and gaps in provision and provided insight into skills which provide a ‘platform’ for further development.
Within our work, we recognise the disproportionate impact that the COVID emergency has had on the most disadvantaged and marginalised communities. We sense-checked all our activity through an intersectional lens, and in doing so identified the shortfalls and inequalities of access that exist with regard to under-represented voices, especially within neighbourhoods.
1.3	Policy context
Changes to Higher Education funding mean that creative and arts courses now attract a lower tariff than some other subjects (notably, STEM) – with the result that Russell Group universities say that arts courses will run at £2,700 deficit per student. 
This is balanced by an increased focus on addressing employer needs through Further Education. The Skills and Post-16 Education Bill (May 2021) calls for funding for employers to get involved in course development and introduces a duty for colleges to be responsive to local needs. Learners will benefit from a lifelong learning loan (operational from 2025) and a lifelong skills guarantee; whilst FE Colleges with HE centres, such as Wakefield College, will see more funding for Higher Level courses in FE.
The education sector response has included innovations such as ‘microcredentials’, accreditation for smaller units of learning which can be aggregated up toward a qualification or as part of a learning portfolio, and a shift in funding to allow learners with existing qualifications to ‘go back’ a level (or two) and add a ‘fourth year’ through microcredentials – commonly referred to as ‘top-up’ learning. In HE, we have seen universities creating MOOCs (massive open online courses) with an element of free online learning to attract users, who can then progress to either microcredentials or full-time courses.
The introduction of these more flexible forms of training reflect the urgings of creative employers over a number of years.
However, there is a risk that these changes will be accompanied by a loss of funding for creative arts (to STEM subjects) and will lead to HEIs and FE either dropping fine arts or (more positively) incorporating more creative technology-based practice in those courses. In an attempt to address this, the Creative Industries Council and others are lobbying for the definition (and by extension, funding) for STEM courses to incorporate the arts (‘STEAM’).
1.4	Wakefield’s CCDI sector and workforce
Using the same supply-chain based pathways to analyse the CCDI sector and workforce identified that the business base in Wakefield is larger than revealed in national statistics, with close to double the number of businesses in design, music, performing & visual arts, advertising & marketing and publishing as shown in the latest BRES data.
Analysis of LinkedIn showed that 5,505 individuals in Wakefield stated that their main occupation was in one of the cultural, creative or digital industries sectors, as opposed to 2,800 in BRES. These differences highlight the significant numbers of freelance/self-employed individuals in the sector; and give a better indication of potential demand for creative skills from students, employers and creative professionals.

In Further Education, we found excellent provision for pathways 2, 3 and 4 but very little for pathway 5; provision for pathway 1 was mostly evening classes. There were good examples for pathways within subjects (eg, York College Art & Design from levels 1-3 with follow-on pathways at level 6 and Wakefield College from level 1 right through to Level 6) and some provision for 16–19-year-old, including: two BFI Film Academies, in Leeds and at Workstation in Sheffield. 
There is excellent Higher Education provision across Yorkshire with 512 degree courses covering a wide range of creative sectors and disciplines, including TV, film, music, performance, PR, fashion, animation and games design, with good pathways into Masters courses. There is a good range of CPD from universities including Leeds Beckett, York and Nottingham Trent.
We identified a good range of Apprenticeships available at Level 2 and 3, but mostly in the digital space; as expected, we found few apprenticeships in creative areas. 
T-levels are to be introduced in 2021 to provide a focus for vocational studies. They are the equivalent to 3 A-levels and must include a minimum of 45 days’ work experience. To date, only one T-Level in a CCDI pathway has been approved (Digital Production, Design and Development Level 3, designed to lead to jobs in web development, software development, mobile app developer, programmer), with a new T-level in Craft and Design (to cover jewellery, ceramics, furniture and textile making) due for release in 2023. None of the employers we interviewed showed any awareness of T-levels or how they would work. 
Continuing Professional Development (CPD) provision showed an excellent range of courses for pathway 2 (mostly Adobe for creative services companies) and good provision for pathway 4 (including National Film & Television School, Screen Yorkshire Centre of Excellence and Screenskills online and reflecting the Channel 4 presence in Leeds)
Placements
It is clear that placements are highly sought after in both HE and FE, with over 1,000 placement places identified for creative and media courses through our survey participants. Although not required in all courses areas, industry placements are required in others (eg, journalism). Providers cited the difficulty in securing placements during last year due to the pandemic, putting even more pressure on finding placements for this and future years. 
Although 78% of survey participants stated that they had a member of staff dedicated to finding placements, some also cited the lack of staff or staff being “stretched” to do this on top of teaching requirements.

“One of the challenges is that as educational institutions, we can't approve students to miss classes and learning without reasonable notice and many placement opportunities (understandably, due to the way the industries work) are almost immediate.”

This response highlights issues of timing and a disconnect between the needs of employers and of University educators.
Some institutions reported support for placements from a strong alumni network and investment in employability at all levels. 
1.6	Employer needs and future skills
Skills needs change rapidly across the CCDI sector, driven both by developments in technology and changes in markets.
For example, the film and television industry is going through rapid changes not only technically but also on the type of content that is being required, from subscription video on demand (SVOD – services such as Netflix, Prime etc) to ‘high end’ unique dramas. There are new roles appearing all the time, not least on the digital tech side. As we look to an ever faster paced digital world with ‘on demand’ services sitting alongside and competing with ‘terrestrial’ TV, future skills needs will continue to develop apace.
Employer responses are summarised below:
	•	Need for greater “job-readiness”
	•	There is a willingness to work more with providers
	•	Apprenticeships work for technical and engineering grades
	•	More leadership and management and business skills needed
	•	Increasing importance of diversity and inclusion
	•	Rapid changes in technology impacting at all levels 
	•	Pandemic – has been “like falling off a cliff”
1.7	Areas for improvement
Industry awareness and engagement
HE and FE institutions are still dividing the creative study areas into Arts & Design or Digital technologies; this fails to recognise the impact of digital technologies on a range of creative areas and acts as a barrier to both student recruitment and employer engagement. Similarly, providers offered no evidence of success rates of progression into further learning or employment within the creative industries. 
This reflects a ‘gap’ between providers and employers, who reported low levels of engagement with HE and FE institutions. This forms part of a long-term pattern of employers feeling that graduates are not ‘work-ready’, whilst providers assert that they are delivering education, not training. 
There are few examples of interesting industry partnerships in Wakefield and its surrounding areas. We can contrast this with a good example at Manchester College as part of its Industry Excellence Academy, which offers 4-6 week paid internships (Level 3) with Flow Creative with Motion Design & Digital.
1.8	Diagnostic tools 
Analysis of web sites from skills bodies revealed that skills assessments have replaced Training Needs Analysis (TNA) – eg, the National Careers Service skills assessment web site offers a comprehensive range of online resources.
Whilst individual providers and trade associations are no longer offering TNA, hybrid sites – like the intogames site provided by Ukie, the UK games trade association - offer an experiential way for learners to explore skills needs.
This led us to explore the potential to create a ‘blended’ solution, in which training providers and/or employers refer individuals to the National Careers Service, and learners/employees bring back their results as the basis of an individual diagnostic session with an expert advisor
In response to this, the course data collected was input to Tableau to provide a searchable database of some 1300 arts and creative courses across West Yorkshire. This can be easily updated by importing spreadsheets containing new course details.
1.9	Opportunities
Wakefield has several key sectors with established capacity and potential to stimulate further growth and demand for skills across the district:
	•	Cultural venues, both National Portfolio Organisations and smaller hubs
	•	Games, where Team 17 represents a ‘hero’ brand for the sector and region
	•	Production services, for which Production Park represents a hub of national significance
Our analysis of the business base and workforce also revealed nascent strengths in other sectors notably music, visual & performing arts, design, advertising & marketing and publishing. Most of these are microbusinesses - many individual freelancers who may alternate between leading projects and forming part of supply chain for others. Policy makers and providers need to find ways of engaging with the employment and entrepreneurial opportunities in these sectors which helps match supply with demand for skills and innovation which are not overly bureaucratic and burdensome to the employer. 
In particular, there is a need for innovative thinking to establish how the creative sector can collectively meet demand for placements across apprenticeships, T-Levels and degree courses in a way which reflects the working patterns and structural capacity of individual organisations.
Investment in workspace – not just WX3, but private sector investment in Tileyard and the expansion of Production Park, points to confidence in the sector’s ability to overcome the shocks of the Covid-19 pandemic and return to innovation led growth. 
The commitment from local and regional government and providers to ‘joined-up’ provision is evident from our consultation. The creation of Creative Wakefield provides further opportunity to lobby regional and national bodies to represent opportunities in Wakefield, including Screen Yorkshire, WYCA and creative sector skills councils, Screenskills and CCS.
1.10	Embedding diversity
Subsectors within CCDI are responding differently to the challenge around embedding diversity and inclusion: the film and television sector is linking commissioning spend to targets around gender, BAME representation and disability for both on-screen performers, employees and freelance crew.
Further research in this area is needed, but having a voice is an absolute necessity. Policy makers, providers and industry should continue to consult with underrepresented groups and existing cultural groups that currently do not have a voice within the industry (eg, ethnically diverse, queer and trans people, disabled people, older people) to ensure that education and skills training provision reflects the whole of Wakefield district in its governance and operation. There is a role for Creative Wakefield in identifying and supporting ‘champions’ going out to solicit and support the development of formal and work-based learning. Those representatives should in turn become ‘ambassadors’ to ensure equitable access to provision and to grow the new cultural leaders from within Wakefield’s communities.

1.11	Recommendations
	•	Use Creative Wakefield and the WX3 programme together to build on existing strengths within the cultural sector – top-down and bottom up – and provide a platform for working collectively, providing resources for artists and freelancers
	•	Build on the extraordinary efforts made by the cultural sector during COVID emergency to reinforce its organisations and its activities within communities.
	•	Offer increased diversity (young people, older people, disabled, d/Deaf and neurodivergent people, LGBTQ+, ethnically diverse and SEB etc). Brokering relationships with VCSE organisations, the education sector and the culture and heritage sectors, increases access and provides equity for underrepresented groups to have a voice and stake in the cultural life of the city. 
	•	Creative Wakefield provides a mechanism for discovery, development, diversification and growth of a new generation of leaders. Diversification and distribution of cultural power and equity means challenging established orthodoxies and reframing what culture in the city and its neighbourhoods look like. This transition leads to a powerful cultural scene that is predicated on co-design and collaboration and develops a new cultural leadership that has generosity, inclusion and distributed power at its core.
	•	Promote (and establish protocols and data privacy policies for) sharing of knowledge and intelligence amongst existing Council business support services to employer relationships across culture, heritage, VCSE and communities, and skills providers. Tableau tool should be a regularly updated resource shared between providers, employers, policy makers, schools and creative organisations of all kinds. It should be complemented by creation of a resource bank and ‘easy reads’ on skills policy and funding.
	•	Bring WYCA closer to the ambition of Creative Wakefield. The focus on Leeds (as a point of aggregation and attractor of investment for CCDI) and Bradford (with its focus on increasing ethnic diversity and youth engagement within the sector) is both rational in policy terms and a reflection of stretched resources, but Wakefield has a distinctive offer which combines both national centres of excellence and an opportunity to address the disparity in opportunities from more deprived socio-economic backgrounds.
	•	Strengthen employer-provider dialogue on the principle of knowledge exchange means this is about levelling the playing field – not ‘levelling up’. Local artists have an important role in leadership here, creating and communicating a narrative of the sub-region’s cultural and creative strengths and ambition in a way that brings it more frequently and more forcefully to the attention of employers, investors, students, mid-career professionals, arms-length bodies and policy makers in government in a way that results in Wakefield getting not just a larger slice of investment, but the investment it deserves and needs to further its creative and cultural ambitions. 
	•	Skills and opportunities need to be developed in a way that is geographically permeable – removing the perception and reality of a focus on Wakefield city centre and recognising the value of and connections between assets, audiences and communities across Wakefield and the five towns, across the West Yorkshire region, nationally and internationally.
	•	Explore the potential within Cultural Development Fund, Kickstarter and other funds to support placements with CCDI employers – including microbusinesses. The ‘ask’ from the sector within this small consultation is quite aspirational. If it is to happen there will need to be a dedicated resource.
	•	Use Creative Wakefield as a forum to establish industry links to provide mentoring support from larger organisations for smaller organisations and individual artists.

5.3	Diversity and inclusion
It is clear from our consultations with both providers and employers that diversity and inclusion are crucial to release the true potential of the region’s creative talent. 
Whilst there was almost unanimous agreement on this point, many commented that the pandemic had set back progress being made into recruiting a more diverse workforce by several years. 
Some employers have clear diversity targets which are becoming increasingly difficult to hit. With all new BBC productions having to hit a 20% diversity target since last November, TV content producers remarked on the impact of the pandemic which has seen many in a largely freelance workforce choose to seek employment outside the industry. 
Monitoring of Widening Participation within FE and HE is also a requirement, and several providers believe there is a role for local authorities to champion the value of the arts and creative industries as important and fulfilling careers.

“In terms of placements, we frequently find nepotism (placements going to those related to people who already work in the industry). In addition, our students are often offered placements which are well below minimum wage. For example, a student has just been offered a six-month placement in London and they have offered £300 a month - which wouldn't even cover transport costs. Unpaid or unfairly paid placements are thus only 'available' to students who come from privileged backgrounds.”

5.5	How is the current offer being developed and what will it look like in the future? 
	•	Higher Education funding
Current Government policy is that creative arts courses are not a strategic priority with a view that funding should be reprioritised “towards the provision of high-cost, high-value subjects“.
Earlier in 2021, the Education Secretary, Gavin Williamson announced changes to the funding for arts to create savings of around £20m. Under the plan spending for non-prioritised subjects is to be cut from £36m to £19m, with the savings being redirected to other “prioritised” areas, such as nursing and computing.
In HE, changes to funding levels during the 2021-22 year onwards could impact heavily on future growth. Funding from the Office for Students (Ofs, the independent regulator of higher education) is to be cut by half for students of art and design, music, dance, drama and performing arts, media studies and archaeology. In effect, this cuts the award for each student from £243 to £121 per academic year.
The Russell Group of universities argues that the affected courses will now run at a deficit of about £2,700 per student, including the income from tuition fees.
	•	Further Education
The announcement of a Skills Lifetime Guarantee by the Prime Minister in April will entitle around 11 million people who have no Level 3 qualifications to access qualifications for free. In line with Government policy, the 400 initial qualifications on offer are for “in-demand” skills and do not include creative arts/media areas but do include digital (eg, software development, digital engineering, digital product design, cyber security, computing etc).
In our provider survey, the impact of these funding changes is evident with clear expansion in areas of digital technology (87% predict growth in this pathway) and 77% indicating that funding levels will influence future provision.

	•	Bootcamps 
Government has also announced a skills bootcamps programme. These are free flexible courses of learning between 12 and 16 weeks for adults aged over 19 designed to develop sector-specific skills and fast-track to an interview with an employer. 
Over the last two years, trailblazer bootcamps were delivered in a range of digital areas including software development, data analytics and digital marketing. Further funding for more bootcamps is expected imminently. 
Government states that,
“Key requirements will be to deliver the skills and wider competencies that employers require to recruit to specialist digital roles and that a learner gets a job at the end.”
The initiative aligns substantially with what employers say is lacking – the gap between education and being ‘job ready’ is too wide. However, with the concentration of funding for bootcamps on digital and tech areas, impact on creative and arts employers will be minimal. 
Bootcamp-type training in West Yorkshire includes ‘Beyond Brontës’, delivered by Screen Yorkshire, which has just recruited its third cohort. Set up two years ago with funding from the City of Bradford Metropolitan Borough and the Leeds LEP, the scheme offers a range of training over an intensive six-month period for 50 18-24-year-olds from the Leeds City Region (including Wakefield). The package includes mentoring, 1-2-1 support, paid internships and careers information. Whilst the scheme has impressive employment results, it is designed for one sub-sector: the screen industries.
	•	Placements
It is clear that placements are highly sought after in both HE and FE with over 1,000 placement places identified for creative and media courses through our survey participants. Although not required in all courses areas, industry placements are required in others (eg, journalism). Providers cited the difficulty in securing placements during last year due to the pandemic, putting even more pressure on finding placements for this and future years. 
Although 78% of survey participants stated that they had a member of staff dedicated to finding placements, some also cited the lack of staff or staff being “stretched” to do this on top of teaching requirements. 
“One of the challenges is that as educational institutions, we can't approve students to miss classes and learning without reasonable notice and many placement opportunities (understandably, due to the way the industries work) are almost immediate.”
This response highlights issues of timing and a disconnect between the needs of employers and of University educators.
Some institutions reported support for placements from a strong alumni network and investment in employability at all levels.

New entrants are not work ready, or to not have the right skills for the industry.
We run outreach projects such as Northern Lights where we do workshops, projects, talks and advisory sessions with various universities. In these workshops feedback from students is that they’ve learnt more in that week/day than in a whole chunk of their courses. 
Lack of direction is an issue for many employers.
The university model seems to cast a very wide net offering multiple courses across industry subjects and it’s a concern that sometimes this is more driven by income than by offering learning. 
Schools need to be brought into the equation, getting the message across to younger students about the options, roles, careers available in the industry are available to them not only helps with the choices they make to enter the industry (apprenticeship route rather than Uni etc) but also the wider message of D & I – the fact this is an industry open to the masses for successful long-term careers.
They are trying but don't really turn out the people that we need.

6.3	Impact of COVID
With a predominantly freelance workforce, and a huge amount of face-to-face interaction, the creative industries has been one of the sectors hardest hit by the measures to counteract the pandemic. Film and television production was first suspended, then allowed to reopen subject to imposing sanitary measures which add an estimated 15% to any production budget; large parts of the live entertainment sector have, at time of writing, yet to reopen. Many live events and festivals have lost two full years of revenue, whilst the uncertainty about dates and conditions under which the sector might reopen has forced some employers to suspend or scale back training and development.
COVID has meant huge changes to the way we film and the way our buildings work. Our priorities remain to produce the best content we can and we’re very proud of the fact we led the world in getting back to filming whilst the pandemic progressed. A lot of our workforce has been working from home throughout and a big priority for us is to make sure they still feel part of the team and are able to manage their work life balance whilst not being in the office for over 12 months. There is a good deal of support given to ensure the wellbeing of staff throughout the pandemic and the change in working environment. A Wellbeing manager is responsible for training courses and activities to ensure that people can collaborate virtually and have sufficient breaks away from meetings.
The main impact on skills is being unable to progress any of our normal outreach and training initiatives for the past 12 months, we have supported FE remotely as and when we can and we will soon be restarting various initiatives including ensuring diverse and under-represented groups get a good understanding of the roles available within the industry, and ultimately the chance to work with us. 
The pandemic presented a very real challenge for us. It was like “falling off a cliff” and the company lost many clients. Some have returned and some new ones not on board. Fiscal and cashflow was the main problem.

Summary of findings
8.1	Opportunities
Wakefield has several key sectors with established capacity and potential to stimulate further growth and demand for skills across the district:
	•	Cultural venues, both National Portfolio Organisations and smaller hubs
	•	Games, where Team 17 represents a ‘hero’ brand for the sector and region
	•	Production services, for which Production Park represents a hub of national significance
Our analysis of the business base and workforce also revealed nascent strengths in other sectors notably music, visual & performing arts, design, advertising & marketing and publishing. Most of these are microbusinesses - many individual freelancers who may alternate between leading projects and forming part of supply chain for others. Policy makers and providers need to find ways of engaging with the employment and entrepreneurial opportunities in these sectors which helps match supply with demand for skills and innovation which are not overly bureaucratic and burdensome to the employer. 
In particular, there is a need for innovative thinking to establish how the creative sector can collectively meet demand for placements across apprenticeships, T-Levels and degree courses in a way which reflects the working patterns and structural capacity of individual organisations.
Investment in workspace – not just WX3, but private sector investment in Tileyard and the expansion of Production Park, points to confidence in the sector’s ability to overcome the shocks of the Covid-19 pandemic and return to innovation led growth. 
The commitment from local and regional government and providers to ‘joined-up’ provision is evident from our consultation. The creation of Creative Wakefield provides further opportunity to lobby regional and national bodies to represent opportunities in Wakefield, including:
	•	Screen Yorkshire
	•	WYCA
	•	Screenskills/CCS
8.2	Embedding diversity 
Further research and consultations are needed concerning governance and sector representation. Subsectors within CCDI are responding differently to the challenge around embedding diversity and inclusion: the film and television sector is linking commissioning spend to targets around gender, BAME representation and disability for both on-screen performers, employees and freelance crew, whilst other sectors are taking a lighter touch, self-regulating approach. From a policy and skills provider perspective, understanding how the sector pivots, flexes and shifts in response to market and societal changes should be an on-going piece of work.
This highlights the need for greater shared understanding within the sector, and a sharing of learning and best practice. Creative Wakefield should facilitate cross sector working to make sure representation is a call to action and not an invitation. In order to support this, there needs to be resource (funding) to support small organisations and individual artists/ practitioners to take part and to also take the lead.
Having a voice is an absolute necessity. Policy makers, providers and industry should continue to consult with underrepresented groups and existing cultural groups that currently do not have a voice within the industry (eg, ethnically diverse, queer and trans people, disabled people, older people) to ensure that education and skills training provision reflects the whole of Wakefield district in its governance and operation. There is a role for Creative Wakefield in identifying and supporting ‘champions’ going out to solicit and support the development of formal and work-based learning. Those representatives should in turn become ‘ambassadors’ to ensure equitable access to provision and to grow the new cultural leaders from within Wakefield’s communities.
Recommendations

	•	Build on existing strengths within the cultural sector – top-down and bottom up – including:
	•	NPOs – existing joint activity, including COVID response and CRF, but also leverage in the national conversation.
	•	What we learnt from smaller organisations and freelancers’ agile response to COVID. 
	•	Creative Wakefield and the WX3 programme together provide a platform for working collectively and providing resources for artists and freelancers. It offers potential to build on the current mapping with the aim of creating meaningful partnerships, creating opportunities to work together as well as creating and holding the space for networking to happen.
	•	Build on the extraordinary efforts made by the cultural sector during COVID emergency to reinforce its organisations and its activities within communities, the new cultural consortium will provide capacity to sustain that level of engagement and extend it to encompass potential strengths, resources, energy and audiences not currently engaged within Creative Wakefield or other forums – eg, Leeds universities, other West Yorkshire colleges, VCSE.
	•	Offer increased diversity (young people, older people, disabled, d/Deaf and neurodivergent people, LGBTQ+, ethnically diverse and SEB etc). From the survey, the World Cafés and the Stakeholder interviews diversity was referenced by participants throughout the process, and indeed different voices were platformed throughout the process. However, many voices were not. Creative Wakefield has the potential to truly present the intersectional diversity of the district - in particular, barriers imposed by class and poverty - and move beyond instrumentalist representation of diverse characteristics. Brokering relationships with VCSE organisations, the education sector and the culture and heritage sectors, increases access and provides equity for underrepresented groups to have a voice and stake in the cultural life of the city. 
	•	Creative Wakefield provides a mechanism for discovery, development, diversification and growth of a new generation of leaders. Diversification and distribution of cultural power and equity means challenging established orthodoxies and reframing what culture in the city and its neighbourhoods look like. This transition leads to a powerful cultural scene that is predicated on co-design and collaboration and develops a new cultural leadership that has generosity, inclusion and distributed power at its core.
	•	Promote (and establish protocols and data privacy policies for) sharing of knowledge and intelligence amongst existing Council business support services to employer relationships across culture, heritage, VCSE and communities, and skills providers. Tableau tool should be a regularly updated resource shared between providers, employers, policy makers, schools and creative organisations of all kinds. It should be complemented by creation of a resource bank and ‘easy reads’ on skills policy and funding.
	•	Bring WYCA closer to the ambition of Creative Wakefield. The focus on Leeds (as a point of aggregation and attractor of investment for CCDI) and Bradford (with its focus on increasing ethnic diversity and youth engagement within the sector) is both rational in policy terms and a reflection of stretched resources, but Wakefield has a distinctive offer which combines both national centres of excellence and an opportunity to address the disparity in opportunities from more deprived socio-economic backgrounds.
	•	Strengthen employer-provider dialogue on the principle of knowledge exchange means this is about levelling the playing field – not ‘levelling up’. Local artists have an important role in leadership here, creating and communicating a narrative of the sub-region’s cultural and creative strengths and ambition in a way that brings it more frequently and more forcefully to the attention of employers, investors, students, mid-career professionals, arms-length bodies and policy makers in government in a way that results in Wakefield getting not just a larger slice of investment, but the investment it deserves and needs to further its creative and cultural ambitions. 
	•	Skills and opportunities need to be developed in a way that is geographically permeable – removing the perception and reality of a focus on Wakefield city centre and recognising the value of and connections between assets, audiences and communities across Wakefield and the five towns, across the West Yorkshire region, nationally and internationally.
	•	Explore the potential within Cultural Development Fund, Kickstarter and other funds to support placements with CCDI employers – including microbusinesses. The ‘ask’ from the sector within this small consultation is quite aspirational. If it is to happen there will need to be a dedicated resource.
	•	Use Creative Wakefield as a forum to establish industry links to provide mentoring support from larger organisations for smaller organisations and individual artists.
9.1	Further research
We would recommend that the WX3 programme consider investing in additional work in:
	•	Understanding aspirations and barriers at a very local level – individual schools and communities - involves doing the groundwork.
	•	More conversations with the sector as an ongoing strand of work.
	•	Bridging the gap – facilitating and putting in place closer working between Wakefield Council, WYCA, providers and creative sector employers and their representative organisations in implementing and updating the findings of this research.
	•	The use of technology to support collective action and endeavour – including implementing the pathways mapping tool.
	•	Additional longitudinal research to map progression in CCDI from early career onwards.
It would be instructive to revisit the business and workforce data in January 2022 to assess the longer-term effects of Covid on business start-up and survival rates and employment in CCDI in Wakefield.

Appendix 3: Comments from provider survey
3.1	Employer Engagement
Engaging with employers is central to HE Development at the College, in terms of both curriculum design and delivery. A good recent example would be the employer involvement in the design of a new BSc in Computer Science which was a key factor in its successful validation and implementation. We would like to generate more graduate level employment opportunities in Wakefield for our graduates and increase the number of internships and work placements.
We're an incredibly large Faculty (8K students studying subjects as diverse as Fashion design, film, digital media, performance, and music - and much more). We engage with employers in many different ways - our Cultural Institute provides an important point of contact, but we also have University and Faculty employability teams who engage with employers, and we have employability support in many of our 9 schools.
There is a module on each of the two BA(Hons) programmes that I wrote, and which are validated by the University of Hull which requires students to gain industry experience. As a result, various placements are sought and vary year in year out. The two programmes are the BA Creative Industries programme and the BA Performance Industries programme.
We engage with employers in lot of different ways, but it can be hard to do consistently and across each pathway, especially in creative industries.
Insufficient time to engage with employers due to teaching workload.
We engage with employers for works placement, curriculum design and also for live briefs for assessments for the students as we feel that they need to mirror industry to ensure real work situations.
3.2	Placements
We have around 150 Creative and Digital HE students on full-time programmes. Industry / work placement is an integral part of the level 6 Top-up degrees (30 students in 2020-21). Placements for students on level 4 or level 5 programmes are optional.
The course specification does not require placements, with the emphasis on students to secure them.
I couldn't even begin to answer this question - the placement needs of students across the Faculty are so different. Also, we have people working at different levels - we have industry required placements in some schools (journalism) and placements that are optional and available to any student (not just 'creative' students) 
Placements are always being sought on a range of levels. The cohort size on average for the BA programmes is approximately 14 per programme. The level of the industry experience sought is more intense than the lower levels and both programmes focus on the transition from education into the creative industries. Further discussion about the design of the programmes may assist you in moving things forward in building Wakefield's CI.
All our students require placements so that is approximately 450 covering Performing Arts, Music, Media, Design and Photography and Art and fashion. Unfortunately, last  year due to the pandemic we did not achieve any placements as we tend to start after Christmas and try to do them in week long blocks.
We have over 300 learners and less than 100 placements this year. Less last year, before there were more placement officers.
We draw on the extensive links that the College has with hundreds of employers spearheaded by our employer engagements and work placement teams, as well as our strong links with the Council.
We are a new course and have not established placements yet.
This is not at all a simple question - we have very strong relationships with the cultural and creative sector through the cultural institute, strong alumni networks, and lots of investment in employability support. 
Range of placements depending on discipline - further discussion would help with this.
Many placements are sourced from industry working partnerships that the department has due to many staff still working within the industry and having contacts. We also work with the local authority who provided placements for our L3 Media students who looked at doing promotional videos for council leisure facilities. We struggle with music and performing arts as there is only one theatre, so we find ourselves needing to be creative, we also use the Artwalk and the Ridings for our Art student placements which has worked extremely well.
One of the challenges is that as educational institutions, we can't approve students to miss classes and learning without reasonable notice and many placement opportunities (understandably, due to the way the industries work) are almost immediate. So essentially, issues of timing and a disconnect between the needs of employers and of University educators. In a large department, it is difficult for one colleague to respond to all employer requests and University systems are always significantly stretched in this regard. In terms of placements, we frequently find nepotism (placements going to those related to people who already work in the industry). In addition, our students are often offered placements which are well below minimum wage. For example, a student has just been offered a 6 month placement in London and they have offered £300 a month - which wouldn't even cover transport costs. Unpaid or unfairly paid placements are thus only 'available' to students who come from privileged backgrounds.
What works well is placements that are advertised in a timely manner, and who care about developing the placees and pay them a living wage.
They are difficult to find and to support.
3.3	Recruitment
It depends on how much our funding gets cut.
We're looking at our provision as part of an institutional review but it's just not as simple as saying that we are or are not going to increase provision in certain areas. There are plans in place in Fashion. The other thing to remember is that institutions are incredibly different vis creative courses. Our pressures as a Russell Group institution are going to be very different from other parts of the sector locally. 
T Levels and focus for Art and Media, looking more at Media and event technician roles which we currently don't do and also specialist courses in ceramics, jewellery, furniture and fashion.
Recruitment is still high for us, but an ongoing challenge is always ensuring a good balance between critical thinking and practical experience. There is increasing desire for Digital Media programmes. 
3.4	Expanding provision
Creative courses are expensive but their value to society is great. We cut funding to the arts at our peril!
T-levels aren't relevant to us - although I do generally think that it's a retrograde step in terms of admission RG institutions. We currently accept BTEC qualifications, but T-levels are less certain. Obviously, questions about funding in the current climate are live - but as yet not impacting on course development (beyond a general assessment of the 'market' for an individual subject). 
I have worked in further education a long time and there always has been "a bums on seats" mentality. If courses are not financially viable, they won't run. Creative ways have been implemented to combat this - but this is becoming more difficult.
Some courses such as music technology and media require heavy investment to ensure resources and valid, current and industry standard. Because recruitment in the Arts is usually lower numbers it's very difficult to justify the investment with low viability.
Funding is crucial, not just at HE level, but also at School and HE levels, paving the way for teaching and learning. Funding cuts in these areas will continue to have a detrimental effect on the provision that we can over, on the social value of these courses, and ultimately on democracy.
Some courses are expensive to run and funding is tight or not sustainable.
