Executive Summary

This report provides a comprehensive review of Herefordshire's cultural strategy at the midway point of its 2019-2029 implementation timeline. Drawing on stakeholder consultation, skills analysis, and review of progress to date, it offers recommendations to focus strategic efforts for the second half of the implementation period.

Key findings include:

- The cultural sector makes significant contributions to Herefordshire's economy and quality of life, but official data sources underestimate its true size and impact. LinkedIn data suggests the creative workforce is much larger than government statistics capture.  
- Consultation with businesses and practitioners highlighted challenges around lack of affordable creative spaces, patchy skills development pathways, limited public transport access, and need for more cross-sector collaboration.
- Herefordshire has a fairly robust creative skills training offer with 100 courses across five key pathways. However, apprenticeship and T Level options are limited. Major new initiatives at HCA and NMITE have potential to boost future skills development.

- The current strategy has spurred positive activity but suffers from lack of focus, inconsistent communication of impacts, and difficulty balancing targeted actions with broad network building. There are opportunities to better align cultural initiatives with other regional development efforts.

To capitalize on Herefordshire's unique cultural assets and drive progress over the next five years, concerted efforts are needed to address skills and diversity gaps, strengthen talent pipelines, and support the full cultural ecosystem. Priorities include:

1. Foster collaboration between education, industry and civic partners to expand technical training and work-based learning opportunities, with emphasis on inclusion. 

2. Develop a robust communications plan to raise visibility of cultural sector impacts, assets and opportunities both within Herefordshire and externally.

3. Pursue strategic alignment of cultural initiatives with other regional priorities around economic development, placemaking and quality of life. 

4. Invest in cultural infrastructure, spaces and programming county-wide to expand access and participation, fuel innovation and nurture creative enterprises.

5. Empower the cultural partnership to drive ongoing collaboration, track progress against measurable goals, and adapt strategies in Response to data and stakeholder input.

With focused leadership and sustained collaboration, Herefordshire's creative sector can overcome barriers to growth, become a greater source of skills, opportunity and inspiration for all residents, and firmly establish the county as a thriving cultural destination.

Introduction

Herefordshire's 10-year cultural strategy, launched in 2019, established an ambitious vision for the county to become a "culturally vibrant, prosperous and sought after" destination known for its landscape, heritage, creativity and sense of ambition. At the strategy's midpoint, this review assesses progress to date, identifies ongoing challenges and charts a path forward.

The creative industries are a major driver of the UK economy, generating £115.9 billion annually and growing at four times the rate of the broader economy. As emerging technologies transform creative professions, investment in digital skills is a national priority. However, the sector faces significant challenges around diversity, access and sustainability. Only 16% of creative workers are from working class backgrounds.

In Herefordshire, the cultural sector is a source of vibrancy, identity and innovation. However, it must overcome geographic barriers, inconsistent investment and fragmented infrastructure to realize its full potential as an engine of inclusive economic development.

Scope & Methodology

This midpoint review encompasses three core components:

1. Stakeholder consultation to gather input from businesses, practitioners, educators and policymakers on strengths, challenges and priorities for Herefordshire's cultural sector. Methods included a survey, interviews and focus groups.

2. Analysis of creative skills provision in the county, including course offerings, attainment levels, and alignment with employer needs and growth opportunities. 

3. Strategic review of the goals, actions and delivery structures underpinning the cultural strategy as well as broader policy and funding landscape.

The aim is to develop a holistic view of the cultural ecosystem and its interfaces with economic development, education, placemaking and civic life. Insights from each line of inquiry inform a set of strategic recommendations.

Cultural Sector Overview

Herefordshire is home to a rich cultural ecosystem spanning arts, heritage, digital innovation and creative enterprise. However, limited data collection obscures the true size and composition of the sector.

- Official government statistics show 590 creative businesses in the county as of 2021, flat compared to 2019 after gradual growth over the previous decade. IT software and computer services are the largest subsector.

- But these figures, based on VAT and PAYE registrations, miss large numbers of freelancers and micro-enterprises. LinkedIn data surfaced over 4000 creative professionals, suggesting the workforce is 3x larger than DCMS estimates.

- Heritage and arts sectors are especially concentrated in Herefordshire relative to national averages, while creative services like architecture and design are underrepresented.

- Creative and cultural activity ranges from nationally-renowned festivals to hyperlocal heritage initiatives, but lack of coordination makes it difficult to assess collective impacts.

Key assets include The Courtyard arts centre, Hereford College of Arts, NMITE engineering university, a growing cluster of studios and makerspaces, and rural workshop facilities. However, infrastructure is geographically dispersed and of variable quality.

Herefordshire's location -- nestled between dynamic creative economies in the West Midlands, Southwest and Wales -- is both an opportunity and a challenge. While some creatives are drawn to the county's landscapes and lifestyles, others struggle to access clients, collaborators and industry networks.

Stakeholder Consultation Insights

Over 100 stakeholders shared input through surveys, interviews and workshops. Key themes included:

- Affordability: Lack of affordable studio, rehearsal and exhibition spaces is the top challenge, constraining production and audience development.  

- Skills pipelines: Employers struggle to find skilled talent, with 79% citing difficulty filling technical roles. Careers education is limited, work placements are scarce, and post-secondary offerings don't fully align with industry needs.

- Diversity and inclusion: The cultural workforce doesn't reflect Herefordshire's diversity, with cost and geography limiting access. Initiatives to widen creative careers are small-scale and lack coordination.

- Visibility and collaboration: Many feel the cultural sector is fragmented and needs a clearer collective voice. Over 80% want more networking and showcase events to fuel collaboration.  

- Strategic alignment: Stakeholders urge greater integration of culture into economic development, planning and regeneration efforts. Securing investment is difficult without a clear value proposition.

Alongside challenges, bright spots include Hereford's new arts and culture quarter, growing creative clusters in market towns, and emerging digital production facilities. Stakeholders were optimistic about increased local authority engagement and see potential for culture to support wellbeing and quality of life if coordination improves.

Skills Supply & Demand

Herefordshire has a relatively strong creative education offer for its size, with 100 post-16 courses across five career pathways:  

- Creative Originals (40%): art, craft, fashion
- Creative Experiences (30%): performing arts, heritage 
- Creative Content (15%): film, digital media
- Creative Services (7%): design, advertising
- Digital Creative Technology (8%): software, immersive

Hereford College of Arts is the main provider, complemented by NMiTE's new creative computing degrees. Assets like the Performing Arts Hub and Digital Skills Centre provide industry-grade facilities. But apprenticeship options are very limited, with no T Level routes available.

The skills ecosystem has important gaps and pressure points:

- Capacity issues and lack of feeder courses constrain progression into HE, especially from diverse backgrounds. 16-19 arts enrolment declined 29% since 2014 due to funding cuts.

- Graduate retention is a challenge with limited entry-level roles. 56% of creative graduates leave for work elsewhere. Placement and incubation schemes are subscale.

- Existing workforce development is patchy, with 58% of creatives accessing no training in the past year. CPD offerings are scarce outside core craft/tech skills.

- Employers report persistent shortages in areas like digital marketing, UX design, animation, and live event tech. Leadership and entrepreneurship skills are also in demand.

Herefordshire faces stiff competition from neighboring creative hubs in attracting talent. But it has opportunities to pioneer rural creative economy models and cross-cutting skills for emerging markets in areas like agritech, ecotourism and wellbeing.

Strategic Review

Herefordshire's cultural strategy set out 9 goals for 2029 alongside 5 Pledges and 19 actions. At the halfway mark, progress is mixed.

Strengths: 
- Delivery of flagship projects like Hereford's museum and library redevelopment
- Expanded networking through initiatives like Creative Connections and Digital Seminars
- Increased investment in public art and festivals
- Launch of dedicated film office and music development programme

Challenges:
- Securing revenue funding to sustain new infrastructure 
- Inconsistent action plan delivery with limited accountability  
- Weak alignment of culture with broader economic and social policy agendas
- Fragmented leadership and lack of shared vision across stakeholders

The wider policy and funding landscape is challenging, with Arts Council England budgets falling 35% in real terms since 2010. But opportunities exist around Levelling Up, UK Shared Prosperity Fund, and cultural development trusts.

Locally, culture and tourism have been integrated under a unified department in the county council. The new Big Economic Plan identifies creative industries as a growth sector. And the Local Enterprise Partnership has launched a cultural action zone program.

Analysis of comparator regions like Cornwall, Cumbria and Lincolnshire highlights success factors including:
- A compelling cultural narrative tied to economic and social value
- Coordinated investment across infrastructure, skills and business support
- Devolved governance and intermediaries to drive collaboration
- Leveraging natural and cultural heritage assets through regional partnerships 

To accelerate progress, Herefordshire's strategy needs to:  
1. Articulate a bolder, place-based vision balancing distinctiveness and connectivity  
2. Set clear growth targets and monitor consistently
3. Embed culture across economic and social policy, aligning funding and decision-making
4. Develop fit-for-purpose delivery vehicles and strategic partnerships
5. Invest in innovative models at the intersection of culture, high-tech and purpose-driven enterprise

Recommendations & Next Steps  

Herefordshire has strong foundations to build a thriving, inclusive cultural economy. But it needs a gear shift to overcome structural barriers and keep pace with change. Priorities include:

1. Foster collaboration & collective voice
- Establish a cultural compact with shared vision and governance 
- Develop joint advocacy and data capacity to communicate value
- Programme regular convenings and networks around strategic challenges

2. Boost sector sustainability and resilience
- Facilitate access to business support, investment and shared services
- Develop a freelancer and micro-enterprise growth programme  
- Launch a cultural infrastructure investment fund aligned with economic plans

3. Diversify skills and talent pipelines  
- Expand creative careers education and employer encounters
- Incentivise apprenticeships and work placements with a brokerage service
- Pilot creative leadership and "fusion" skills programmes

4. Amplify culture's role in placemaking & social equity
- Embed culture and wellbeing across local development plans and funding bids 
- Launch a cultural partnerships challenge fund to back grassroots initiatives
- Develop a pioneering rural cultural destination plan aligned with regional tourism

5. Experiment at the edges
- Develop a creative innovation hub connecting culture, tech and purpose
- Pilot social investment models like match trading and impact bonds
- Forge a rural creative industries "grand challenge" with HE and industry 

Delivering this agenda will require focus and adaptive leadership. The cultural partnership should evolve into a more empowered intermediary driving collaboration and unlocking resources. Stronger data capacity and shared delivery metrics are essential.

By 2029, we believe Herefordshire can be a beacon of rural cultural innovation - a place that nurtures creative talent, enriches community life, and powers inclusive economic growth. The time to double down is now.
