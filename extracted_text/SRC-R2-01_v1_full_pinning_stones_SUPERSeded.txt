Preface: Two studies, conducted twelve years apart, reveal structural similarities
In the winter of 2012–13, François Matarasso travelled across Aberdeenshire speaking to artists, makers, musicians, community organisers and cultural practitioners. He published his findings in 2014 as The Pinning Stones: Culture and Community in Aberdeenshire. It is a precise and honest assessment of a regional creative culture. Its central argument was structural rather than celebratory: that Aberdeenshire’s cultural life was rich, dispersed, self-reliant, largely invisible to policy, and, like the small stones set between the larger granite blocks of a traditional rubble wall, essential to holding everything together. Remove the pinning stones, he wrote, and the mortar cracks faster. The wall looks fine until it doesn’t.
Twelve years later, this research arrives at a different place by a different route and finds, in several important respects, the same thing. And something more.
The methodology is entirely different. Where Matarasso walked and talked, this analysis counted: LinkedIn profiles, company registrations, four tiers of workforce modelling, gross value added calculations, skills comparisons and place-based counterfactuals. Where Matarasso offered a humanist portrait, this report offers an economic audit. The core finding about scale and visibility is structurally identical to his: the region holds a creative economy that is significantly larger, more economically significant, and more strategically important than any official dataset records, and it is hidden in plain view.
But this research finds something Matarasso’s lens could not have seen in 2014: a second structural asset, visible only to an entirely different set of lenses. The simulation and visualisation firms of the Westhill-Dyce corridor and the creative, marketing, and content agencies of Aberdeen City centre each hold precisely what the other commercially lacks. The gap between them is ten miles and the absence of any institution with the mandate to broker a connection. The analysis does not locate a deficit to be remedied, but locates a trading relationship waiting to be made.
The question is why twelve years of intervening policy, strategy and investment have not closed the visibility gap that Matarasso first documented. The answer to that question is also the answer to the Regional Economic Strategy and Local Outcomes Improvement Plan question the commissioners are now asking.

Part One: What this document is and who it is for
1.1 Scope and purpose
This document presents the findings of a comprehensive assessment of the creative economy of Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire. It is addressed at once to Regional Economic Partnership members and local authority decision-makers; to practitioners and organisations working across the creative, cultural and digital sectors; and to any reader with an interest in how North East Scotland positions itself for sustained economic growth during a period of rapid structural transition.
It draws on original analysis combining official government data, real-time company intelligence and workforce profiling with stakeholder consultation and comparative research. It makes the case for strategic investment grounded in evidence, identifies specific funding opportunities that are available now, and charts the growth pathways available at each level of a creative economy that is larger, more productive and more strategically distinctive than any previous account has recognised.
1.2 Articulating the region’s creative economy
North East Scotland contains three geographically disparate and commercially disconnected creative communities: 
The creative, marketing, and content production agencies in the City of Aberdeen hold the narrative capability, client management experience, and commissioning relationships through which major contracts flow. 
The second community is the one identified by Matarasso’s 2014 study, The Pinning Stones: Culture and Community in Aberdeenshire: distributed and often disconnected creative practitioners from Banff to Fraserburgh to Montrose and across rural Aberdeenshire, 
The third group, a cluster of  digital-technical firms collocated alongside the energy sector in Westhill,  hold world-class technical capability at concentrations far above those of comparable UK cities but have no connection, awareness of or stimulus to connect with the other two groups.
Each is arriving at the limit of what its standalone capability can deliver. Neither currently has a commercial relationship with the other. The separation between them is entirely institutional: a product of geography, sectoral self-identification, and the absence of any mechanism through which a cultural and commercial exchange could be brokered.
This finding sits alongside, and reinforces, the broader finding about scale. The creative economy of North East Scotland accounts for more than 10,700 workers, generates direct gross value added of £582 million, and produces a total annual economic impact of £894 million. Simply acknowledging that there could be greater convergence between those three disparate groups would raise those figures to 12,000 workers, £732 million in GVA and more than £1.1 billion in total economic impact. These figures are not visible in any current policy framework because the measurement tools applied to date were not designed to find them.
1.3 Reframing the strategic question
The tender for this research asked for evidence to demonstrate the position that creative industries should occupy in Regional Economic Strategy. That framing accepts the existing category of creative industries as the unit of analysis. The evidence from both Matarasso’s 2014 analysis and this 2026 research shows that framing is insufficient.
The question the evidence demands is this: what distinctive role can the creative economy of North East Scotland play in sustaining communities through the energy transition, economically, socially, and culturally, in ways that no other region in the United Kingdom is positioned to deliver?
This reframing has four immediate consequences. 
It changes the competitive frame: the region does not need to compete with the Central Belt or London in conventional creative industries terms. What the region can offer, and what no other UK city or region can match, is the combination of industrial-scale simulation capability, authentic energy domain knowledge, and the rural-coastal cultural identity that makes technical storytelling about the energy transition credible to international clients and communities. 
It changes the measurement framework: if the question is about sustaining communities through transition, then gross value added is one measure but the Local Outcomes Improvement Plan outcome domains become equally important. 
It changes the policy architecture, removing a false binary choice between investment in digital sectors and creative industries, and argues that the right approach is a sequenced strategy that addresses different populations at different timescales. 
It calls for a change to current institutional configuration: no existing body is currently configured to provide solutions that respond to the evidence presented in this research, and the answer is a new mandate rather than a new agency.
How this document is organised
Part Two documents how the economy was measured and explains why previous accounts of this economy have understated it. Part Three presents a new four-tier economic model and its aggregate findings. Part Four analyses the convergence opportunity. Part Five gives an overview of skills supply. Part Six correlates data analysis with findings from primary research. Part Seven describes the cultural ecosystem as economic infrastructure, including its role in the city centre. Part Eight documents the structural challenge of the organisational “churn” that results from time-bound public funding. Part Nine sets out five growth strategies. Part Ten presents five strategic recommendations. Appendices provide the full methodology, skills dashboard data, case studies, funding landscape detail, options assessments and policy playbook.

Part Two: Scale and significance of the Creative Industries in North East Scotland
2.1 What this analysis measures, and how
National statistics are designed to count employment within registered companies. The structural and methodological limitations of applying those measures to analysis of the creative industries are well known and documented. In particular, national statistics typically fail to count creative capability that exists outside registered creative sector employers, or technical capability within non-creative sector firms that has a creative origin and a creative application. In a regional economy structured like that of North East Scotland, both of these omissions are significant.
This research applies a methodology that works across three distinct layers of evidence: official employment and company registration data, real-time firmographic classification derived from company website analysis, and LinkedIn workforce profiling treated as a large-scale occupational survey. These are reconciled through a sequence of analytical steps designed to avoid both double-counting and systematic exclusion. The result is a more complete structural picture than official statistics alone can provide, identifying firms and workers not captured by previous analyses. The differences in scale and composition that follow reflect what becomes visible when the frame of analysis is widened to match the actual shape of the economy being studied. It also reflects Throsby and Bakhshi’s recommendations that analysis should reflect three different but complementary types of value: economic value (gross value added, employment, exports); non-market cultural value (cultural goods’ intrinsic and social worth); and symbolic and heritage value (longer-run identity and heritage impacts).
2.2 Official baseline
Standard approaches to measuring creative industries rely on two official data sources: the Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES), which counts formally employed workers in registered businesses; and the Inter-Departmental Business Register (IDBR), which records companies by their declared Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code, the system used to categorise businesses by their primary economic activity at the point of registration. Both systematically undercount the creative economy in Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire.
Employees
BRES data for Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire, filtered against creative industries SIC codes and adjusted to remove general IT consultancy, produces a count of 4,965 full and part-time employees. 
Of that total, the IT consultancy sub-sector (SIC 62.02) accounts for between 1,400 and 1,600 full and part-time jobs and an estimated £150 million in gross value added. Detailed analysis of sector intelligence and stakeholder consultation confirm that this activity functions primarily as enterprise support for the whole economy, in particular for the energy sector. This part of the workforce is already recognised in Opportunity North East’s mapping of the digital sector in North East Scotland; including them in our assessment of Creative Industries would inflate the creative economy data while misrepresenting its character. As the comparison in Table 1 shows, it has also been a volatile employment sector in recent years; for those reason, and to avoid double counting, we exclude this section of the workforce from the resulting total of around 3,300 employees who form the basis of our assessment of the strategic creative core (see Part Three, below).

2.3 Real-time data sources
Real-time workforce data
BRES data forms the floor of the workforce analysis: the population whose economic activity is formally registered, reliably counted and analytically secure. It captures the region’s publicly funded cultural institutions, its registered design and architecture practices, and its other commercial creative firms; but it misses the large population of self-employed and freelance practitioners who are neither employed by a firm nor registered as an employer. In a sector where over 90% of registered creative enterprises employ fewer than ten people, and where many practitioners operate through portfolio working, part-time activity and informal contracts, this is a methodological shortcoming rather than a marginal omission. 
We address this through analysis of LinkedIn profiles, treating the platform as a large pre-filled workforce survey rather than a social media tool, to identify more than 8,000 unique profiles of residents of North East Scotland working in Creative Industries, with a further 4,000 IT services and IT consultancy occupations. Excluding the IT services and consultancy component, comparing LinkedIn data with BRES provides evidence of more than 1,500 additional employees and 3,280 self-employed and freelance workers not captured in official employment statistics.

Real-time companies’ data
IDBR measurement of business populations suffers from similar limitations to those of BRES. We used The Data CityTM Industry Engine to validate data from IDBR, and to distinguish active regional operations from holding companies, dormant registrations, and businesses registered in the region but operating primarily elsewhere.
The Data City platform analyses company website content and other proprietary data sources to assign Real-Time Industrial Classifications to companies: descriptions of what firms actually do rather than how they are officially classified. This reveals a differently configured population of active creative businesses from that shown in official statistics, particularly in the Createch sub-sector (creative technology: firms applying creative methods to digital and technological products and services). All company identifications were subject to human validation to confirm operational presence in the region and to exclude subsidiaries with no substantive local operation.
Analysis of the Industry Engine provided evidence of over 1,700 companies registered under at least one of the DCMS Creative Industries SIC codes with either a registered office or commercial activity undertaken in North East Scotland; further analysis and validation using The Data City’s Real Team Industry Classifications and manual validation of the company’s activities reduced this to list of around 1,200 active companies, from which a further 280 companies were excluded as being primarily computer consultancy businesses. (A detailed description of the methodology used can be found at Appendix B, below).

A four-tier model was constructed to reflect the actual structure of the regional creative economy rather than the categories imposed by official classification. Each tier is defined by its relationship to formal employment statistics, by its economic character, and by its function in the wider ecology.

3.2 Tier 1: Institutional anchors
Identified through BRES and confirmed against Creative Scotland funding records, Tier 1 comprises 65 organisations, including public library sites, museum and gallery sites and  performing arts anchor organisations and venues in Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire. It accounts for some 1,180 workers (equivalent to 800 full-time roles, reflecting the high incidence of part-time, casual and sessional working common in event-led cultural organisations). 
The Creative Scotland Multi-Year Funding portfolio, confirmed for 2025 to 2028 with awards to Aberdeen Performing Arts (£1.69 million), Peacock and The Worm (£1 million), Citymoves Dance Agency (£450,000), Sound Festival (£350,000), North East Arts Touring (£400,000) and The Barn (£500,000), provides the structural security that has distinguished these organisations from those that have not survived the past decade. The correlation between multi-year structural funding and institutional durability is explored in more detail in Part Seven.
3.3 Tier 2: SME commercial core
Identified primarily through BRES and augmented by Data City real-time classification analysis, particularly in the Createch, video production and digital agency sectors, Tier 2 as a whole comprises registered businesses in architecture, design, advertising, creative software, film, television, music, visual and performing arts and related fields. It accounts for some 4,340 workers (3,950 FTE, reflecting high full-time stability).
Two specific sub-sectors drive the high performance of this tier:
Architecture and Built Environment: Employing approximately 400 staff with high full-time intensity (0.91 FTE), this sector is a historical strength of the region, supported by ongoing urban regeneration and legacy demand from the oil and gas sector and new opportunities in capital projects for the renewable energy sector.
CreaTech: Excluding general IT support from our analysis allows us to more clearly observe a robust cluster of high-value creative software, film/TV/video and gaming firms remains. This sub-sector functions as the region's productivity engine, delivering GVA per head markedly higher than the creative sector average.
3.4 Tier 3: Visible freelancers
Identified through verified LinkedIn data, Tier 3 comprises 3,240 independent professionals (2,430 FTE) describing themselves as freelance, self-employed or contractors, working across advertising, marketing, film, television and video production, games development, photography, publishing and Createch. 
As well as filmmakers and others creating original IP, there is a cohort of freelance professionals offering marketing and design services on digital platforms to B2B clients across the region. This tier provides a critical mass of flexible, skilled practitioners, whose presence allows commercial creative SMEs and Tier 1 cultural anchor institutions to rapidly scale capacity to meet short-term project opportunities without increasing overheads.
In a sector characterised by short-term commissions and variable demand, a deep and visible freelance pool is a structural competitive advantage. 
3.5 Tier 4: The invisible workforce
Tier 4 is inferred from penetration gap analysis: the difference between the estimated total population of creative practitioners in low-digital sub-sectors and the proportion identifiable through LinkedIn and official data. Independent musicians, artists, writers and craft makers show LinkedIn penetration rates estimated at 30% to 45%, against over 80% for technology and design professionals. The estimate of 1,855 individuals (835 FTE) is derived from this gap, using workspace density modelling, open studio participation data, trade body membership analysis,. 
The Tier 4 population spans three distinct registers. At one end are practitioners for whom creative work is a primary professional activity generating substantial commercial revenue: internationally trading makers, working artists with established gallery and retail relationships, musicians with professional performance income. At the other end are those for whom creative work is a serious sustained vocation rather than a primary income source. Between them are the portfolio workers for whom creative practice is one strand of a mixed economy. All three segments are invisible to BRES, to IDBR, and to LinkedIn analysis, but together they are a significant source of employment in the regional economy. The FTE intensity of 0.45 applied to this tier reflects the wide range of activities within Tier 4: is an average of those in full-time work and the greater prevalence of pat-time and portfolio working. A further 180 FTE of “shared utility” employment in hospitality and events is attributed to this tier using the same methodology, giving a combined total of 2,035, and 860 FTE. (Shared utility demonstrates a lower FTE intensity of 15% - full methodology for calculating FTE intensity, derived from the Liverpool Music Futures cluster mapping study, can be found at Appendix B.)
Tier 4's significance is cultural, social, and foundational to the tier structure above it. These are the practitioners who carry the craft and making traditions - silversmithing, textiles, applied arts - that represent some of the region's most distinctive cultural strengths, sustain the community infrastructure that makes the region worth living in, and supply the creative imagination that the commercial tiers draw on. They are also the population at greatest risk from the structural fragility described in Part Eight, and the population whose proximity to Tier 2 and Tier 3 practice, through shared tools, shared spaces and informal skill exchange, defines one of the most significant and least recognised growth opportunities in the regional economy.
This tier of the creative economy generates millions of pounds from retail sales of original work, staging shows and exhibitions, providing training and other service activities from a practitioner population that BRES records as zero, generated at near-zero public cost when given minimum institutional conditions. Practitioners include internationally trading makers with sales through the Goldsmiths Fair and US markets, and a documented graduate retention pathway running from RGU's Gray's School of Art into Edit and DAST studio tenancy: a pipeline that costs the public sector nothing and currently has no policy protection. A full case study, including the concentric circles structure of the Tier 4 ecosystem from DAST membership through North East Open Studios to the wider rural and coastal dispersal of individual practitioners, is at Appendix D.
3.6 Economic impact
Gross value added estimates are derived from ONS regional output data apportioned to SIC codes on the basis of official employment, benchmarked against sector averages from Data City company records and DCMS economic estimates, and adjusted for the full-time equivalent intensity that characterises each tier’s employment structure. Total economic impact is calculated by applying Type II multipliers to each tier’s direct gross value added figure. Type II multipliers, as defined by the Scottish Government, include direct, indirect and induced effects, measuring the total economic impact of a change in final demand for an industry’s output.
Estimates of direct GVA have been calculated using a combination of:
Analysis of ONS data on regional output at Division level, apportioned to SIC codes representing the Creative Industries on the basis of official employment data
Sector averages for GVA per capita benchmarked against evidence from companies’ records parsed via The Data City and DCMS Economic Estimates for the Creative Industries
Calculation of FTE intensity reflected in the differing employment structures which characterise each of the four tiers of strategic creative core sectors

Together the four tiers account for a total creative workforce of more than 10,500 people, generating direct gross value added of £582 million and a total annual economic impact of £894 million. 49% of this workforce works independently, as freelancers, sole traders or micro-enterprise operators invisible to standard employment counts. Type II Multipliers
Type II multipliers, as defined by the Scottish Government, include direct, indirect and induced effects, measuring the total economic impact of a change in final demand for an industry's output and accounting for how increased spending ripples through the economy, affecting suppliers and household income."
For example, a multiplier of 1.40 means that for every £1.00 of direct GVA generated by the sector, an additional £0.40 is generated in the wider regional economy through these knock-on effects."
The three components are:
Direct effects -the GVA generated by the sector itself: the output of the creative firms, freelancers and practitioners measured in the four-tier model.
Indirect effects -the GVA generated in the supply chain as creative firms and workers spend on inputs: rent, equipment, software, professional services, print, materials. This is what distinguishes Tier 2's multiplier of 1.60 from the others -its extensive local supply chain relationships mean more of its indirect spend stays in the regional economy rather than leaking out.
Induced effects -the GVA generated as employees and self-employed workers spend their earnings in the local economy: housing, food, retail, leisure. This is the primary driver of Tier 3's multiplier of 1.40 despite lower supply chain depth -freelancers tend to spend higher proportions of income locally than employees of geographically dispersed large firms.
The weighted average multiplier of 1.53 across the four tiers is slightly above the Scottish Government's published average of 1.48 for the creative industries sector. The methodology document (Appendix B) notes this reflects the concentration of high-value technical and commercial roles in Tier 2 whose supply chain relationships and professional salary levels generate above-average indirect and induced effects, partially offset by the conservative 1.20 applied to Tier 4.

Part Four: The convergence opportunity
4.1 Spatial and sectoral dimensions of the Creative Industries in North East Scotland
The standard creative industries cluster model, as described in the Creative Industries Policy & Evidence Centre (PEC)’s Creative Clusters research (Newcastle University, December 2023) and operationalised in the Creative Industries Clusters Programme, assumes a geographic concentration of creative firms that generates agglomeration benefits: shared labour markets, knowledge spillovers, supplier networks, and the kind of serendipitous collaboration that happens when creative practitioners are physically proximate to one another.
By that standard, North East Scotland does not have a creative cluster. It has a dispersed population of creative firms spread across a geography that runs from Fraserburgh in the north to Montrose in the south, from the Aberdeen city centre to rural Aberdeenshire. The density required for conventional cluster dynamics is absent. It is important to address this honestly rather than seek to paper over the gaps with language about “clusters” which does not fit the evidence. 
The region is home to three spatial and sectoral groupings that are not clusters in the sense expressed by the PEC but that have cluster-like characteristics that provide opportunities for future growth.
The city centre creative core: a concentration of small and micro-businesses in advertising, PR, design, architecture and screen production within the Aberdeen city centre and inner suburbs (AB10, AB11, AB15, AB24 postcodes). This is the closest Aberdeen has to a conventional creative cluster, and it is the population most likely to benefit from shared workspace, networking infrastructure and cluster development investment.
The rural and coastal cultural base: reflecting Matarasso’s observations, our analysis identifies active creative practitioners from Banff to Fraserburgh to Montrose and across rural Aberdeenshire. Whilst there are some important hubs - Deemouth Artist Studios in Torry, Scottish Sculpture Workshop in Lumsden, The Barn at Banchory, Deveron Projects (Huntly), and North East Open Studios, which collectively represent confirmed active organisations across the city-rural spectrum, each with documented community-facing programmes - most of this group are individual artists and makers operating separately from the creative commercial core, mostly outside of registered business structures and without access to “formal” business support. These structural dynamics mean that the  economic contribution is invisible in every conventional dataset. They represent a distributed creative economy that requires a different kind is policy response - connectivity, peer networks, mobile support - rather than the place-based investment that suits the city centre cluster model. 
The third, and arguably most distinctive group is a concentration of more than 200 digital technology companies in the AB21/AB32 postcode corridor running from Dyce to Westhill. This Westhill-Dyce corridor reflects the geography of Aberdeen's energy services sector and contains the firms most likely to develop creative-technical hybrid capabilities relevant to both the energy transition and the growing CreaTech sector. It is not a creative cluster; it is a technology cluster with creative potential.
4.2 The Westhill-Dyce corridor
Some 250 firms in the Westhill-Dyce corridor, identified through real-time industrial classification and human validation, constitute a convergence tier whose existence was not analytically visible before this research. Over decades of safety-critical offshore operations, these energy services and engineering firms have developed deep capability in simulation, three-dimensional visualisation, digital twin development and immersive training: technical disciplines directly contiguous with the creative technology sector. Under standard SIC classification, none appears in creative industries counts. Their combined estimated direct gross value added of £137 million, and the strategic significance of the simulation skills surplus they represent, are invisible in every previous assessment of the regional creative economy.
Simulation, in this context, means the use of real-time three-dimensional computational models to replicate physical systems and processes for industrial training, operational planning and asset management.
The corridor firms give no sign of shared identity, hold no collective voice, and have no relationship with the organisations through which the creative economy conversation in the region happens. Their absence from all four stakeholder workshops, despite direct outreach, is the most direct empirical evidence available of this separation. Tier 2 therefore contains two distinct sub-populations that are geographically and institutionally separated but economically adjacent: the simulation, visualisation and digital-technical firms of the Westhill-Dyce corridor, and the creative, marketing, design and content agencies of Aberdeen City centre. Neither currently has a commercial relationship with the other.
The consequences run in both directions. Aberdeen City's agencies - firms including BIG Partnership, Fifth Ring, ThinkPR and Korero- hold established energy sector client relationships and narrative capability, but lack the technical depth to produce immersive assets, data visualisations and technically credible simulations within a single commission. The corridor firms hold exactly that technical depth but have no route to the content and communications markets where it would command a premium. If market overlap between the two communities can be demonstrated, the case for a structured connectivity programme becomes substantially stronger, and the suggestion from consultation participants that transition communications contracts are going to agencies from Edinburgh and London moves from stakeholder observation to evidenced claim. What the current analysis establishes is the precondition: both capability sets exist in the region, the market for their combination is real and growing, and no mechanism currently exists to bring them together. That is precisely the gap that further rounds of CoSTAR funding and the Creative Industries Cluster programme are designed to address.
4.3 Skills analysis: what each community holds
To characterise the capability profile of the two communities, this research compared LinkedIn skills data for North East Scotland against Nottinghamshire, normalised per capita against working-age population and validated against BRES sector employment figures. Nottinghamshire was selected on the basis of comparable city-region population, similar distance from the dominant national creative economy cluster, and data availability. The comparison measures distinctive concentrations and relative absences -not deficits against a target.
The pattern is structurally inverse between the two communities. North East Scotland has 2,298 simulation-skilled practitioners, representing 6.6 times the per capita concentration of the Nottinghamshire comparator. Against that surplus it shows a per capita deficit of 3.8 times in virtual production and 2.8 times in data storytelling: revealing a community that is at once technically deep and commercially constrained in the content and narrative markets where that technical depth would command a premium.
The city-centre creative community shows the mirror image. Narrative, design and marketing skills are present at rates broadly comparable with Nottingham. Simulation, visualisation and immersive production skills are substantially absent. These firms hold the client relationships and the creative strategy capability; they lack the technical substrate to produce evidentially grounded outputs in high-value energy stakeholder and community engagement work.
These are not regional deficits. Describing North East Scotland as lacking virtual production or data storytelling capability conflates two communities with entirely distinct profiles. The region holds both sets of skills, in separated locations, in a configuration that is commercially constraining for each and commercially transformative if connected. The skills gap is not between North East Scotland and other regions.; it is between two parts of the same regional economy that have never had a mechanism for exchange.
That gap has direct commercial implications beyond the energy transition. City-centre agencies used to standard service models in graphic design and conventional video production cannot move up the value chain into digital twin development, safety simulation, or high-fidelity immersive content without access to the technical capability sitting ten miles away. Corridor firms cannot diversify their client base into screen, games, or public-facing content markets without the narrative, production and commissioning skills the city-centre community holds. Low levels of graduate retention (only 17% of creative graduates currently remain in the region) are partly a consequence of this disconnect: a graduate who can see a clear professional pathway between creative practice and opportunities in the energy transition economy has a reason to stay that is currently invisible to them at the point of graduation.
4.4 Five convergence points
The skills profile analysis establishes that the capability asymmetry between the two communities is structural and quantifiable. The following five convergence points establish that creative-industrial crossover is not a future aspiration: it is already occurring at firm and practitioner level across the region, without policy support and without institutional recognition. Each point represents a site where the combined argument can be seen in operation.
Animation and engineering visualisation
Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire have a documented sector of animation and technical visualisation studios serving the energy industry. Signal Film, Perspective, Survitec Viscom, and related firms produce three-dimensional animations and graphics for subsea engineering, safety training, and renewable energy communications. Their work sits at the intersection of creative practice and industrial application and represents the convergence argument made visible at firm level. Aberdeen Animates, established in 2025 as a community animation event with roots in the engineering visualisation community, provides evidence of audience demand and an emerging practitioner identity in the city.
Landscape and site-specific practice and energy transition community engagement
As offshore wind, hydrogen infrastructure and transmission lines are built across Aberdeenshire's coast and farmland, demand is growing for cultural practitioners who can work with communities on the meaning and visual transformation of that landscape. Community engagement requirements for major energy infrastructure are statutory and are currently met almost entirely by consultation professionals. The skills of landscape and site-specific practitioners -landscape literacy, site-specific practice, community co-production -are directly applicable to the heritage interpretation and engagement work large infrastructure projects are required to fund. Scottish Sculpture Workshop's Creative Climate Futures strand is the institutional anchor; the economic claim is limited to the statutory consultation and community engagement market.
Craft practice and digital fabrication
The silversmithing, textiles and applied arts traditions of North East Scotland represent internationally recognised cultural strengths. Digital fabrication tools -precision CNC machining, laser cutting, additive manufacturing -are standard in engineering and manufacturing contexts across the region. The convergence between craft knowledge and digital fabrication is already occurring informally in shared workspaces and through individual practitioners who move between traditions. Formalising those crossings through recognised pathways and shared infrastructure is simultaneously an economic and a cultural policy objective, with direct relevance to the Tier 4 to Tier 2 commercial pathway described in Strategy 3.
Immersive performance and industrial simulation
The game engine platforms -primarily Unreal Engine and Unity -used by corridor firms for industrial simulation are the same platforms that underpin immersive performance and location-based entertainment. The technical substrate is shared; the professional cultures and market contexts are entirely distinct. The convergence point is the potential for immersive performance practitioners to access the corridor's technical depth, and for corridor firms to access the narrative and spatial design expertise of performance practice. No institutional bridge currently exists between these communities, making this the convergence point most dependent on the connectivity infrastructure that Strategies 1 and 2 propose.
Sound practice and environmental communication
Sound Festival has a documented track record of commissioning work that engages audiences with the material reality of environmental change, working with composers, sound artists and electroacoustic practitioners to produce place-rooted responses to landscape transformation. These skills -affective, audience-facing, environmentally grounded -are directly relevant to the community engagement challenges the energy transition requires and are not currently connected to the statutory consultation and communication market. Sound Festival is a Tier 1 organisation with demonstrated adjacency to the transition agenda; this convergence point is currently cultural rather than commercial, and requires the market development work proposed in Recommendation 5 before it becomes investable.

5.1 The regional education pipeline
North East Scotland produces a substantial volume of creative and digital talent. Robert Gordon University is the primary supplier, with 19.1% of its total student population enrolled in creative or digital disciplines against 5.6% at the University of Aberdeen. The pipeline is weighted toward technical disciplines, particularly Computing and IT (1,765 students, 48% of the total), reflecting the region's industrial character and the historic demand from the energy sector for technically literate graduates. RGU's BSc Computing and Creative Design and BSc Games Design represent deliberate attempts to develop creative-technical hybrid curricula; both feed directly into articulation pathways from NESCol's HND portfolio, which provides well-developed provision in games design, software development, web development, television production and sound production. The question is not whether the pipeline produces creative talent but whether the local economy currently provides enough that is visible to graduates at the point of graduation to retain them.

Mapping the location of alumni who studied creative courses at the two universities reveals a structural failure to retain the talent the pipeline produces.

Of the 1,527 mapped RGU and UoA creative alumni, 709 (46%) are working in North East Scotland. University of Aberdeen creative graduates are now more likely to be employed in Glasgow or Edinburgh (193) than in the region (179). RGU retains 36% of its mapped creative alumni locally; the equivalent figure for the University of Aberdeen is 23%. The departure pattern is not random. Graduates leave for the Central Belt above all other destinations, which indicates that the primary competition is not London or international markets but Edinburgh and Glasgow, where the combination of a larger creative economy, more visible entry-level roles, and more obvious professional networks provides a compelling alternative to staying. London takes a smaller share (183 combined), suggesting that the pipeline is feeding Scotland's Central Belt creative economy at least as much as it is feeding London.
This is not a quality failure. The graduates who leave are doing so rationally, in response to a local economy that does not yet generate sufficient visible entry-level employment to give them a professional reason to stay. The structural condition is absorptive capacity: the creative economy, until the corrections documented in Part Three of this report, appeared smaller than it is, and the convergence tier - which would provide the most compelling creative-technical employment proposition for technically capable graduates - has had no shared identity, no recruitment presence, and no relationship with the universities that supply the talent it needs.
5.3 The skills capability profile: what the region holds and what it lacks
The graduate pipeline analysis describes the supply side: what the region's education institutions are producing and where it is going. The LinkedIn skills dashboard, that compares the North East Scotland workforce against Nottinghamshire county (population 824,800) as a per-capita comparator, describes the demand side: what skills the regional workforce currently holds and where the gaps are relative to a comparable city-region.
The two pictures together tell a more precise story than either tells alone, and the story is structural rather than simply quantitative.
What the region holds in abundance
The regional workforce holds a per-capita concentration of simulation skills 6.6 times the Nottinghamshire comparator: 2,298 practitioners against 576 across a city-region of comparable scope. This surplus is not the product of the education pipeline. It is the accumulated result of fifty years of safety-critical offshore operations, in which simulation-based training for drilling, well control and subsea systems became standard industrial practice. The same pattern appears in two adjacent disciplines: production management, where the region holds a 6.0 times per-capita surplus (3,528 against 972), and VFX, where the surplus is 5.8 times (869 against 247). Procedural workflows - the node-based process automation used in both industrial simulation and animation - show a 4.5 times per-capita surplus (535 against 197).
These surpluses share a common origin. They are the technical disciplines that the offshore energy industry required and systematically developed across its workforce. They are also, without exception, the disciplines that underpin the convergence economy: simulation is the substrate of immersive industrial training; VFX and procedural workflows are the creative disciplines most directly contiguous with that substrate; production management is the coordination capability that allows large-scale simulation and visualisation projects to be delivered. The region has not accumulated these skills through creative economy investment. It has accumulated them through industrial investment, and they are now sitting at the intersection of two economies that have not yet found each other.
What the region lacks
The deficits in the dashboard are as structurally significant as the surpluses, and they are a different kind of gap. Virtual production -the film and television production method combining real-time game-engine rendering with physical camera work - shows a per-capita deficit of 3.8 times (25 practitioners against 159). Data storytelling - the ability to translate complex datasets and technical information into compelling visual and narrative form -shows a per-capita deficit of 2.8 times (377 against 1,725). Technical AI and data analysis as applied to creative production shows a deficit of 2.5 times (329 against 1,360). Real-time rendering for screen and content production shows a deficit of 2.2 times (351 against 1,265).
These are precisely the skills that a well-functioning creative education pipeline would produce -and that the graduate destination analysis shows the regional pipeline is producing and then losing. Virtual production, data storytelling, and AI-enhanced creative workflows are the disciplines taught in design, media and creative technology programmes of the kind that RGU and NESCol offer. The graduates who hold or develop those skills are the same graduates who are leaving for Glasgow, Edinburgh and London.
The structural picture is therefore not a skills shortage in the conventional sense. It is a skills distribution failure: the region produces technical practitioners with industrial simulation depth but without creative production breadth, and it produces creative graduates with narrative and content capability but without the technical substrate to deploy it in the highest-value markets. The dashboard deficits are concentrated in the skills that bridge those two populations -virtual production, real-time rendering, data storytelling -and those bridging skills are the ones the region's graduates are most likely to develop and most likely to take elsewhere.

The skills gap the dashboard cannot show
There is a third gap that neither the dashboard nor the destination analysis captures directly, and it is the gap identified most clearly in the Appendix C skills assessment: business, enterprise and freelance skills embedded in creative and technical training. The Skills Development Scotland sectoral assessment and the Creative Industries Policy and Evidence Centre report both identify this as a structural absence in the national curriculum; the North East Scotland pipeline reflects and amplifies it. With 49% of the regional creative workforce operating independently as freelancers, sole traders or micro-enterprise operators, the absence of systematic business capability development in creative and technical programmes has direct economic consequences. Graduates who leave to work in larger creative economies find the business and commercial infrastructure they need in the environment rather than having to create it themselves; graduates who stay face that challenge without the preparation to meet it.
5.4 What the skills analysis means for strategy
The skills picture described in this section defines both the urgency and the tractability of the five strategies in Part Seven.
The simulation surplus is the region's primary competitive asset. It is not the product of recent investment; it is the accumulated depth of an industrial workforce. What it lacks is the narrative and creative production layer that would allow it to reach the content, communications and public-facing markets where its value would be highest. That layer is present in the region - in the city-centre agencies, in the creative graduate pipeline, in the Tier 4 practitioner community - but it is not connected to the technical depth that would make it distinctive.
The virtual production and data storytelling deficits are tractable. They are the natural outputs of the kind of creative-technical curriculum that the region's institutions are partially already delivering. Closing them requires not new institutions but curriculum development, structured placement pathways, and the short-course and CPD provision that the skills assessment at Appendix C identifies as currently absent. The CoSTAR bid and the Aberdeen-Dundee Creative Technology Corridor provide the mechanism; the Appendix C recommendations provide the curriculum framework.
The graduate retention problem will not be solved by improving the quality of the pipeline. Graduates who leave do so because the local economy does not yet show them the professional future they are looking for. Making the convergence economy visible - through the connectivity programme in Strategy 1, through the Creative Career Bridge in Strategy 4, and through the consistent NES Creative Economy Narrative in Strategy 5 - is the precondition for retention, not an optional complement to it. A graduate who can see a clear pathway from a creative-technical degree into the convergence economy has a reason to stay that is currently invisible to them at the point of graduation.



Part Six: Aligning data analysis and primary research
Qualitative and quantitative validation was conducted through industry consultation, and benchmarking and counterfactual analysis of workforce and graduate destinations data. Interviews with stakeholders from policy, education, cultural and creative sector organisations took place between October 2025 and April 2026. Four in-person workshops held in Aberdeen in November 2025 brought together practitioners, anchor organisations, local authority officers, and representatives of the national agencies.
The primary research reveals a strong correlation with quantitative findings on skills and scale from the desk-based research. It underlines the fact that the sense of fragility expressed by practitioners is not a perception issue; it is a structural reality confirmed by workforce data. The sector is heavily weighted towards freelance and micro-enterprise activity and lacks the deep commercial middle ground found in competitor regions.
Mapping specific consultation responses against the economic data explains why opportunities to diversify the regional economy are currently being missed and identifies specific areas for intervention.
5.1 Building commercial capacity of creative SMEs 
Our analysis of the economic baseline identifies Tier 2 as a potential productivity engine, comprising design, marketing, and screen agencies. However, the skills benchmarking exercise reveals a specific deficit in data storytelling capability.
Consultation responses suggest this skills gap limits the commercial standing of these agencies. Participants described being treated as transactional providers, where, 
"The relationship stops at ‘deliver the brief’ rather than co-creating value" (AAMD_044).
This limitation is structurally reinforced by the silo separating creative agencies from the region's significant technology sector. Software and simulation businesses generally did not participate in the consultation, reflecting a disconnect where high-value technical creativity sits within industrial supply chains while creative execution remains isolated in arts and marketing.
Because these sectors do not interact, Tier 2 SMEs and microbusinesses struggle to develop high-value, technology-led products of their own. Instead of developing independent intellectual property (IP) in digital twinning or simulation, they remain reliant on service contracts from legacy clients. Being aware of and equipped with the skills to bridge this gap enables agencies to acquire the technical capability to build their own scalable products and export services to new markets – including the global renewable energy and environmental sectors which are drivers of the just transition – reducing the overdependence locally based oil majors.
6.2 Addressing freelance precarity and retention 
The Scale and Significance analysis estimates a freelance workforce of approximately 2,370 individuals. While this group offers agility, Skills Supply data indicates that early and mid-career creative professionals are exiting the region due to a lack of sustainable mid-tier opportunities.
Testimony from this tier highlights the cost of this precarity, with practitioners describing the sector as "hidden, hungry, struggling and undervalued" (AAMD_002). In screen and media, the lack of continuous production work creates a retention crisis: 
"Finding experienced freelance crew locally is hard; people either move away for work or burn out" (SNE_009).
This tier currently bears the greatest risk. The region possesses a surplus of engineering simulation skills (>2,000 workforce) but a deficit in the application of those skills for creative virtual production. If this freelance tier can harness simulation tools currently utilised for engineering for wider creative and B2B applications, they can decouple their earning potential from the volatility of the local economy. Equipping freelancers with virtual production skills and greater awareness of production workflows across different creative sub-sectors would enable them to generate high-end content for global media markets, including B2B and B2C applications in gaming, advertising, and VFX, creating a resilient income stream that does not link their continuing ability to find contract work to the fortunes of the North Sea energy sector.
6.3 Convergence and divergence of sector needs
Divergence in support needs 
Cultural practitioners (particularly those in tiers 1 and 3, but also the “invisible workforce” that constitutes tier 4) explicitly request financial stabilisation and infrastructure. They cite "fragile" spaces run by volunteers (ABSH_008) and funding systems where,
"As soon as the grant ends the whole thing collapses" (ABSH_007). 
Conversely, commercial creative firms (Tier 2) rarely request subsidy. Their frustration is regulatory and reputational, noting that the "aspirational branding of the city" does not match the reality of doing business (ACI_032).
This divergence indicates that a single mode of engagement and intervention directed to these differing viewpoints is at high risk of failing to address the needs of either. The cultural sector requires investment in physical and organisational stability, whereas the commercial creative sector requires a shift in how the region is positioned to international markets.
Convergence on regional identity 
Across all tiers, there is unanimity that the region’s external narrative acts as an economic brake. The dominance of the narrative regarding the decline of North Sea oil obscures creative potential: 
"The loudest story about Aberdeen is still that it’s an oil town past its peak" (AAMD_001).
Whether in screen industries design, or advertising and marketing, practitioners agree on the opportunity to take ownership of the transition narrative. They articulate a role for the region as a place to,
"Develop stories about climate, transition and future industries" (SNE_048) 
from a community and creative perspective, rather than leaving this story to be told by corporate PR departments.

Part Seven: Cultural ecosystem as economic infrastructure
7.1 What the tier model does not capture
The four-tier economic model captures formal economic activity. It does not fully capture the cultural ecosystem within which that activity is embedded and on which it depends. Three distinct formations of that ecosystem map onto the tier structure in specific ways. Understanding their relationship to the economic tiers is a precondition for understanding why the convergence opportunity is sustainable, and why the structural challenge described in Part Seven is, if unaddressed, fatal to it.
7.2 The heritage substrate
The deepest formation is the heritage substrate: four millennia of built and landscape heritage, a concentration of prehistoric monuments found nowhere else in Europe, a literary and musical tradition carried in the Doric language, the bothy ballad corpus and the Greig-Duncan folk song collection. None of this appears in firm counts or gross value added calculations. All of it constitutes a significant draw for creative practitioners to come to and remain in North East Scotland, the reason visitors come, and the distinctive regional identity that differentiates North East cultural production from its competitors. The heritage substrate underpins the entire tier structure: through the museums, libraries and heritage institutions of Tier 1, it is held, interpreted and made accessible; through the independent artists, makers and musicians of Tier 4, it is carried forward as living practice.
7.3 The established cultural formation
The professional anchor organisations confirmed as substantially intact by a 2025 review of sector asset records constitute the established cultural formation. Scottish Sculpture Workshop, Deveron Projects, The Barn, Sound Festival, Haddo Arts, North East Arts Touring, Peacock Visual Arts, Aberdeen Performing Arts and North East Open Studios have survived and in most cases grown since 2014. The common characteristic of these organisations is multi-year structural funding through Creative Scotland’s Multi-Year Funding portfolio. The 34% uplift committed in the first year of the new awards, rising to 54% from the second year onwards, signals that the funder recognises the gap between standstill funding and genuine sustainability. The post-2028 landscape includes a Development Fund pathway designed to bring organisations toward full portfolio membership from 2026 to 2027, and the Aberdeen City Council Catalyst Fund, designed as a bridge toward Core Operating Support by 2029.
7.4 The emergent formation
The emergent formation comprises organisations established since 2014, primarily in response to structural gaps identified but not resolved by the Pinning Stones audit: Look Again, Culture Aberdeen, the Freelance Independents North East network, Creative Aberdeenshire Network, Aberdeen Animates and Creative Aberdeen. This stratum performs the connective, skills development, audience development and sector intelligence functions that the commercial economy needs but that no established institution is currently resourced to provide.
The membership of this tier spans a range that official statistics flatten into invisibility. Speaking to Peter Bowman as Deemouth Art Studios (DAST) in Torry and Sally Reaper at RGU added colour to the spectrum: at one end are practitioners for whom creative work is a primary professional activity generating substantial commercial revenue: Emma Louise Wilson, a raised enamel jeweller based in Torry, sells through the Goldsmiths Fair in London, the Mad About Jewellery fair in the United States, and has been featured in Vanity Fair. Megan Falconer, a DAST graduate who now runs her own studio and teaches, produces bespoke commission work and sells through the Balmoral gift shop. At the other end are practitioners for whom creative work forms one element of a portfolio life, and others for whom it is a serious sustained vocation rather than a primary income source. All three segments contribute to the Tier 4 economy; all three are invisible to every framework currently used to measure it.
The ecosystem within which DAST and Edit operate extends well beyond their immediate membership. The North East Open Studios network, which has  consistently engaged more than 300 artists and makers across the region, provides the connectivity infrastructure between more than 2,000 rural and urban practitioners within Tier 4, that Matarasso identified as the distinguishing characteristic of the Aberdeenshire creative ecology. The relationship between DAST membership, NEOS participation, and the wider rural and coastal dispersal of individual practitioners forms the concentric circles structure within which the Tier 4 estimate is grounded: the registered and LinkedIn-visible business base captures only the innermost ring. It documents commercial activity at the centre to the dispersed community practitioners at the edge, whose contribution to cultural life is real and whose contribution to economic life is systematically undercounted.
Current support is fragile and time-limited across the board. Aberdeen City Council's Culture Investment Programme allocates approximately £1.5 million across 15 partner organisations annually through a competitive process that provides none of the structural security multi-year funding confers. FINE, formed in 2023 and formalised in 2025, received £63,000 from the UK Shared Prosperity Fund, a programme with no confirmed successor. Aberdeenshire Council's Cultural Tides initiative was similarly UKSPF-dependent. Aberdeen Animates was formed without public funding and has no structural income. Full funding detail is at Appendix A. The emergent formation is at a familiar point in the churn cycle.
7.5 Cultural funding deficit
Aberdeenshire's historic underclaim on national funding reflects the same self-reliance and distaste for reporting requirements that Matarasso identified in 2014: the region received £13.12 per head in National Lottery arts grants against a Scottish average of £58.57, not because the work was absent but because practitioners did not feel entitled to ask. No programme in the intervening twelve years has addressed the underlying cultural disposition that produces this pattern. Closing the differential requires more than additional resource. It requires the institutional will to tell a different story about what this economy is and what it is worth, in terms that practitioners across all four tiers can recognise as their own; the function of the Joint Board proposed in Recommendation 4.
7.6 The city centre as Tier 4 infrastructure
The relationship between Tier 4 practitioners and Aberdeen’s city centre retail environment is not only a cultural question. It is a planning, property and regeneration question with documented economic dimensions. Edit Aberdeen’s presence in the Bon Accord Centre, supported by RGU’s Gray’s School of Art under provisions for charitable occupiers of otherwise empty commercial space, has contributed to a measurable improvement in the centre’s perceived appeal.
Aberdeen City Council’s City Centre Manager confirmed in April 2026 that Bon Accord’s pitch to new retail tenants explicitly references Edit and the Curated Market as differentiating factors, and that at least one incoming brand, Sostrene Grené, viewed the centre’s direction of travel favourably in part because of what these cultural uses were bringing. Whilst we do not claim this as causal evidence of influence on leasing decision,  it is established that cultural activation at Edit and Curated Market level is a recognised component of the centre’s commercial positioning, contributing to a perception of improvement that has been absent since the loss of John Lewis. The Bon Accord position is consistent with the retail geography evidence that anchor tenants and distinctive independent retailers generate positive agglomeration effects for surrounding commercial space (Wrigley and Lowe, 2002).
The School Hill stretch, running between Bon Accord and Robert Gordon’s College, is identified as having the worst vacancy rate in the city centre and as the priority location where cultural activation could make a material difference. Its architectural character, proximity to the university and the art gallery, and adjacency to an emerging hospitality cluster suggest potential for a coherent creative quarter if the vacancy problem is addressed with that ambition.
Aberdeen City Council is developing a pop-up activation kit: pre-furnished, ready-to-occupy retail spaces. It is currently promoted through Aberdeen Inspired and business gateway channels but is not yet systematically connected to creative organisations. That connection is the practical ask. The CIC model on which Edit operates also occupies a grey area in current rates relief policy. The specific request from practitioners is for institutional clarity: that a CIC operating as creative market infrastructure be recognised as a supportable, institutionally protected entity in planning, licensing and retail tenancy frameworks, with consistent and transparent rates relief that does not require annual renegotiation. This is achievable at no additional cost to the council.

Part Eight: Structural challenges
8.1 Graduate retention and the “brain drain”
Only 17% of graduates from North East Scotland’s higher education institutions remain employed in the region after completing their studies. RGU loses a volume of graduates equivalent to 83% of its local creative workforce to other UK regions. University of Aberdeen creative graduates are more likely to be working in Glasgow or Edinburgh than in North East Scotland. The local economy has not developed sufficient absorption capacity for the graduate pipeline it produces.
A graduate who understands both digital production tools and the energy transition narrative is more valuable to a North East Scotland convergence firm or a city-centre agency pitching on energy transition than to a generic London creative agency. The Creative Career Bridge programme proposed in Strategy 4 is designed to make that connection institutional and visible. The convergence economy, if developed, creates precisely the entry points that make staying worth considering for graduating practitioners.
8.2 The “churn” cycle
A regional creative economy of this scale and depth should be generating institutional infrastructure commensurate with its vitality. The evidence shows that it repeatedly does, and that this infrastructure repeatedly fails to survive the conditions of its formation.
GANE, the audience development network recorded in the 2014 Pinning Stones audit, is gone. Creative Cultures, the creative industries directory of the same period, is gone. Design in Action, the most substantial creative economy research and development programme the region has generated, ended in 2016 when its Research Councils UK funding ended and has no institutional successor. The pattern reflects a structural condition: the creative economy of North East Scotland has never had sufficient structural funding to sustain the connective infrastructure it generates. Practitioners and organisations respond rationally by building what they can on the funding available, knowing it will end.
8.3 Current emergent formation is at risk
FINE, Look Again, Creative Aberdeenshire Network, Aberdeen Animates and Creative Aberdeen are each, at different points, on a trajectory that the historical pattern makes legible. The contrast with the established formation is instructive. The seven organisations now in Creative Scotland’s Multi-Year Funding portfolio have developed, through the structural security that portfolio membership provides, into institutions of regional and national significance. The emergent formation organisations doing the most strategically important work today lack equivalent security. The difference is the presence or absence of a multi-year funding relationship.
8.4 Invisible costs of the “churn” cycle
Each episode of formation, operation and dissolution represents a minimum of three to five years of practitioner time and public investment that cannot be reclaimed. The networks built, the trust established, and the knowledge accumulated do not transfer to successor organisations that must build from scratch. Ken Hay of Culture Aberdeen described the structural consequence to the Scottish Parliament’s Culture Committee: organisations are “spending more and more money on chasing money, which is slightly absurd.” This is a description of what rational organisations do when structural security is unavailable and project funding is the only alternative. The opportunity cost is real: Look Again, the Freelance Independents North East network, Creative Aberdeenshire Network and Aberdeen Animates are performing, on precarious resource, exactly the connective and sector intelligence functions that the convergence economy strategies in Part Eight require.
8.5 What breaks the cycle
The Creative Scotland Multi-Year Funding model has, over decades, provided exactly the structural security that breaks the cycle for the organisations within it. The architecture for the period to 2028 is confirmed. The post-2028 landscape includes a Development Fund pathway designed to bring organisations toward full portfolio membership from 2026 to 2027, and the Aberdeen City Council Catalyst Fund designed as a bridge toward Core Operating Support by 2029. The question for the emergent formation is whether its most strategically important organisations can be positioned to access these routes before the next episode of dissolution. The window is the next twelve to twenty-four months.
Access to these routes should be a stated strategic objective for Look Again, the Freelance Independents North East network, and Creative Aberdeenshire Network. Aberdeen City Council’s Culture Investment Programme allocates approximately £1.5 million across 15 partner organisations in 2025 to 2026, with its Catalyst Fund and Acorn Fund providing a local bridge for organisations not yet eligible for national structural funding. Aberdeenshire Council’s UK Shared Prosperity Fund allocation for cultural activity illustrates both the value and the structural risk of time-limited project funding; capital pipeline development should be directed toward Heritage Lottery Fund and Levelling Up successor mechanisms.
8.6 Live strategic opportunities
CoSTAR and the AHRC Creative Industries Clusters programme
Confirmation of CoSTAR satellite funding from Innovate UK is expected in June 2026. An outline application to the Arts and Humanities Research Council Creative Industries Clusters programme is under active preparation by RGU and the University of Aberdeen. The £100 million UKRI investment in the next wave of Creative Clusters, including a £27 million opportunity opened on 5 January 2026, is the most significant immediate funding opportunity available to the region. North East Scotland’s case rests on a distinct typology: a convergence economy in which creative-origin technical skills have been adopted and scaled by an industrial sector, generating a simulation surplus at 6.6 times the per capita concentration of comparable UK city-regions and an identified pipeline of creative-industrial research and development activity across five convergence points. Both local authorities should be formal co-applicants alongside the universities.
Screen Scotland and the regional screen development model
Comparators from (England’s) North East Screen Industries Partnership, the Liverpool City Region Production Fund and the Yorkshire Content Fund provide a clear template for the Screen North East investment case. A regional film office and production fund at the scale of comparable UK regional models addresses the virtual production skills deficit in the city-centre creative community directly and builds the crew base needed to attract returning productions and develop local intellectual property.
Place-based capital and regeneration funding
The capital pipeline for community creative hubs, including the Portsoy and Banff developments identified in the NES Culture Connects cluster proposals, should be developed into investment-ready business cases aligned with current UK and Scottish Government place-based funding rounds. The Creative Growth Prospectus, identified in Programme 4 of the Action Plan, is the appropriate vehicle.



Part Nine: Five growth strategies
The four-tier analysis establishes what this economy is and what it contains. This part sets out what needs to happen for it to grow. Five strategies are proposed, each addressing one or more tiers, each requiring one or more of the governance and investment decisions set out in the recommendations in Part Nine.
The strategies are sequenced: 
Strategies 1 and 2 create the commercial architecture and the mutual capability that make the convergence opportunity actionable. 
Strategy 3 protects the cultural infrastructure without which the upper tiers lose their roots. 
Strategy 4 addresses the graduate pipeline, which is simultaneously a talent problem and a retention problem and is only solvable if the economy that graduates are being retained into is worth staying for. 
Strategy 5 is the condition of possibility for all four: without a coherent external narrative that positions North East Scotland's creative economy as a strategic asset rather than a cultural amenity, none of the other strategies will attract the sustained institutional attention they need.
The relationship between the strategies and the recommendations is direct. Strategies 1 and 2 are enabled by Recommendation 3 (the CoSTAR bid) and Recommendation 5 (the energy transition programme). Strategy 3 is enabled by Recommendation 2 (the structural fund). Strategy 4 is enabled by Recommendations 1 and 5. Strategy 5 is enabled by Recommendation 4 (the Joint Board), which provides the cross-boundary mandate without which no consistent narrative can be maintained.
9.1 Strategy 1: Connect the two communities commercially
Tiers addressed: Tier 2 (both sub-populations). Primary policy frame: RES. Enabled by: Recommendations 3 and 5.
The gap. The 250 convergent firms of the Westhill-Dyce corridor have no shared identity, no collective voice, and no relationship with the creative industries support infrastructure. They do not recognise their combined value (estimated GVA of £188 million), nor the wider significance of their comparative advantage in simulation and visualisation skills. They cannot access Scottish Enterprise international development programmes, Creative Industries Trade and Investment Board trade missions, or CoSTAR corridor funding, not because they are ineligible, but because they do not know they qualify. The city-centre agencies face the opposite constraint: they hold the client relationships and the creative strategy capability, but lack the technical substrate that would allow them to compete credibly for commissions that require immersive asset production, data visualisation, or technically credible simulation. The gap is ten miles wide and entirely relational.
The opportunity. Both communities already exist. The technical capability is already there. The client relationships are already there. The energy transition is already creating demand, both documented and emerging, for precisely the combination that the two communities together represent. No capital investment is required to create what already exists; investment is required only to make it visible and connected.
The intervention. A Convergence Economy Identification and Connectivity Programme, designed as a structured engagement process, brings the corridor firms into a named network with a shared identity within the regional economic narrative. It connects them to the market development support they currently cannot access, and it creates the conditions for hybrid commercial bids: consortium-style project structures in which a city-centre agency leads on creative strategy and client management while drawing on corridor capabilities for technical production, immersive asset development, and data visualisation. This model is not experimental; it is established in architecture and engineering. The connectivity programme must run in both directions: city-centre agencies should be co-designed participants from the outset, not an afterthought. The primary output measure for the first phase is three joint commercial relationships established between corridor firms and city-centre agencies, using the energy transition communication and community engagement market as the testbed.
Timeline: six to eighteen months for first phase. Low capital cost. High strategic return.
9.2 Strategy 2: Build mutual intelligibility
Tiers addressed: Tier 2 (both sub-populations), Tier 3. Primary policy frame: RES and LOIP shared. Enabled by: Recommendations 3 and 5.
The gap. The commercial structures that Strategy 1 creates require practitioners on both sides to be able to work together. They currently cannot, not because goodwill is absent, but because professional vocabulary, workflow assumptions, and sector identity are entirely distinct. A corridor engineer and a city-centre copywriter could be in the same room and have no productive conversation about a joint commission. That is not a talent failure. It is an infrastructure failure.
The opportunity. The skills dashboard analysis identifies the precise shape of what each community lacks. The corridor firms have simulation-skilled practitioners but lack data storytelling and virtual production skills: they are technically deep but narratively constrained. The city-centre agencies show the inverse pattern: narrative, design, and marketing skills at broadly comparable rates, simulation and immersive production skills substantially absent. The gap is defined and the curriculum is therefore definable.
The intervention. Short-cycle continuing professional development programmes at RGU and NESCol, each running in both directions simultaneously. Corridor-facing: data storytelling, virtual production workflow, and technical communication - the narrative layer that simulation-skilled practitioners need to reach content and communications markets. Agency-facing: energy systems literacy, simulation technology fundamentals, and the technical vocabulary of digital twins and immersive asset production - not to make copywriters into engineers, but to make city-centre practitioners credible interlocutors for the corridor firms they are attempting to bring into joint bids. Cohorts should be mixed from the outset: the professional relationships that commercial collaboration requires should begin forming during training, not after it. This lays the tracks for a formal Aberdeen-Dundee Creative Technology Corridor, established as an industry partnership node with CoSTAR and Abertay, providing the institutional spine, enabling the simulation workforce of North East Scotland to access virtual production training while creative technology graduates from North East Scotland and Tayside access international energy sector and renewables market opportunities.
Timeline: partnership establishment six to twelve months; first CPD cohort twelve to twenty-four months; first co-production commission eighteen to thirty-six months.
9.3 Strategy 3: Protect and develop the cultural sector
Tiers addressed: Tier 1, Tier 4. Primary policy frame: LOIP primary, RES enabling. Enabled by: Recommendation 2.
The gap. Matarasso identified in 2014 that the independent artists, makers, musicians and community practitioners of Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire were systematically overlooked by policymakers focused on the larger structural blocks of the economy. That condition is unchanged. The emergent formation organisations doing the most strategically important connective work, including Look Again, FINE, Creative Aberdeenshire Network, Aberdeen Animates, are each at a familiar point in the churn cycle. The Tier 4 practitioner population, estimated at 1,855 individuals generating £27 million in direct gross value added from a base that BRES records as zero, operates without institutional recognition, without protected workspace, and without the policy clarity that would allow community interest companies to function as stable market infrastructure. The useful precedent provided by RGU Gray's School of Art and Edit graduate retention pathway - from student brief to commercial practice to studio tenancy - currently exists outside every institutional record and has no policy protection.
The opportunity: The Tier 4 evidence is now documented and evidenced in ways that Matarasso's 2014 account could not be. Edit Aberdeen's £100,000 retail sales figure, the named practitioners with international commercial reach, the Curated Market expansion, the Editions graduate pathway, and the agglomeration effect that the cultural offer has in mainstream retail environments are documented economic activity generated at near-zero public cost. The institutional conditions that produced it - CIC status, university charitable backing, available retail space - are replicable. The ask from practitioners is modest and precise: recognition, not subsidy.
The intervention combines four actions, each achievable within existing frameworks and at low or zero cost.
Recognise the community interest company operating as creative market infrastructure as a legitimate, protected economic model in planning, licensing and retail tenancy frameworks. CIC rate relief should be made consistent and transparent, removing the need for annual renegotiation that currently threatens the sustainability of models that are demonstrably working.
Formalise the Gray's School of Art and Edit relationship as a recorded graduate retention mechanism within RGU's institutional outcomes, reporting and within the Regional Economic Partnership's skills pipeline monitoring. Extend the model to NESCol to encourage other artists’ groups. This costs nothing, but closes a measurement gap and creates the institutional visibility that makes the model replicable.
Extend the Creative Neighbourhoods framework explicitly to rural Aberdeenshire, with connectivity, peer networks and mobile support as the primary investment. The Banff and Portsoy hub investments in the Place-Based Spine option are the right physical anchors; the connective tissue between them and the dispersed rural practitioner population is the missing element. The School Hill corridor in Aberdeen city centre, identified as having the worst vacancy rate in the city and the strongest case for cultural activation, is the priority urban location.
Commission a Creative Wellbeing Economy pilot explicitly as preventive health infrastructure rather than arts programming, generating the longitudinal LOIP wellbeing data that currently does not exist for this population and connecting the cultural economy to the health outcome domains in which both councils hold policy commitments.
9.4 Strategy 4: Reframe the graduate pipeline as a transition asset
Tiers addressed: Tier 3, Tier 2. Primary policy frame: RES and LOIP shared. Enabled by: Recommendations 1 and 5.
The gap: RGU loses creative graduates equivalent to 83% of its local creative workforce to other UK regions. University of Aberdeen creative graduates are more likely to be working in Glasgow or Edinburgh than in North East Scotland. This is not a quality failure. It is a structural one: the local economy has not generated sufficient mid-tier employment to give graduates a visible professional reason to stay. A graduate who understands both digital production tools and the energy transition narrative is more valuable to a North East Scotland convergence firm or a city-centre agency pitching on energy transition than to a generic London creative agency. Once it is visible, connected, and supported, the creative economy this report describes is precisely the environment that makes staying worth considering. The graduate retention problem is not separable from the other four strategies; it is their output measure.
The opportunity: the energy transition creates entry points that did not exist five years ago. A graduate who can visualise offshore wind systems, communicate decommissioning to communities, or produce technically credible simulation content is sought after in a market that is creating demand faster than it is creating supply. Framing those opportunities explicitly, and connecting graduates to them through structured pathways before they leave, is the intervention the data identifies.
The intervention: a Creative Career Bridge programme: a twelve-month paid placement pathway placing creative graduates with convergence tier firms and with city-centre agencies developing their energy transition offer, funded by a combination of Scottish Enterprise, RES, and employer contribution. The graduate gets a foothold in the local economy; the sponsor firm gets the storytelling and content creation skills it currently lacks. The placement is the mechanism through which the mutual intelligibility Strategy 2 develops becomes professionally embedded.
The parallel intervention is an Energy Transition Creative Pathways pilot targeting mid-career energy sector workers facing redundancy, mapping skills transfer potential from industrial technical roles to creative practice, and testing the DAST and Edit model as a market access mechanism for practitioners entering the commercial creative economy from an industrial background. This connects the RES employment transition agenda to the LOIP outcome on economic participation and creates a measurable outcome in the number of energy sector workers transitioning to creative employment that no current programme tracks.
Timeline: programme design and employer partnership development year 1; first cohort year 2.
9.5 Strategy 5: Tell a different story
Tiers addressed: All tiers. Primary policy frame: RES and LOIP shared. Enabled by: Recommendation 4.
The gap: a creative economy of more than 10,500 people generating £582 million in direct GVA, rising to 8,825 people and £466 million when the convergence tier is included, does not feature in how North East Scotland presents itself to investors, to graduates, to potential creative residents, or to its own practitioners. The region's most important competitive asset, a concentration of simulation and visualisation skills at 6.6 times the per capita rate of comparable UK city-regions, generated through fifty years of safety-critical offshore operations and now sitting at the intersection of industrial heritage and creative future, is not mentioned in the Regional Economic Strategy. The loudest story about Aberdeen is still that it is an oil town past its peak. Every other strategy suggested here depends on replacing that story with one that is more accurate, more useful, and already supported by the evidence assembled here.
The opportunity: there is evidence to support the NES creative economy narrative but the message is not reaching either the creative sector or its clients; it needs to be sold. Three claims, each evidenced in this analysis, each distinctive to this region and not replicable by any other UK city or region, should be deployed consistently across every investment prospectus, every skills engagement, and every national funding conversation.
The simulation surplus is the lead claim for every Regional Economic Strategy investment prospectus and every Scottish Government creative industries conversation. A concentration of simulation skills at 6.6 times the per capita rate of comparable UK city-regions, built through fifty years of offshore operations and now powering five identified convergence points across animation, landscape practice, sound, immersive performance, and craft, is an economic asset of national strategic significance.
The DAST and Edit model is the lead case study for every Local Outcomes Improvement Plan submission and every creative economy funding application. A retail till receipt for £100,000 from a practitioner population that BRES records as zero is the proof of concept for a policy model: that the invisible economy generates measurable economic value at near-zero public cost when given the minimum institutional conditions to do so.
The energy transition is the region's distinctive creative economy proposition. No other UK city or region can offer the combination of industrial simulation capacity, authentic energy domain knowledge, and the rural-coastal cultural identity that makes North East Scotland's creative economy credible and legible, technically, narratively, and geographically, to the global clients the transition is already creating.
The intervention: a NES Creative Economy Narrative, constituted as a formal policy commitment rather than a branding exercise, maintained by the Joint Board recommended in Recommendation 4 and deployed consistently across all five strategy areas and all five recommendations. The narrative is the frame within which every other strategy operates. Without it, measurement is data. With it, measurement is evidence of a distinctive economy worth investing in.
Note on sequencing and the recommendations
None of these five strategies is self-executing; each requires one or more of the governance and investment decisions set out in the recommendations in Part Nine. The Joint Board (Recommendation 4) is the precondition for maintaining the narrative across institutional boundaries. The structural fund (Recommendation 2) is the precondition for keeping the cultural actors in place long enough for Strategy 3 to take effect. The CoSTAR bid (Recommendation 3) is the mechanism through which Strategies 1 and 2 are resourced at the scale they require. The measurement framework (Recommendation 1) is what converts the outputs of all five strategies from anecdotal successes into an evidenced case for continued investment. The energy transition programme (Recommendation 5) is what gives Strategies 1, 2 and 4 a named market to test themselves against.
The strategies describe what needs to happen. The recommendations describe what the Regional Economic Partnership needs to decide to make it possible.

Part Ten: Five Strategic Recommendations
Recommendation 1: Commit to full-spectrum measurement (RES and LOIP)
Current frameworks capture, at best, the upper two tiers of the creative economy. A shared measurement framework, covering all four tiers, tracking institutional durability alongside firm count and gross value added, and updated at three-year intervals, is the foundation for every other recommendation. The NES Culture Data Hub, identified in both cluster development options and in Programme 4 of the Action Plan, is the appropriate vehicle. Governance should be joint between Aberdeen City Council and Aberdeenshire Council, with formal reporting into both the Regional Economic Strategy and Local Outcomes Improvement Plan partnership structures.
The first-year measurement programme should treat two questions as Priority 1 research: whether the markets served by Aberdeen City’s creative agencies and the Westhill-Dyce corridor firms overlap, and whether city-centre agencies have identified energy transition as a growth market. The answers to these questions determine the strength of the case for the connectivity programme in Strategy 1 and the market development programme in Recommendation 5.
Implementation lead: both local authorities jointly. Indicative cost: £100,000 for development and first iteration. Timescale: year 1.
Recommendation 2: Break the churn cycle with structural support (LOIP primary, RES enabling)
A Creative Ecology Structural Fund, jointly governed by both local authorities with Creative Scotland and Scottish Enterprise, should provide multi-year revenue funding to emergent formation organisations whose work is strategically critical and whose current resource base is precarious. Minimum funding periods of three years, with a clear pathway toward Creative Scotland Multi-Year Funding status or demonstrated income diversification as exit conditions, would provide the structural security the anchor formation holds and the emergent formation lacks. Immediate candidates are Look Again, the Freelance Independents North East network, Creative Aberdeenshire Network and Aberdeen Animates. The Creative Scotland Development Fund pathway and the Aberdeen City Council Catalyst Fund are the primary routes where organisations are eligible.
Implementation leads: Aberdeen City Council, Aberdeenshire Council, Creative Scotland. Indicative cost: £150,000 to £300,000 per year for a cohort of four to six organisations. Timescale: years 1 to 2.
Recommendation 3: Advance a joint CoSTAR bid (RES primary)
Both local authorities, RGU, the University of Aberdeen and Scottish Enterprise should formalise participation as co-applicants in the UKRI Creative Clusters bid, in advance of the Innovate UK CoSTAR satellite funding confirmation expected in June 2026. The bid should be built on the simulation surplus as its primary competitive differentiator, supported by evidence from Signal Film, Perspective, Survitec Viscom, Sound Festival, Scottish Sculpture Workshop and the industrial simulation cluster across all five convergence points. The bid should position North East Scotland as a distinct typology: a convergence economy in which creative-origin technical skills have been adopted and scaled by an industrial sector, generating a pipeline of creative-industrial research and development activity that no existing urban cluster is positioned to replicate. Formal demand-side validation, including market evidence from industrial partners, is identified as a priority for the bid development process.
Implementation leads: RGU and University of Aberdeen, with both local authorities and Scottish Enterprise as co-applicants. Timescale: outline stage active now; full application to follow June 2026 confirmation.
Recommendation 4: Resolve the City and Shire structural fault line (RES and LOIP)
A Joint Cultural and Creative Economy Board, with a shared budget line, dedicated officer resource and a formal mandate covering measurement, structural funding, the UKRI bid, and advocacy to national bodies, is the minimum governance infrastructure required to make the other recommendations actionable. The existing Creative Industries Working Group provides a natural foundation. It requires formal decision-making authority and dedicated capacity.
As identified directly in consultation, the sector is currently reliant on people’s extra time and motivation to perform the facilitation and alignment functions that other sectors receive dedicated investment to support. Experience from comparable regional bodies in Glasgow City Region, Yorkshire and the West Midlands suggests a minimum coordination resource of one full-time equivalent supported by a programme budget of £50,000 to £100,000 per year. The joint board proposed here is a framework: it requires the officer time and programme budget needed to make it effective.
Implementation leads: Aberdeen City Council and Aberdeenshire Council jointly. Timescale: year 1.
Recommendation 5: Align the creative economy explicitly with energy transition (RES and LOIP)
Creative technology skills, including real-time three-dimensional visualisation, simulation, immersive media and digital twin development, should sit within the energy transition skills strategy. A Creative Communication for Energy Transition programme should pair cultural and creative organisations with the community engagement, environmental communication and place-based narrative challenges the transition requires. Actions under Programme 3 of the Action Plan, An Outstanding Natural Environment, including the Creative Climate Futures strand and Scottish Sculpture Workshop’s land and ecology research, should be framed explicitly within the transition agenda and funded accordingly.
The programme should include four specific actions: developing and publishing the NES Creative Economy Narrative; creating a formal Aberdeen-Dundee Creative Technology Corridor partnership with CoSTAR and Abertay; establishing the Creative Career Bridge placement programme; and commissioning a market development programme with Aberdeen City’s creative and marketing agency community, working alongside the Joint Creative Economy Board, to develop the joint commercial propositions and consortium structures that would allow the region’s agencies to win energy transition communication contracts that currently go to agencies in other cities. The programme should produce a named pipeline of energy transition communication opportunities open for tender within the next twenty-four months, mapped against the combined capability of city-centre agencies and corridor firms working in partnership.
Implementation leads: Joint Board, Energy Transition Zone, Opportunity North East. Indicative cost: £200,000 to £400,000 over three years. Timescale: years 2 to 4.
A note on governance and capacity
Effective intervention must account for where sub-sectors agree and where their needs conflict. Previous strategic reviews of the creative sector in North East Scotland have recommended that existing bodies align their activities rather than proposing new governance structures. That instinct is reasonable. It has not resolved the underlying problem, which is that none of the existing bodies holds the mandate, the resource, or the cross-boundary authority to act as the sector’s strategic champion. Whatever governance model is adopted, it must carry sufficient dedicated resource to act.

Conclusion: The wall, the stones, and the missing mortar
François Matarasso titled his 2014 essay after the small stones set between the larger granite blocks of Aberdeenshire’s traditional rubble walls. His argument was that these pinning stones, individually modest and collectively essential, were at risk of being overlooked by policymakers focused on the big structural blocks of the economy. Remove them, and the mortar cracks faster than anyone expects.
This research has found those pinning stones still in place, twelve years on, now quantified. It has also found something Matarasso’s lens could not have seen in 2014: a second structural asset, invisible to an entirely different set of frameworks, in the form of the  convergence firms and simulation-skilled workers sitting at the intersection of the region’s industrial heritage and its creative future.
Both assets require the same fundamental intervention: the connective tissue, the mortar, that allows what already exists to function as a coherent system. In Matarasso’s terms, that mortar is community: the networks, shared spaces, informal relationships and mutual recognition that hold a dispersed creative economy together. In the terms of this research, that mortar is institutional and commercial: the cross-agency mandate, the shared measurement framework, the named connectivity roles, and the joint commercial structures that would allow the region’s convergence tier and its pinning stone practitioners to find each other, support each other, and present themselves credibly to the markets and investors that the energy transition is already creating.
Direct gross value added of £582 million, rising to £894 million in total economic impact, with a workforce of over 10,500 people of whom 49% work independently: adding in the convergence tier raises that workforce to over 12,000, direct GVA to 3732 million and total economic impact to over £1.1 billion.
These are not the figures of a peripheral cultural amenity; they are evidence of an economy with the scale to justify serious strategic investment, the distinctiveness to make a credible case for national and international funding, and the structural fragility, at the freelance and emergent formation levels, that makes investment urgent.
What has been building in North East Scotland, through oil booms and downturns and through decades of under-investment, has not been eroded. It has been consolidating, in plain view, in three separate communities whose capabilities are complementary and whose commercial constraints are mutually soluble. The analysis does not locate a gap to be filled; it locates a connection waiting to be made.

Appendix A: The Regional Creative Funding Landscape, 2020 to 2031
A.1 Purpose and scope
This appendix documents the funding architecture that supports the creative economy of Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire: the national public investment flowing through Creative Scotland's Multi-Year Funding programme, the local authority investment from Aberdeen City Council's Culture Investment Programme and Aberdeenshire Council's cultural support activity, and the time-limited UK Shared Prosperity Fund allocations that have sustained several of the region's most vulnerable organisations through the transition period. It then maps the funding outlook to 2031, identifying the structural gaps and decision points that the recommendations in Part Nine are designed to address.
The period from 2020 to 2026 has been one of the most turbulent in the recent history of public arts funding in Scotland. Emergency Covid funding, standstill budgets, a controversial 10% cut to Creative Scotland in 2023, restoration and then substantial uplift -the cumulative effect on regional organisations has been a cycle of crisis planning, partial recovery, and renewed uncertainty that has consumed the management capacity of precisely those organisations the sector most depends on to function. The new architecture that emerges from April 2025 is, by any measure, a significant improvement. It is also, in several of its structural features, incomplete: the post-2028 landscape contains real risks for the region that this report's recommendations are designed to mitigate.
A.2 Continuing instability
The period opened with the pandemic emergency. Creative Scotland distributed approximately £17 million in emergency Covid funding across Scotland between 2020 and 2022; the North East Scotland beneficiaries included Aberdeen Performing Arts, Peacock Visual Arts, Citymoves Dance Agency, Sound Festival, The Barn and the Scottish Sculpture Workshop, as well as a significant number of individual practitioners through the Open Fund for Individuals. These emergency funds maintained organisations that would otherwise have closed during the period of enforced closure, but they were not a substitute for the core operating support that those organisations had previously relied upon, and they accelerated a process of reserve depletion that continued to affect balance sheets well into 2023 and 2024.
The regular funding round that should have provided stability was repeatedly delayed. Creative Scotland had last made new regular funding commitments in 2018; the organisations in the North East Scotland portfolio, Aberdeen Performing Arts, Citymoves, Sound Festival, Peacock Visual Arts and The Barn among them, were operating on extensions of agreements from that round, with no new multi-year certainty, throughout the period from 2020 to 2025. Aberdeen Performing Arts ran deficits of approximately £360,000 in 2022/23 and £355,000 in 2023/24, with income falling to £12.3 million against expenditure of £12.6 million. The Creative Scotland element of its income was £333,000 in 2023/24, a figure that reflected standstill or reduced grant rather than any increase in real terms. Aberdeen City Council's contribution of approximately £1.1 million in the same year partially compensated, but both income streams were below the levels required for the organisation to break even against rising costs.
The 2023 budget cut - Creative Scotland's grant-in-aid was reduced by 10%, some £6.6 million, in 2023/24- landed on organisations already weakened by the pandemic period. Creative Scotland's overall budget was broadly static over the preceding decade and the cut represented a real-terms contraction in support at the moment of greatest need. Its reversal in February 2023 and subsequent restoration were welcome, but the management time consumed by uncertainty across that period, and the impact on multi-year planning, staffing decisions and programme development, represents a structural cost to the regional creative ecosystem that is not captured in any funding figure.
The UKSPF period from 2022 to 2025 provided partial mitigation for organisations outside the Creative Scotland portfolio. Freelance Independents North East (FINE) received £63,000, Aberdeenshire Council's Cultural Tides programme received £170,126 in 2024/25, and a number of Aberdeenshire-based creative practitioners and micro-organisations received smaller awards. These were significant sums to the organisations receiving them but time-limited by the nature of the fund: there is no confirmed UK Government successor to UKSPF, and the creative sector allocations within the Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire UKSPF programmes had no pathway to continuation.

A.5 Aberdeenshire Council: cultural support and the UKSPF transition

A.7 The post-2028 architecture: risks and gaps
The MYF programme runs to March 2028. What happens after March 2028 is, as of April 2026, not determined. The decisions that Creative Scotland and the Scottish Government make in 2026/27 about the next funding round will shape the regional creative ecosystem for the decade to 2031 and beyond. Several structural risks are visible from the current position.
The MYF renewal question
The MYF programme has no automatic renewal mechanism. The current programme replaced the Regular Funding for Organisations programme, which had itself experienced a seven-year gap between rounds. Creative Scotland must distribute Scottish Government funding within the financial year but can carry over National Lottery funding to future years; the Board has chosen to use National Lottery reserves to top up the Scottish Government-funded MYF, creating a National Lottery deficit projected at £10.9 million in 2025/26. This means that the financial basis of the MYF programme is already partly dependent on reserves rather than recurrent income, which creates a structural fragility in the post-2028 period if Scottish Government grant-in-aid does not continue to increase at the pace established in 2025/26 and 2026/27.
The Scottish Government's commitment to reach £100 million in additional annual culture investment by 2028/29 provides the framework within which MYF renewal will be negotiated. By 2026/27 the government has delivered over £70 million of the £100 million committed increase, with the remaining approximately £30 million still required by 2028/29. Whether that remaining uplift arrives in 2027/28, 2028/29, or is spread across both years will determine whether a post-2028 MYF programme can be capitalised at a level that maintains the current portfolio without contraction.
For North East Scotland organisations, this uncertainty has direct operational implications. MYF provides the financial certainty that allows Aberdeen Performing Arts, The Barn, Sound Festival and others to plan programmes, employ staff and commission artists more than twelve months ahead. If MYF renewal is delayed, as the gap between the 2018 regular funding round and the 2025 MYF launch illustrates is entirely possible, the region's anchor organisations will face a return to the planning instability of the 2018 to 2025 period at the moment when the recommendations of this report require them to be functioning as stable partners in a convergence economy strategy.
The Development Fund pathway and Core Operating Support
The 13 organisations in the MYF Development Stream have been confirmed in the portfolio from April 2026. All 13 organisations have been reviewed and approved to join the Multi-Year Funding cohort from April, receiving uplifts on their previous funding levels or 100% of their application request. Creative Scotland This creates a new population of MYF-funded organisations with one less year of multi-year certainty than the original 2025 cohort; their funding runs to March 2028 rather than the full original three-year term.
Creative Scotland has indicated its intention to develop a Core Operating Support model from 2029 as the successor to MYF. This model, the broad outlines of which are visible in the Independent Review of Creative Scotland published in November 2025, would provide rolling support to a core portfolio with periodic reviews rather than fixed three-year rounds. If implemented, it would represent the most significant structural improvement in the funding architecture since the introduction of regular funding in the 2000s: it would eliminate the planning gap between rounds that has caused repeated crises in the sector. The ACC Catalyst Fund, introduced in 2025/26 as a bridge mechanism for organisations working toward Core Operating Support eligibility, is a local-authority parallel to this ambition.
Neither the Core Operating Support model nor the ACC Catalyst Fund are confirmed. Both are trajectories rather than settled policy. The gap between ambition and implementation in both cases is the central risk in the post-2028 landscape.
The sub-threshold gap
The organisations most at risk in the post-2028 landscape are not those in the MYF portfolio. They are the organisations below the £50,000 MYF threshold - Look Again, Aberdeen Animates, FINE, the Curated Market, the open studios network, the rural creative practitioner community in Aberdeenshire - whose funding depends on the ACC Culture Investment Programme, Aberdeenshire Council's cultural support budget, and on the willingness of both councils to maintain and develop those budgets in a severely constrained fiscal environment.
The constraints are real. Aberdeen City Council's 2025/26 budget identified an £18.1 million funding gap and increased Council Tax by 9.85%. The Common Good Fund, which provides the majority of the CIP envelope, is an investment fund whose income is subject to market performance. There is no committed multi-year cultural budget line in either council's medium-term financial strategy that provides the sub-MYF organisations with the equivalent of the certainty that MYF provides to the larger bodies.
The Creative Ecology Structural Fund proposed in Recommendation 2 of this report is designed precisely to address this gap: a jointly administered fund, capitalised by both local authorities and Creative Scotland, providing multi-year revenue support to the sub-threshold organisations that are most at risk of churn and most critical to the ecosystem's functioning. Its design draws directly on the pattern of the churn documented in this report - the loss of GANE, Creative Cultures, Design in Action, and the repeated failure to sustain organisations that have built genuine creative infrastructure - and on the comment from Ken Hay of Culture Aberdeen that the sector is spending money on chasing money rather than on creative work.

Appendix B: Methodology
B.1 Purpose and structure of this appendix
This appendix sets out the complete methodological basis for the workforce estimates, gross value added calculations, skills analysis and economic impact figures presented in this report. It is structured to allow independent scrutiny and replication of each analytical step.
The research was conducted between September 2025 and April 2026. The workforce model, GVA estimates and skills analysis draw on data collected and validated during that period. Where figures in earlier working documents differ from those presented here, the figures in this report are authoritative. Two corrections to earlier drafts are documented explicitly at B.4 and B.8.
The methodology combines three distinct analytical approaches: official administrative data (BRES and IDBR), real-time firmographic and workforce data (The Data City platform and LinkedIn Talent Insights), and qualitative primary research (stakeholder workshops and practitioner interviews). Each approach has different strengths, limitations and appropriate uses; the combination is designed so that the limitations of each are addressed by the strengths of the others.

B.2 Establishing the business base: the crosswalk methodology
B.2.1 The gross registration universe
The starting point for the business base analysis is a dataset of 1,711 firms provided by The Data City platform, representing every business registered in the Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire area under creative or technology-adjacent Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes. The Data City uses artificial intelligence to analyse company website content combined with Companies House registration data, financial filings and other public sources, assigning each firm a Real-Time Industrial Classification (RTIC) that reflects its actual trading activity rather than its registered SIC code. SIC codes are assigned at point of registration and rarely updated; a firm registered in 2005 under SIC 62020 (IT consultancy) may have moved into games development or immersive content production in the intervening years without updating its registration. The RTIC system addresses this by updating classification continuously on the basis of current website content.
Both The Data City platform and the Inter-Departmental Business Register (IDBR) capture only firms with a formal legal structure. Self-employed sole traders and working proprietors without a registered company do not appear in either source. The business counts in this analysis represent the registered business base only; the self-employed and unregistered workforce is captured separately through the LinkedIn analysis and penetration gap modelling described at B.5 and B.6.
B.2.2 The crosswalk filter
To move from 1,711 gross registrations to a verified creative business base, this analysis applies a crosswalk methodology that assesses each firm against a single defining criterion: does the firm derive value primarily from original intellectual property and cultural curation, rather than from the provision of labour hours or technical infrastructure services?
The filter was applied in two passes. The first pass removed clearly excludable firms on the basis of four evidenced grounds: brass plate registrations using corporate hub addresses with no independent evidence of physical operations (60% of the firms at corporate hub addresses were confirmed as noise through spot checks of 425 registrations); personal service companies established as tax-efficient vehicles for individual IT contractors in the energy sector (40% of technology-adjacent SIC registrations followed this pattern); nominee registrations with no local staff, office footprint or evidence of trading activity (25% of the excluded population); and industrial utility firms whose primary output is technical infrastructure support for the energy sector -asset integrity management, reservoir telemetry, technical safety documentation -rather than creative content, products or experiences. The second pass involved spot-checking, contractor pattern analysis and physical footprint verification.
Application of the crosswalk filter produces an active creative core of 922 verified firms, from a gross universe of 1,711, with 789 entities excluded. The 922 active core firms include 910 mapped to the nine DCMS creative industries sub-sectors and 12 in the cultural sector, covering historical sites, music retail and instrument manufacture, that fall outside the strict DCMS definition but are integral to the regional creative ecosystem.
B.2.3 The IT consultancy exclusion
SIC 62020 (IT consultancy) is excluded from the creative industries count throughout this analysis. Of the 435 firms registered under this code in IDBR, the crosswalk reduces the verified creative technology population to 130 firms. The remainder - primarily energy sector enterprise support, system integration and personal service contractor vehicles - are excluded on the grounds established in B.2.2.
The BRES employment count similarly excludes the 1,600 employees and estimated £150 million GVA attributable to SIC 62020 activity in the region. This exclusion is applied consistently across the business base, workforce, GVA and economic impact calculations throughout the report. Readers wishing to compare these figures with national estimates that include IT consultancy within the creative industries definition may add £150 million to the direct GVA figure and £228 million to the total economic impact figure (applying the Tier 2 multiplier of 1.60).
The IT Services and Consultancy population identified through LinkedIn analysis - 3,992 individuals across IT Services (1,710) and IT Consulting (2,282) - was held in a separate segment of the LinkedIn dataset throughout the analysis and was not included in the creative workforce count at any stage. The significance of this separation is documented at B.4.
B.2.4 The convergence tier
Beyond the active creative core of 852 firms, The Data City analysis identifies a convergence tier of firms in the energy services and engineering sectors that have developed, over decades of safety-critical offshore operations, a technical capability in simulation, three-dimensional visualisation, digital twin development and immersive training that is directly contiguous with the capability base of the creative technology sector. Under standard SIC classification these firms do not appear in creative industries counts, but real-time industrial classification and human validation confirm their creative-technical hybrid character.
The convergence tier comprises 115 validated firms with confirmed creative-technical crossover activity and a further 135 firms with comparable sector characteristics that are conservatively excluded from headline figures pending further validation. The 115 validated firms employ approximately 1,145 workers (1,090 FTE) and generate an estimated £137 million in direct GVA, producing a total economic impact of £220 million at the Tier 2 multiplier of 1.60. A further 150 convergence freelancers (110 FTE) are attributed to a Tier 3b segment using the same methodology as the core Tier 3 analysis.
When the convergence tier is incorporated, the total creative economy of North East Scotland accounts for approximately 12,090 workers, £616 million in direct gross value added, and £930 million in total annual economic impact. These converged figures are presented separately from the base four-tier model throughout the report, to preserve the analytical clarity of the creative core estimate while making the strategic significance of the convergence tier visible.
B.3 Official employment baseline: BRES
The Business Register and Employment Survey provides the administrative floor of the analysis. BRES data for Aberdeen City and Aberdeenshire, filtered against creative industries SIC codes and adjusted to remove general IT consultancy (SIC 62020), produces a count of 3,305 employees. This figure represents the minimum that can be confirmed from official data: it captures the region's publicly funded cultural institutions, its registered design and architecture practices, and its commercial creative firms, but misses the self-employed, freelance and portfolio workforce entirely.
BRES data is the primary source for Tier 1 (institutional anchors) and provides the employed workforce baseline for Tier 2 (SME commercial core). BRES headcount figures are cross-validated against The Data City company intelligence and adjusted to include directors of registered micro-companies who are remunerated via dividends rather than salary and therefore do not appear in BRES employment counts. These dividend-remunerated directors are identified through cross-referencing of Companies House officer records against The Data City company profiles; their inclusion in the formal workforce count is methodologically appropriate as they are directors of registered limited companies, not unregistered sole traders.
B.4 LinkedIn workforce analysis
B.4.1 Methodology
LinkedIn Talent Insights data was used to identify the creative workforce population beyond the formal BRES-captured employed base. The platform was treated as a large pre-filled occupational survey rather than a social media tool: profile data was extracted for residents of the North East Scotland geography with occupations in creative sector classifications, structured into six creative supply chain segments and one separate IT Services and Consultancy segment.
The six creative supply chain segments are: Content (film, television, video, music, broadcast media, photography), Experiences (performing arts, museums, heritage, dance), Originals (visual arts, fashion design, craft, independent artists and writers), Services (advertising, marketing, PR, architecture, design), Tech (creative technology including software development and computer games), and a Total Creative Supply Chains summary sheet. The IT Services and Consultancy segment -covering IT Services (1,710 individuals) and IT Consulting (2,282 individuals), total 3,992 -was maintained as a separate, parallel dataset throughout the analysis and was not included in the creative workforce count. It was captured for reference purposes only, to allow comparison with the excluded BRES IT consultancy population.
B.4.2 Correction of a double-subtraction error in earlier drafts
Earlier working documents in this project applied a subtraction of the IT Services and Consultancy population (3,992) from the Total Creative Supply Chains figure (9,414), producing a net figure of 5,422. This was an error. The 3,992 IT Services and Consultancy workers were never included in the 9,414 Total Creative Supply Chains figure; they were held in a separate segment throughout. Subtracting them from 9,414 therefore removed a population that had not been added, reducing the creative workforce count by 3,992 people who should have been included.
The correct total from the LinkedIn creative supply chain analysis, incorporating all six creative segments, is 9,414. This figure is then adjusted as described at B.4.3 to produce the working creative workforce total of 8,760.
All workforce, FTE, GVA and economic impact figures in this report reflect the corrected analysis. The figures 5,422, 7,530, £315 million and £474 million appearing in earlier working documents should be disregarded. The authoritative figures are those in Part Three of this report.
B.4.3 Software development and computer games
The Tech segment of the LinkedIn creative supply chain data includes 1,957 individuals identifying as working in software development and 179 in computer games, alongside 275 in computer and network security, 599 in information services, and 83 in other technology classifications (desktop computing software products, IT system custom software development, digital accessibility services, social networking platforms). 
Software developers and games developers working in creative technology contexts are creative economy workers; their inclusion is consistent with the DCMS creative industries framework, which includes IT, software and computer services within the creative industries taxonomy. However, the computer and network security and information services populations within the Tech segment were omitted from our workforce calculations: they are more likely to come under other Digital SIC codes and to have been included in estimates of employment for the ONE Digital sector report. This is a methodological boundary case that is acknowledged as such; their combined headcount of 874 represents 10% of the creative Tech segment and a smaller share of the total workforce.
B.4.4 Adjustment for museums and libraries underrepresentation
Comparison of the LinkedIn-derived Experiences segment (591 individuals, of whom 136 are attributed to museums and galleries) against the BRES count for museums, galleries and libraries (435 employees) reveals significant underrepresentation of this workforce in LinkedIn data. This reflects the well-documented pattern of lower LinkedIn adoption among public sector cultural workers, part-time museum and library staff, and older workers in heritage roles.
To correct for this underrepresentation, the museums, galleries and libraries workforce is taken from BRES (435) rather than LinkedIn. Historical sites workers (185, from the LinkedIn Experiences segment keyword count) are added to this population as they represent the same institutional type and are included in our analysis of Tier 1 organisations. The combined museums, galleries, libraries and historical sites workforce is 620, of which 620 are attributed to Tier 1.
The resulting adjusted LinkedIn total of 8,760 (replacing 9,414) is the basis for the tier allocations in Table B.1 below.

B.6 Tier 4: the invisible workforce
Tier 4 cannot be measured directly. It is estimated through penetration gap analysis: the difference between the estimated total population of creative practitioners in low-digital sub-sectors and the proportion identifiable through LinkedIn and official data. LinkedIn penetration rates for independent musicians, artists, writers and craft makers are estimated at 30% to 45%, against over 80% for technology and design professionals. B.8 GVA calculation
B.8.1 Methodology
Direct gross value added is calculated by multiplying the FTE count for each tier by a GVA per FTE figure derived from a combination of ONS regional output data and imputed financial modelling of individual company performance. The imputed financial model cross-references company turnover data from Companies House filings with employee counts from BRES, applying sector-specific GVA-to-turnover ratios derived from the ONS Annual Business Survey and DCMS economic estimates. GVA per FTE figures are derived from the resulting GVA per head figures, scaled by the appropriate FTE intensity factor for each tier.
For Tier 3 (visible freelancers), a productivity discount is applied to the sector-specific GVA per FTE rates. This reflects the well-established finding that freelance workers in creative industries generate lower GVA per FTE than their employed counterparts in the same sector, owing to downtime between commissions, lower average billing rates and limited supply chain depth. The discount factor of 0.480 is derived from the ratio of the Tier 3 GVA per FTE rate in the original methodology document (£44,769) to the Tier 2 blended GVA per FTE rate in the same document (£93,174). Applying this discount to the sector-specific rates produces an effective Tier 3 GVA per FTE of £47,583 -close to the original blended rate, with the small upward difference reflecting that the corrected Tier 3 population is weighted more heavily toward higher-GVA sectors (IT and software, film and television) than the original model assumed.

B.9 The convergence tier: methodology
The convergence tier comprises firms in the energy services and engineering sectors whose real-time industrial classification confirms creative-technical hybrid activity in simulation, three-dimensional visualisation, digital twin development and immersive training. B.10 Skills analysis
The skills analysis draws on LinkedIn skills data for the North East Scotland geography, structured by the Data City LinkedIn skills dashboard and normalised per capita against working-age population for comparison with a chosen comparator geography. Nottinghamshire county (population 824,800, Census 2021) was selected as the comparator on the basis of geographic equivalence: both datasets cover a principal city and its rural hinterland across two or more administrative areas. Using Nottingham city proper (population 329,300) as the comparator would produce different ratios; that approach was rejected on grounds of geographic inequivalence, as Nottingham city is substantially smaller than North East Scotland's combined area.
North East Scotland's combined population for per-capita normalisation is 496,860 (Aberdeen City 231,780, Aberdeenshire 265,080; NRS 2024 mid-year estimates).
Key per-capita ratios against the Nottinghamshire county comparator:
Simulation: North East Scotland 2,298 practitioners against Nottinghamshire 576, representing a per-capita concentration 6.6 times the comparator -a significant surplus reflecting fifty years of safety-critical offshore simulation training in the region.
Virtual production: North East Scotland 25 against Nottinghamshire 159, representing a per-capita deficit of 3.8 times the comparator.
Data storytelling: North East Scotland 377 against Nottinghamshire 1,725, representing a per-capita deficit of 2.8 times the comparator.
These ratios describe the distinctive capability profiles of the two Tier 2 sub-populations -the Westhill-Dyce corridor and the city-centre creative community -rather than deficits of the region as a whole. A national benchmark against a UK average would require Data City platform data or LinkedIn Economic Graph data at national scale; that data is not currently available in this research and is identified as a priority for the Joint Board's first-year measurement programme.

B.11 Qualitative primary research
Four in-person stakeholder workshops were held in Aberdeen in November 2025, bringing together practitioners, anchor organisations, local authority officers and national agency representatives across the creative economy. Participants were recruited through the Creative Scotland stakeholder database, the Opportunity North East network, the Freelance Independents North East membership, and direct outreach to organisations identified in the sector mapping. Outreach was also extended to digital-technical firms in the Westhill-Dyce corridor through a dedicated web page and direct email contact; no corridor firms attended any of the four workshops.
In-depth research conversations were conducted with Peter Baxter (Deemouth Artist Studios), Sally Reaper (RGU Gray's School of Art), Dean McGlynn (Aberdeen City Council City Centre Manager) and Eloise Grey (Creative Industries Lead, Aberdeenshire Council), among others. Transcripts of these conversations are held by The Fifth Sector and are available to the commissioning bodies on request.
Qualitative findings were used to validate and contextualise the quantitative analysis, to identify patterns not visible in administrative data, and to provide the practitioner testimony and named case study evidence used throughout the report. They are not used as the basis for quantitative estimates except where explicitly noted.

Appendix C: Creative skills needs and gaps for Aberdeen city and Aberdeenshire
C.1 Purpose and scope
This appendix assesses the extent to which current creative and digital skills provision in North East Scotland equips practitioners, graduates and employers to meet the demands of a growing creative economy and, specifically, the convergence opportunity identified in the main report. It draws on three published assessments of skills needs: the Skills Development Scotland Sectoral Skills Assessment for the Creative Industries (October 2025), the Regional Skills Assessment for Aberdeen City and Shire (October 2025), and the Creative Industries Policy and Evidence Centre's 2025 report on skills mismatches in the UK creative industries. It also draws on desk analysis of the current course portfolio at North East Scotland College (NESCol), Robert Gordon University and the University of Aberdeen.
The analysis covers three questions: what skills the sector needs over the next five to ten years; what the regional education and training pipeline currently provides; and where the gaps between supply and demand are most acute and most tractable.
C.2 Future skills demand: what the evidence shows
The three source assessments converge on a consistent picture of sector need. The dominant demand is for practitioners who combine creative capability with advanced technical and digital competence -particularly in software development, programming, multimedia production, data analysis, interactive media and immersive content. These are not new skills categories, but the depth of technical capability required is increasing, and the pace of change -driven principally by AI, real-time rendering and extended reality technologies -is accelerating faster than training providers have so far adapted.
Five priority areas are identified across the three reports.
Advanced technical and digital skills. Software development, programming, multimedia production, data analysis, design engineering, app and web development and digital storytelling are identified as persistent unmet needs. The Creative Industries PEC report specifically identifies advanced and specialist IT skills as the single largest category of unfilled vacancy in the sector nationally. The Aberdeen and Shire Regional Skills Assessment confirms that this gap is present locally and is not being closed by current provision.
Capacity for rapid technological change. AI adoption, immersive media production, real-time content creation and digitally integrated workflows are identified in all three reports as skills that employers require and that the current graduate pipeline does not systematically develop. The issue is not only what is taught but how quickly curricula respond: programmes designed around tool sets that were current at the point of validation may be significantly out of step with employer needs by the time students graduate.
Business, enterprise and freelance skills. The structural character of the regional creative economy -where the majority of the workforce is self-employed, freelance or working through micro-enterprises -creates specific demand for skills that are not primarily technical: contract management, intellectual property, financial planning, pitching and client relationship management. These skills are poorly served by the current curriculum in all three institutions. The Skills Development Scotland sectoral assessment identifies entrepreneurship and business management as critical for sustainable creative careers and notes that they are systematically absent from creative and technical training programmes.
Work-based and practical learning. All three reports emphasise employer demand for graduates who can operate effectively in professional settings from their first week of employment rather than requiring substantial on-the-job training. The gap between course design and employer need is described in the PEC report as a persistent and growing problem, particularly as the tools and workflows required in practice evolve faster than assessed coursework.
Flexible and modular provision for the existing workforce. The current regional provision is almost entirely oriented toward initial qualification routes. Short courses, continuing professional development programmes, micro-credentials and bootcamps -the forms of provision that allow the existing workforce to update skills in response to technological change -are very limited. This gap is particularly acute for the immersive media and AI-enhanced creative workflows that are transforming practice in the highest-value parts of the sector.

C.3 Current provision: strengths and limitations
The pipeline from NESCol to university
The most significant structural strength of regional skills provision is the articulation pathway from NESCol through to degree-level study at RGU and, to a lesser extent, the University of Aberdeen. NESCol's portfolio of HNC and HND programmes in games design, web development, software development, media production, television, sound production and filmcraft maps directly onto a range of RGU undergraduate programmes through the Degree Link Associate Student scheme, which provides over 80 mapped pathways, with HND typically offering entry to Year 3 of a matched degree. The University of Aberdeen has maintained a formal articulation agreement with NESCol since 2014, covering a range of subject areas including computing and music, with HNC typically offering Year 2 entry and HND Year 3 entry where curriculum alignment is confirmed.
This pipeline is a genuine regional asset. It allows students to move from vocational qualification to honours degree with reduced cost and time, and it reflects a level of institutional coordination between further and higher education that is not present in all parts of Scotland. The NESCol NextGen computing suite -which explicitly incorporates AI, cybersecurity and IoT content -represents a deliberate attempt to keep vocational provision aligned with the direction of the sector. The Television (NextGen) HND, the HND Sound Production and the HND Games Design programmes all provide practical, industry-facing preparation that employers in broadcast, audio and games recognise directly.
Limitations of current provision
The provision has several systematic limitations that the source assessments and desk analysis both confirm.
The region has no dedicated specialist programme in immersive media, extended reality, virtual production or real-time content creation at degree or postgraduate level. RGU's BSc Computing and Creative Design incorporates VR headsets and immersive technology equipment in its facilities, but this represents access to tools rather than a substantive curriculum in XR or virtual production. Given that the convergence opportunity identified in the main report rests substantially on the region's existing simulation and visualisation capability, and that connecting that capability to creative production markets requires practitioners who can work across both technical and creative production disciplines, the absence of any formal programme addressing this intersection is significant.
Business and enterprise skills are poorly embedded. The Skills Development Scotland sectoral assessment identifies this as a national problem; in the North East Scotland curriculum it is confirmed by the absence of any programme that systematically combines creative or technical training with business management, freelance practice or entrepreneurship as co-equal components rather than optional additions. Given that 49% of the regional creative workforce is independent -freelancers, sole traders and micro-enterprise operators -this is a structural misalignment between what is taught and what the workforce needs.
Short-course and CPD provision for the existing workforce is minimal. There is no publicly visible programme at NESCol or RGU offering intensive training in game engine workflows (Unreal Engine or Unity), virtual production fundamentals, creative AI tools or data-driven media for practitioners already in employment. This matters particularly for the freelance and micro-enterprise majority of the Tier 3 workforce, who cannot access full-time programmes but require systematic access to skills updating as the technology landscape changes.
Data-driven and AI-enhanced creative skills are not a substantive component of any current programme. The PEC report identifies data literacy, AI workflow integration and machine learning for creative applications as increasingly important across the sector; the regional curriculum addresses these as technical computing skills rather than as applied creative competencies. There is no postgraduate provision of the kind that several other UK institutions have introduced in creative AI and generative media.
The heritage, cultural and digital heritage intersection is underserved. Given the depth of the regional heritage asset -documented in the Pinning Stones analysis and in the heritage substrate described in Part Six of this report -and the growing market for immersive heritage experience, digital curation and XR-based heritage interpretation, the absence of any programme addressing this intersection represents a missed opportunity that is both economic and cultural.

Appendix D: Deemouth Artist Studios and Edit Aberdeen Case Study
D.1 Purpose and scope
This appendix provides a case study of Deemouth Artist Studios (DAST) and Edit Aberdeen, which serves as the primary direct evidence for the Tier 4 workforce population described in Part Three of this report. It should be read alongside Section 3.4 of the main report, where the abbreviated version appears, and alongside Appendix B, which sets out the methodological basis for the Tier 4 estimate.
The case study is based principally on a research conversation between Iain Bennett of The Fifth Sector and Peter Baxter of Deemouth Artist Studios, conducted in February 2026 (Bennett, 2026). It draws additionally on research notes compiled by The Fifth Sector in March 2026 identifying named practitioners associated with DAST and the Edit retail model (The Fifth Sector, 2026c), on a research conversation with Dean McGlynn, Aberdeen City Council's City Centre Manager, conducted in April 2026 (McGlynn, 2026), and on the broader evidential context provided by Matarasso (2014) and the Culture Collective programme evaluation (Blanche et al., 2023).
The case study should be read with the following limitations in mind. The primary source is a single practitioner account. Membership figures, demographic data, detailed financial information and the funding split between earned income and any grant support were not captured in the research conversation and are not available in the materials provided to this research. Where claims made by Peter Baxter have not been independently corroborated, they are presented as practitioner observations rather than verified findings. The retail environment effect described in Section D.5 is presented as a claim requiring further corroboration, not as an established finding.
D.2 Background and origins
Deemouth Artist Studios was established at the Deemouth Business Centre on South Esplanade East, Torry, Aberdeen, by Peter Baxter and Allan Watson. Baxter's professional background is in accountancy and plumbing. He came to the creative sector through a conviction that talented people deserve the infrastructure to earn from their work rather than through artistic training. That pragmatic orientation shapes the character of DAST and distinguishes it from a subsidised arts project: it is primarily concerned with helping practitioners build viable commercial practices rather than with supporting creative activity as an end in itself.
Torry is among the most deprived communities in Aberdeen. It is geographically separated from the city centre by the River Dee, and culturally and economically peripheral to the commercial creative economy that this report maps in its upper tiers. That DAST is based there rather than in the city centre is not incidental: it reflects both the economics of studio space in the city and the character of the community from which its practitioners are drawn.
The choice of location also positions DAST within a specific tradition of artist-led studio development in peripheral urban communities, a tradition documented and analysed in the Pinning Stones mapping of the Aberdeenshire creative ecosystem. Matarasso notes that the region's arts infrastructure has historically been built by practitioners rather than institutions, and that the result is a distributed, self-reliant network of studios, collectives and community organisations whose combined cultural and economic contribution substantially exceeds what any single institutional account would suggest (Matarasso, 2014, pp. 42-47).
D.3 The studio community: membership and model
DAST operates as a shared studio community. The precise number of members is not confirmed in the available evidence, but Baxter's account describes a community that includes the Aberdeen Ceramic School, a trading entity offering school facilities and membership; an upholstery school whose operator both trades and teaches; and individual artists, designers and makers working across a range of disciplines including painting, sculpture, jewellery, ceramics and textiles.
The membership spans the full spectrum of the Tier 4 population as defined in this report. Baxter describes it as running from "ladies that paint" through to "serious trading people that deal with five-star hotels and whisky companies and go to Goldsmiths and sell commercially as well. This characterisation maps directly onto the three-part Tier 4 typology: fully professional commercial practitioners for whom creative work is the primary professional activity and principal source of income; portfolio workers for whom it forms one element of a mixed income structure; and serious non-professional practitioners for whom it is a sustained vocation rather than a primary income source. The FTE intensity assumption of 0.45 applied to Tier 4 as a whole reflects the economic reality of all three segments taken together.
Baxter's characterisation is consistent with the profile of the Aberdeenshire arts and maker community documented by Matarasso (2014), who found that the region had attracted a relatively mature cohort of established artists and makers, many of whom had moved to or returned to the area in their thirties, forties or fifties, bringing with them established practices and market relationships. Matarasso notes that this profile differs from that of younger urban creative communities and reflects the specific economic and social conditions of the region, including relatively high property costs and distance from Central Belt networks (Matarasso, 2014, pp. 80-82).
D.4 Named practitioners: international reach from Torry
Two named practitioners associated with DAST illustrate the range of the commercial activity taking place within an organisation that is entirely invisible to official economic statistics.
Emma Louise Wilson is a raised enamel jeweller based in Torry. Her work is sold through the Goldsmiths Fair in London and the Mad About Jewellery fair in the United States; it has featured in Vanity Fair. She is also an occupant of Edit, the retail outlet described in Section D.5. Her practice represents the commercially active end of the Tier 4 spectrum: an established maker with documented international markets, operating from an address in one of Aberdeen's most deprived communities, generating export revenue that no regional economic dataset currently records.
Megan Falconer is a jeweller who trained at DAST and subsequently established her own studio. She teaches, runs workshops, produces bespoke commission pieces including for weddings, and has a presence in the Balmoral gift shop. Her practice is aligned with the wellbeing economy argument that Aberdeenshire Council has made in connection with the maker sector: she works in part on the restoration and rejuvenation of existing jewellery, an activity that sits at the intersection of craft, personal history and sustainable consumption.
These two practitioners are not exceptional. They are representative of a wider population of makers across Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire whose commercial reach, documented market presence and professional seriousness is simply not visible to any of the data sources that regional economic assessments conventionally rely upon.
D.5 Edit Aberdeen: the retail model and its commercial results
Edit Aberdeen is a Community Interest Company operating a retail outlet in the Bon Accord Centre in Aberdeen city centre. It was initially established as a retail platform for DAST members and has since broadened to include makers from across Aberdeenshire. The community of interest for which it operates is defined as both the artists who benefit from it and the buyers who visit it: the CIC model is designed to prevent the retail infrastructure being redirected away from its founding purpose.
Edit's presence in the Bon Accord Centre is supported by Robert Gordon University's Gray's School of Art, which acts as a charitable backer. This enables Edit to occupy commercial retail space at zero business rates under the provisions that apply to charitable occupiers of otherwise empty commercial lots. The model is practically effective and replicable, but it depends on the availability of vacant commercial space and the willingness of a charitable institution to act as backer. It is not a model that can be assumed to scale without those preconditions being met.
In the year prior to this research, Edit generated £100,000 in retail sales for the artists and makers whose work it stocks. Of that revenue, 60% is returned directly to makers and 40% is retained by Edit to sustain its operations. These figures are drawn from Baxter's account and have not been independently verified against Edit's accounts. They are presented here as indicative of the scale of commercial activity rather than as audited financial data. Even on a conservative reading, they represent a meaningful and documented contribution to the incomes of practitioners who do not appear in any official economic statistics. The £100,000 figure is the most significant single piece of direct quantitative evidence in support of the Tier 4 estimate in this report.
The tenancy position of Edit in the Bon Accord Centre is precarious. The centre changed ownership, and Baxter's account makes clear that Edit could be ejected at short notice. This precarity is a structural feature of the model rather than an exceptional circumstance: it reflects the wider vulnerability of small creative organisations occupying commercial retail space in a city centre environment where lease decisions are made by private landlords on commercial grounds. The policy response to this precarity is addressed in Section D.8.
D.6 The Curated Market
A second retail initiative in the Bon Accord Centre, directly adjacent to Edit, provides further evidence of the market infrastructure being built for Tier 4 practitioners. The Curated Market was established in the former River Island space, taken on by Charlie House, a local children's hospice charity. Charlie House's charitable status provides rates relief on the space in the same way that RGU's involvement provides it for Edit.
The Curated Market launched on 3 April 2026 following a renovation of the space that addressed what was felt to be an overly informal presentation in its earlier incarnation. The renovated market offers ten booths at £30 per stall, workshop space at £25 and gallery space at £50, all at entry-level rates intended to be accessible to practitioners who could not afford conventional retail tenancies. It offers a workshop and gallery function alongside the retail function, providing the kind of multi-purpose creative infrastructure that the broader ecosystem currently lacks.
Edit made a proposal to take on and reposition this space, recognising its potential as a complementary venue that would raise the overall quality of the cultural retail offer in that part of the centre. The Curated Market's launch represents the most recent addition to a small but growing cluster of creative retail and workshop infrastructure in the Bon Accord Centre, whose significance for the city centre's commercial positioning is addressed in Section D.7.
D.7 The city centre context
Dean McGlynn, Aberdeen City Council's City Centre Manager, provided a research conversation in April 2026 that partially corroborates the case for Edit's significance to the Bon Accord Centre's commercial positioning, while stopping short of making causal claims that the available evidence does not support.
McGlynn confirmed that Bon Accord's pitch to new retail tenants now explicitly references Edit and the Curated Market as differentiating factors, and that Sostrene Grene, a Scandinavian homeware brand that opened in the centre in 2025, viewed the centre's direction of travel favourably in part because of what these cultural uses were bringing. He was clear that this does not constitute evidence that Edit caused any specific leasing decision: the factors that determine where a retail brand takes premises are numerous, and the commercial intelligence available to property teams is not shared with city centre management in a form that would allow this causal claim to be made with confidence. The observation is recorded here as a qualified corroboration of a plausible hypothesis rather than as a finding.
McGlynn identified the School Hill stretch, running between Bon Accord and Robert Gordon's College, as having the worst vacancy rate in the city centre. He described it as a location where cultural activation could make a material difference, noting its architectural character and its proximity to the university, the art gallery and the existing cluster of hospitality and creative activity nearby. He identified it as a potential location for a more coherent creative and cultural quarter, if the vacancy problem is addressed with that ambition in mind rather than with conventional retail recruitment.
McGlynn also described Aberdeen City Council's development of a pop-up activation kit: a set of pre-furnished, ready-to-occupy retail spaces designed to reduce the cost and logistical burden for organisations seeking a city centre presence. This mechanism, currently being promoted through Aberdeen Inspired and business gateway channels, is not currently connected systematically to creative organisations. The gap between this resource and the organisations that could most benefit from it is both obvious and addressable.
The retail geography literature provides the conceptual basis for the agglomeration hypothesis that Baxter's practitioner account suggests. Wrigley and Lowe (2002) document the established evidence that anchor tenants and distinctive independent retailers can generate positive spillover effects for surrounding commercial space. The DAST and Edit case is consistent with this literature, but the specific claim requires the kind of independent corroboration that would come from footfall data, leasing records or retailer interviews: none of these are currently available to this research.
D.8 The university partnership: graduate retention and the Editions pathway
The relationship between DAST, Edit and Robert Gordon University's Gray's School of Art is one of the most significant analytical findings of this case study, not least because it was entirely absent from the research team's formal conversations with RGU about its engagement with the regional creative economy.
Third and fourth year cohorts from Gray's now engage with Edit through live commercial briefs, developing products that are commercially scalable, reflect the history and identity of Aberdeen, and are suitable for retail through Edit. Students who complete these briefs leave with a product in a shop, a retail relationship and a practical understanding of the commercial creative economy in Aberdeen.
Sally Reaper at RGU has led an Editions programme in which three or four recent graduates have developed work for a permanent exhibition; at least one of those graduates has subsequently taken a studio space at DAST. This is a direct, documented graduate retention pathway: a student moves from coursework brief to commercial exhibition to studio tenancy, each step in the sequence enabled by the DAST and Edit infrastructure. It is not captured in any institutional data, it does not appear in graduate destination surveys, and RGU did not mention it when asked about its engagement with the regional creative economy.
This gap between the formal institutional account and the lived reality of practitioner-university relationships is itself a finding of this research. It suggests that the creative economy benefits of university-practitioner engagement are systematically under-reported in the data sources on which regional economic assessments rely. The Editions pathway is a graduate retention mechanism of the kind that Part Five of this report calls for at scale: its virtue is that it costs nothing in additional infrastructure and depends only on the continuing relationship between a willing academic, a working community of practice and a retail outlet prepared to take commercial risk on graduate work.
D.9 The broader ecosystem: NEOS, the Pinning Stones and the concentric circles structure
DAST and Edit are not exceptional. They are representative of a distributed ecosystem of community studios, craft collectives, open studio networks and maker markets across Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire.
Matarasso documents a cultural landscape characterised by dispersal, self-reliance and a strong tradition of voluntary and community-led cultural activity, noting that "the links between culture and community are everywhere, if you trouble to look" (Matarasso, 2014, p. 40). The North East Open Studios programme, which by 2013 involved more than 320 artists and makers across the region, is identified as a cornerstone of this ecosystem, providing both a commercial outlet and a network function for practitioners who would otherwise be isolated (Matarasso, 2014, pp. 79-80). The profile documented in the Pinning Stones, a mature and established cohort of practitioners working from home studios and converted rural buildings and generating income through direct sales, commissions, teaching and open studio events, maps directly onto the Tier 4 typology developed in this report a decade later (Matarasso, 2014, pp. 80-82).
Culture Collective’s evaluation provides complementary evidence on the policy conditions that enable this kind of practitioner community to sustain itself. The evaluation identifies flexible, long-term funding, fair pay for creative practitioners, and the development of peer networks and shared infrastructure as the primary enabling factors for community-embedded creative practice (Blanche et al., 2023, pp. 42-43). It also identifies the absence of these conditions, particularly short-term funding, lack of affordable workspace and the failure of public institutions to recognise the value of community-led creative activity, as the primary constraints (Blanche et al., 2023, pp. 44-47). Both sets of findings apply directly to the DAST and Edit context.
Baxter's account of the DAST community suggests a concentric circles structure that provides qualitative validation for the penetration gap methodology used to estimate the Tier 4 population. DAST sits at the centre: a registered community of practitioners with studio space and a shared commercial infrastructure. Edit sits at the commercial interface: the point at which the studio community meets the public economy. NEOS forms the wider network: encompassing practitioners across Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire who may or may not have a DAST connection but who participate in the same distributed ecosystem. Beyond NEOS lies the full dispersal of individual rural and coastal practitioners working in relative isolation from any institutional structure. The 2,035-person Tier 4 estimate is grounded in the assumption that this full structure, taken together, constitutes a significant and systematically undercounted component of the regional creative economy. The DAST and Edit case study provides direct qualitative evidence for that assumption at the innermost ring, and the NEOS participation figures provide the quantitative anchor for the rings beyond.
D.10 Policy implications
Baxter's primary policy ask, when invited to describe what he would most like to see from a supportive council, is not a grant or a subsidy. It is recognition: specifically, that the Community Interest Company model should be understood as a supportable entity with some assurances over its tenure. The legal and governance structures through which organisations like Edit operate should be recognised and protected by public policy, rather than treated as anomalies falling outside conventional support frameworks.
This ask is consistent with the findings of the Culture Collective evaluation, which identifies the recognition of artists' professional knowledge and skills as a primary enabling factor for community-embedded creative practice and the absence of that recognition as a primary constraint (Blanche et al., 2023, p. 42). It is also consistent with the argument in the Pinning Stones that the cultural and social fabric of Aberdeenshire's communities, the pinning stones of the title, is systematically overlooked by policymakers focused on the larger building blocks of economic development, and that this oversight carries real costs for the sustainability of community life (Matarasso, 2014, pp. 104-106).
The policy ask is modest and achievable. It does not require new funding. It requires institutional literacy about the creative economy: an understanding of how Community Interest Companies function, what they contribute and what they need in order to remain viable. It requires the protection of affordable studio and retail space in the city centre, including a consistent and transparent approach to rates relief for creative organisations occupying otherwise vacant commercial space, which does not require annual renegotiation. It requires the formalisation of university-practitioner partnerships that currently exist informally and are therefore invisible to the institutions that could most benefit from them. And it requires the connection of open studio networks and maker communities to regional economic strategy in a way that recognises their contribution without imposing the reporting and governance burdens that have historically deterred Aberdeenshire's creative community from engaging with public funding (Matarasso, 2014, p. 47).
McGlynn's identification of the pop-up activation kit as an existing mechanism not yet connected to creative organisations provides a practical starting point for this work. The recommendation in Part Nine that the Joint Board facilitate a systematic relationship between the council's culture team and its city centre management function is designed to close this gap. It requires no new funding and no new infrastructure: it requires two parts of the same institution to work together in a way they currently do not.
D.11 Conclusion
The DAST and Edit case study provides direct qualitative evidence in support of the Tier 4 estimate and the broader argument of this report. It confirms that a significant population of creative practitioners exists in Aberdeen and Aberdeenshire who are invisible to official statistics but generating real commercial revenue. It confirms that the demographic of this population is mixed and that the economic contribution of each segment is real, if variable. It confirms that the infrastructure through which this population operates is fragile, informally constituted and dependent on the energy and resourcefulness of individual practitioners and community entrepreneurs.
It also confirms the limits of what a single case study can establish. The membership figures, demographic data and financial details that would allow DAST and Edit to be fully integrated into the quantitative model are not available in the current evidence base. Follow-up research should seek to establish these figures, to corroborate the retail environment claims through independent evidence, and to map the relationship between DAST membership, NEOS participation and the wider Tier 4 population more precisely.
What the case study establishes beyond reasonable doubt is that the challenge for policy is not to prove that this population exists. Peter Baxter has already done that, with a retail till receipt for £100,000. The challenge is to build the institutional frameworks that would allow this population to grow, to earn more and to stay in the region.
This case study draws on research conversations between Iain Bennett of The Fifth Sector and Peter Baxter of Deemouth Artist Studios, conducted in February 2026 and March 2026, with Sue Reaper of RGU in March 2026 with Dean McGlynn, Aberdeen City Council City Centre Manager, in April 2026. The authors are grateful to both for their time and the precision of their accounts.

Appendix E: Options assessment
E.1 Cluster development or convergence economy: the basis for the sequenced recommendation
Two development pathways were assessed for the regional creative economy: a concentrated creative cluster in Aberdeen city centre, anchored by shared infrastructure and institutional investment, and a convergence economy programme building on the region's distinctive energy-creative technical capability.
The case for city-centre cluster development rests on the region's existing concentration of creative agencies, architecture practices, design firms and screen production businesses in the AB10 to AB24 postcode geography, the availability of tested national frameworks through UKRI's Creative Industries Clusters Programme and CoSTAR, and the potential for Screen North East studio investment to generate the kind of anchor effect that BBC Salford demonstrated in the North of England. The Creative Industries Policy and Evidence Centre's cluster research confirms that geographic concentration generates agglomeration benefits (shared labour markets, knowledge spillovers, supplier networks) that dispersed provision cannot replicate.
The case against leading with cluster development is threefold. The density required for spontaneous agglomeration effects is not currently present; the 10-to-15-year development timeline for cluster effects to materialise is incompatible with the political and funding cycles the region operates within; and the upfront capital requirement for screen studio infrastructure, shared workspace and maker space investment represents a substantial public commitment in advance of any demonstrable return. More fundamentally, a city-centre cluster strategy does not address the two populations whose commercial potential is the most distinctive and most immediately actionable: the convergence tier firms of the Westhill-Dyce corridor, which are already generating £137 million in direct GVA from a position of complete institutional isolation, and the Tier 3 freelance and independent workforce, which accounts for 30% of the regional creative workforce and benefits from none of the infrastructure that cluster models are designed to provide.
The convergence economy programme requires no capital investment in its first phase. The firms exist. The skills base exists. The energy sector client relationships exist. What is required is visibility, connectivity and a shared strategic narrative, all of which can be developed through the Joint Board, the CoSTAR bid and the connectivity programme at costs substantially below the threshold of any cluster infrastructure investment. The convergence programme therefore offers higher return, lower cost and a faster route to demonstrable outcomes than cluster development in the short term.
The sequenced recommendation adopted in this report treats convergence economy development as the years one to three priority and city-centre cluster development as the years three to seven objective, conditional on the convergence programme generating sufficient institutional momentum, commercial credibility and earned income to anchor a broader cluster identity. The Screen North East studio investment, if it proceeds, is the most likely single catalyst for that transition: a screen studio creates the physical anchor, the supply chain demand and the national visibility that cluster development requires. Without it, cluster development remains a medium-term aspiration; with it, the convergence economy and the cluster become complementary rather than sequential.
The recommendation would be revisited if three conditions change. If the CoSTAR bid is unsuccessful and no equivalent national research and development investment is available to support the convergence programme, the case for leading with cluster infrastructure strengthens. If the energy sector's transition timeline accelerates significantly beyond current projections, reducing the window of competitive advantage for convergence economy positioning, the urgency of cluster diversification increases. If the Joint Board is not established or proves unable to exercise the cross-boundary mandate the convergence programme requires, the governance precondition for both options is absent and neither can proceed as described.
E.2 Selection of options
The cluster and convergence options described above were developed from a wider initial assessment that included four numbered options. Options 2 and 3 were interim variants that did not survive analytical scrutiny at the working stage: Option 2 was a freelance-first strategy that correctly identified the Tier 3 population as the majority of the business base but provided no mechanism for addressing the convergence tier; Option 3 was a screen industry-led strategy that depended entirely on the Screen North East studio investment proceeding, a precondition too uncertain to carry the weight of a regional strategy. Neither is developed further in this document.
The Place-Based Spine (Option 4) remains a relevant framework for the Local Outcomes Improvement Plan narrative and for community regeneration funding applications. Its emphasis on place identity, cultural hubs in Portsoy and Banff, and the connective infrastructure between rural practitioners and the city-centre creative core provides the strongest narrative for LOIP outcome domains and for Heritage Lottery Fund and Levelling Up successor funding. It is not an alternative to the convergence programme; it is the cultural resilience complement to it, addressing the Tier 4 and Tier 1 populations that the convergence programme does not primarily serve. The Creative Industries Working Group's stated commitment to a place focus regardless of option is well-judged: place identity is the characteristic that most clearly differentiates this region from every urban creative economy comparator, and it should anchor the investment prospectus regardless of which development pathway is followed.
E.3 Governance vehicle assessment
The five recommendations in Part Ten each require a lead institution and one or more enabling partners. The choice of lead vehicle is not merely administrative: it determines the speed at which each recommendation can move, the funding streams it can access, and the degree of cross-boundary authority it carries. This section sets out the realistic governance options for each recommendation, the considerations for and against each, and the vehicle recommended on the basis of available evidence.
How to read this assessment
Three vehicle types appear throughout. An existing single-authority vehicle means Aberdeen City Council or Aberdeenshire Council acting independently through their existing culture, economic development or skills functions. A national programme vehicle means Creative Scotland, Scottish Enterprise, Skills Development Scotland or a UK-wide programme such as CoSTAR or UKRI Creative Clusters acting as lead. A joint vehicle means either the proposed Joint Cultural and Creative Economy Board (if established under Recommendation 4) or an ad hoc partnership between two or more of the above. Speed, cost and authority trade off against each other across these types: single-authority vehicles can act fastest but carry the least cross-boundary legitimacy; national programme vehicles carry the most funding weight but move slowest and on their own timetables; joint vehicles are most appropriate for the cross-boundary work this report identifies but require the governance infrastructure that Recommendation 4 is designed to create.
The assessment therefore has a sequencing implication that runs throughout: Recommendation 4 (establish the Joint Board) is the precondition for the effective delivery of Recommendations 1, 2 and 5. This is not an argument for delay; it is an argument for treating Recommendation 4 as the first act in year one, not the last.
Recommendation 1: Full-spectrum measurement (NES Culture Data Hub)

Recommended vehicle: Joint Board lead with Creative Scotland as data standards partner. Until the Joint Board is established, both local authorities should jointly commission the first iteration, with a commitment to transfer governance to the Board on establishment. The NES Culture Data Hub should be designed from the outset to feed into Creative Scotland's national data infrastructure to avoid creating a parallel system that requires separate maintenance.
Confirmation needed: whether Creative Scotland has expressed willingness to support a regional data hub of this kind, and whether the Culture Data Lab platform is technically suitable.
Recommendation 2: Creative Ecology Structural Fund


Recommended vehicle: Joint Board as administering body, with both local authorities as joint funders and Creative Scotland as co-funder, where organisations are on the pathway to MYF eligibility. The ACC Catalyst Fund and the Aberdeenshire Council cultural support budget provide the initial capitalisation before a formal joint fund line is agreed. The structural fund should have an explicit exit condition (Creative Scotland MYF status or demonstrated income diversification) rather than operating as a rolling subsidy.
Confirmation needed: whether both local authorities have confirmed in principle that a joint cultural fund with a shared budget line is achievable within current governance arrangements, and whether the Catalyst Fund can be directed toward this purpose.

Recommendation 3: Joint CoSTAR bid


Recommended vehicle: RGU as lead applicant, University of Aberdeen as academic co-applicant, both local authorities as public sector co-applicants, Scottish Enterprise and Opportunity North East as named partners. This mirrors the structure of successful Creative Clusters bids elsewhere. The bid should be developed by the academic partners in close consultation with the Joint Board once established; before that, a working group drawn from the Creative Industries Working Group should hold the development process.
Confirmation needed: whether RGU's research office has formally agreed to lead the bid, and whether the University of Aberdeen is willing to be a co-applicant rather than a parallel lead.
Recommendation 4: Joint Cultural and Creative Economy Board
This recommendation is its own governance question, and the options for how the Board is constituted have materially different implications for its authority and effectiveness.

Recommended vehicle: Joint committee of both councils for year one, using the Creative Industries Working Group as its operational base, with a commitment to review the legal structure after the first year of operation. The joint committee model provides the democratic authority and budget-holding capacity the Board needs without the delay of a new legal entity. The one full-time equivalent officer resource should be jointly funded from year one; without dedicated capacity, no governance structure will be effective regardless of its legal form.
Confirmation needed: whether both councils' legal and democratic services have confirmed that a joint committee with a shared budget line is achievable under current legislation, and what the minimum lead time for establishment would be.
Recommendation 5: Creative economy alignment with energy transition

Recommended vehicle: Joint Board as creative economy lead, ETZ as energy sector lead, ONE as economic development convener, with a formal programme agreement between the three. The four specific actions -the NES Creative Economy Narrative, the Aberdeen-Dundee Corridor, the Creative Career Bridge and the market development programme -have different natural leads and should be assigned explicitly rather than held collectively. The Narrative is a Joint Board function. The Corridor is an RGU/University of Aberdeen academic partnership function. The Career Bridge is a Scottish Enterprise and employer-funded function. The market development programme is an ETZ and ONE function, tested against the convergence research findings from Recommendation 1 before committing to full delivery.
Confirmation needed: whether ETZ and ONE have been consulted on their roles in this recommendation and whether both have expressed willingness to participate in a jointly governed programme.
E.4 The sequencing logic
Reading the five governance vehicle assessments together, the sequencing logic for year one becomes clear. The actions that can proceed without the Joint Board being established are Recommendation 3 (CoSTAR bid, led by the universities) and the initial data collection phase of Recommendation 1 (commissioned jointly by both local authorities). The actions that require the Joint Board are the structural fund (Recommendation 2), the full measurement governance (Recommendation 1), and the creative economy elements of the energy transition programme (Recommendation 5).
Recommendation 4 is therefore not one recommendation among five of equal weight. It is the precondition for three of the others. The most important single governance decision the Regional Economic Partnership can make is to establish the Joint Board in year one, with dedicated officer resource and a shared budget line, before the window created by the CoSTAR timeline closes.
The outer time boundary is June 2026, when CoSTAR satellite funding confirmation is expected. A bid that arrives at that point without a functioning joint governance structure and without both local authorities confirmed as co-signatories will be weaker than one that arrives with both in place. The Joint Board does not need to be fully operational to strengthen the bid; it needs to exist, to have agreed its mandate, and to have appointed its officer resource. That is achievable between now and June 2026 if both councils treat it as the first act rather than a downstream consequence of the other recommendations.
