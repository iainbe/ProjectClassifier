EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Our Year Wakefield District 2024 was a £4.2 million strategic intervention that transformed the understanding of how cultural investment creates lasting community change across Wakefield District. The programme was designed to embed cultural democracy principles throughout council services and community development, empowering residents as co-creators of cultural activity while simultaneously addressing health inequalities, educational disadvantage, economic development, and social cohesion across an entire district.
The programme challenged the traditional relationship between cultural institutions and communities. Rather than "democratising culture" by making existing cultural forms more accessible, Our Year embraced "cultural democracy", empowering residents to become co-creators of cultural activity that reflected their own values, experiences, and priorities. This distinction proved transformational, moving beyond consultation to embed genuine power-sharing in cultural decision-making across all programme strands.
Grounded in principles of "proportionate universalism" adopted from the Marmot Review, Our Year recognised that effective community development requires interventions delivered at a scale and intensity proportionate to need. The programme created a comprehensive cultural ecosystem designed to reach people in every ward community across Wakefield District, while providing targeted support for communities experiencing the greatest barriers to engagement. This systematic approach contrasted with traditional models of cultural investment that concentrate resources in single large-scale projects or established institutions.
Proving cultural democracy at scale
Our Year's most significant achievement lies in demonstrating that cultural democracy principles can operate effectively across an entire local authority area. The programme mobilised extraordinary levels of civic engagement that extended far beyond passive participation to represent genuine community ownership. This mobilisation was supported by a network of Community Partners, creating lasting social infrastructure that continues to distribute opportunities and ensure programme reach extends into every community.
The Culture Grants programme exemplified this democratic approach in practice. Rather than traditional top-down commissioning, the programme devolved decision-making power directly to communities, funding community groups and local artists to deliver projects reflecting their own priorities. The resulting community co-investment rate provides compelling evidence of genuine buy-in that significantly exceeds typical leverage rates for cultural regeneration programmes. This financial commitment from communities themselves proves that Our Year was not simply a council initiative but a shared district-wide endeavour.
The programme's success in achieving authentic co-creation was embedded across all strands. Community groups were able to promote events through partner networks, showcase work at major festivals, and contribute stories to heritage platforms, creating a virtuous circle where investment in one area generated value across the entire programme. The programme also delivered high-quality cultural experiences through flagship events and major commissions, demonstrating that community empowerment and professional excellence can operate together rather than in tension.
Improving the lives and wellbeing of communities across the district
Our Year has catalysed changes in how Wakefield Council operates, embedding cultural democracy principles into corporate strategy and fostering cross-departmental collaboration. The programme's most profound impact lies in proving that cultural investment can function as integrated public policy rather than separate cultural activity.
The transformation is evidenced by the formal recognition of cultural participation in the Council's State of the District report, which now explicitly acknowledges that cultural activities improve residents' quality of life. This represents a policy achievement, elevating the culture-wellbeing link from a service-level aspiration to a district-wide strategic priority. The integration of wellbeing considerations into corporate strategy demonstrates how Our Year's evidence base has influenced high-level strategic thinking across the authority.
The programme's systematic approach to removing barriers exemplifies how cultural programming can address structural inequalities. Community-led programming prioritised social connection and creative participation, with programmes demonstrating how cultural and movement-based activities can engage entire communities in health and wellbeing initiatives while building lasting social connections that continue beyond funded periods.
Increasing happiness with local areas as a place to live across our communities
Our Year has created lasting physical and social infrastructure that continues to enhance community pride and place attachment. The programme's approach to place-making moved beyond temporary interventions to create permanent assets that reflect authentic community identity and values.
The Crossings & Gateways programme's deep community co-design process engaged community groups in developing public art commissions, ensuring final works were genuinely rooted in local identity rather than imposed external interpretations. In Knottingley, workshops with residents, including former factory employees, explored the heritage of Bagley's Glass, with their creative collages woven directly into the final mural design by award-winning artist Peachzz, inspired by the town's mining heritage and bright future. In South Elmsall, the project reached over 100 local residents from primary schools, Air Cadets groups and creative writing and poetry groups, plus drop-ins for people living nearby to the mural. This approach created both physical landmarks and strengthened social connections between neighbours who had never previously collaborated.
The programme's success in enhancing perceptions is evidenced by resident responses to major events, with overwhelming agreement that activities made them see Wakefield district as more creative. This shift in perception has been supported by exceptional media coverage that positions Wakefield district as a model for community-led cultural development and ambition, creating lasting reputational benefits that continue to attract investment and partnership opportunities.
Providing our communities with access to quality creative skills and learning opportunities
Our Year has created lasting educational infrastructure that continues to benefit young people throughout Wakefield district. The Schools Programme's four-pillar approach, combining curriculum resources, teacher training, financial support, and practical tools, represents a complete system for embedding cultural learning that operates beyond the programme period.
The programme's success in engaging schools was achieved through systematic removal of barriers rather than simply expanding provision. The Access Bursary's targeted support directly addressed educational inequality, ensuring that family income did not determine access to high-quality creative learning. This approach demonstrates how cultural programming can function as social policy, addressing structural disadvantages through accessible, community-led interventions.
The creation of permanent resources through the Wakefield Curriculum and trained teacher cohort has built sustainable capacity that continues to benefit the district's educational landscape. The curriculum's immediate uptake demonstrates practical value, while the involvement of multiple cultural organisations in its development ensures it reflects the full breadth of the district's cultural offer.
The international recognition gained through the National Library of Korea's request to publish a case study of summer s’cool’s educational approach as an example of global best practice validates the innovation and transferability of the model, placing Wakefield's approach alongside international exemplars.
Increasing the strength of our creative sector to provide quality employment opportunities
Our Year has transformed the sustainability and connectivity of Wakefield's creative sector through trust-based commissioning that respects artistic expertise while building professional capacity. The programme's approach moved beyond simply purchasing creative services to building long-term sector resilience and creating genuine employment pathways.
The exceptional involvement of creative practitioners demonstrates both the sector's appetite for engagement and the effectiveness of the trust-based approach. Artists report that they can now "keep doing it well around here" rather than leaving the district, indicating a shift in professional sustainability. The growth of professional networks has created enhanced potential for collaboration, knowledge sharing, and innovation.
Business engagement through creative residencies has created new models for cultural-economic integration that extend far beyond traditional sponsorship. The residencies with NHS Pinderfields, OE Electrics, and Wakefield Exchange have created templates for ongoing collaboration, demonstrating how cultural investment can support business objectives and community wellbeing simultaneously while providing ongoing visibility for commissioned works.
Supporting the health of the district's economy by attracting visitors, investment and wider support
Our Year has generated economic returns while creating new models for business engagement that move beyond transactional relationships to genuine cross-sector collaboration. The programme's visitor economy impact represents exceptional value for money, while systematic investment in local suppliers has created direct economic benefits that continue to support jobs and build business resilience.
The programme's innovative commissioning framework has created new approaches to community wealth building, with significant programme spend directed to Wakefield District-based suppliers. This systematic prioritisation of local procurement has moved beyond traditional procurement to prioritise social value, creating a more equitable system that directly supports the local economy while building community assets.
The Leap Club's innovative approach to business engagement demonstrates how cultural programmes can serve as platforms for business network development, creating value for both cultural and economic objectives. The diversity of membership has created opportunities for knowledge sharing and cross-sector innovation that extend beyond the programme itself.
Supporting the fight against the climate emergency
Our Year has moved environmental considerations from secondary concerns to core components of cultural planning across the district. The programme's approach focused on building long-term capacity within the cultural sector to embed sustainable practices while demonstrating how major events can contribute to climate emergency responses.
The development and distribution of the Positive Impacts Toolkit has built sector-wide capacity for sustainable practice, creating a shift in how sustainability is approached in cultural planning from a secondary consideration to a core part of event design processes. This cross-sector learning has enhanced the knowledge and confidence of both Council officers and cultural organisations, building the capacity of the local cultural sector to deliver more environmentally responsible projects in the future.
Regional and national significance
Our Year's success in achieving community buy-in aligns with Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) findings that identify local community engagement as a critical success factor for culture-led regeneration. The programme's integrated approach across multiple strands exemplifies international recommendations for combining approaches rather than concentrating resources in single projects, while its exceptional community co-investment rate provides evidence for the effectiveness of devolved, community-led cultural programming.
Our Year's international reach extended beyond educational recognition. The Light Up Wakefield festival attracted international artists and commissions, positioning Wakefield on the global cultural map and demonstrating the district's capacity to host world-class creative activity. Examples of this include:

The programme offers valuable learning for West Yorkshire Combined Authority's Gainshare investments, demonstrating how cultural democracy principles can deliver against health and wellbeing, economic development, and community cohesion objectives simultaneously. The systematic approach to cross-service integration provides a template for place-based policy development that recognises cultural investment as integrated public policy rather than separate activity.
For Arts Council England, Our Year demonstrates the power of cultural democracy at scale, providing evidence for the effectiveness of community-focused funding programmes. Wakefield’s success in mobilising community co-investment and creating sustainable partnerships offers valuable insights for national cultural policy development.
Sustainable legacy and continuing impact
The programme has created lasting partnerships between schools and the district's cultural sector, while the network of Community Partners continues to function as vital social infrastructure. Physical infrastructure improvements provide lasting community benefits that extend far beyond the programme period, creating permanent assets that continue to enhance community pride and accessibility.
Another significant legacy lies in the sustainable community infrastructure it has created and the shift in how cultural development operates across the district. The community-led consortium which bid for Creative People & Places funding from Arts Council England, although unsuccessful in this instance, validates Our Year's cultural democracy approach. The transition from reliance on council-led intervention to the confidence to established community-owned infrastructure and governance mechanisms demonstrates that the programme's principles have taken root and will continue to drive cultural development.
Strategic recommendations
Our Year has proven that when cultural investment is grounded in genuine community empowerment, systematic cross-service integration, and authentic power-sharing, it becomes a tool for social justice, economic development, and community resilience. Based on this success, the following strategic recommendations emerge:
Embed cultural democracy as a core principle
Make the principles of proportionate universalism and community co-creation central to all future cultural investment, building on the proven success of approaches that achieved exceptional community co-investment rates and genuine community ownership.
Formalise cross-service integration
Create permanent structures to maintain the cross-departmental collaboration pioneered during Our Year, ensuring culture remains integrated with health, education, and economic development strategies rather than operating in isolation.
Maintain community infrastructure
Invest in maintaining and developing the networks of Community Partners and creative practitioners as permanent assets for ongoing community and cultural development, recognising these relationships as infrastructure for democratic participation.
Document and share innovation
Commission detailed documentation of the policy innovation processes that enabled Our Year's success, creating a resource that can inform cultural policy development nationally and support other local authorities in developing similar approaches.
Establish regional knowledge transfer mechanisms
The programme's innovative approaches, particularly the integrated Schools Programme model and business residency framework, should be systematically documented and shared through WYCA's Knowledge Transfer networks to support peer learning across West Yorkshire authorities and maximise the regional impact of successful interventions. Future cultural programmes should integrate environmental impact assessment from inception, aligning with WYCA's net-zero commitments.

1. Introduction and methodology
1.1 Our Year vision and strategic framework
Original strategic aims
Our Year - Wakefield District 2024 was conceived as a transformative programme designed to embed cultural democracy principles across council services and community development. The programme's vision articulated five core objectives that would fundamentally shift how cultural activity was conceived, commissioned, and delivered throughout Wakefield:
	•	Delivering activities across all 366 days of 2024: Moving beyond traditional event-based cultural programming to create sustained cultural engagement that became part of the district's everyday life and identity.
	•	Engaging multiple audiences across demographic and geographic boundaries: Systematic approaches to ensuring cultural participation reached all 21 wards, with particular focus on communities experiencing the greatest barriers to engagement.
	•	Utilising existing connections and creating new collaborations: Building on established community assets while creating innovative partnerships between sectors, organisations, and communities that had not previously collaborated.
	•	Developing cultural communities across the district: Empowering residents to become cultural leaders and co-creators rather than passive recipients of cultural programming.
	•	Embedding sustainability principles throughout delivery: Working toward environmental sustainability alongside social and economic sustainability through community ownership and cross-sector collaboration.
The programme's approach was grounded in cultural democracy principles that positioned communities as active agents in cultural creation rather than consumers of cultural products. This approach recognised that sustainable cultural development required fundamental shifts in power relationships, with communities gaining agency in defining, creating, and evaluating cultural activity that reflected their priorities and aspirations.
Cultural democracy and co-creation principles
Our Year's approach was explicitly informed by principles of "proportionate universalism" set out in the Marmot Review of 2010 and subsequently reinforced in the 2020 review of progress. Our Year's focus on ways in which cultural investment can address health outcomes and other economic, environmental and social inequalities makes an essential distinction between "democratising culture" (making existing cultural forms more accessible) and "cultural democracy" (empowering communities to create cultural forms that reflect their own values and experiences, delivered at a scale and intensity proportionate to their level of need.)
In so doing, it positioned community knowledge, creativity, and leadership as central to all programme strands. Co-creation and co-design principles were embedded across programme delivery through systematic methodologies that ensured community input at conception, development, and evaluation stages rather than in consultation after key decisions had been made. This approach was evident in the Crossings & Gateways public art programme, where community engagement informed artistic briefs from the outset, and the Culture Grants programme, where 96.4% of participants reported feeling able to contribute ideas to project organisation.
Our Year's co-creation approach recognised that effective community cultural development requires more than participation; it demands genuine power-sharing in cultural decision-making. This principle influenced commissioning frameworks, partnership development, evaluation methodologies, and resource allocation across all programme strands.
Connection to Gainshare objectives and wider Wakefield strategies
Our Year was strategically aligned with West Yorkshire Combined Authority's Gainshare objectives, particularly those focused on improving health and wellbeing, creating good jobs and career opportunities, and building strong communities. 
The programme's approach provided innovative methodologies for achieving these objectives through community empowerment and creative participation: its systematic reach was supported by a network of 126 Community Partners distributed across all 21 wards, ensuring cultural development was embedded across all communities. Integration with the district's health and wellbeing strategy was achieved through systematic approaches to community empowerment that recognised cultural participation as a determinant of individual and community wellbeing. Our Year also aligned with the Council's transformation agenda, providing opportunities to test innovative approaches to community engagement, cross-service collaboration, and evidence-based policy development that could inform broader municipal strategy.
1.2 Programme scale and strategic investment context
Investment scale and local authority context
Our Year - Wakefield District 2024 represents a £4.2 million strategic investment in cultural infrastructure and community development, of which £3.9 million (89%) was directly invested in Our Year programming. 
This is a substantial commitment that reflects strategic recognition of culture as a driver of community development, economic growth, and cross-service integration. The investment model was deliberately structured as a one-off strategic intervention designed to create lasting infrastructure, build community capacity, and establish sustainable ways of working that would continue beyond the funded period. The Council's investment was conceived as seed capital for long-term cultural and community development, with success measured not only by immediate outputs but by sustainable systems, relationships, and assets created.
External funding and partnership investment
Wakefield Council's investment featured the alignment of core budgets over two financial years, alongside specific growth investment, and was significantly amplified through external funding and partnership contributions of £1.965 million – 47% of the total:
	•	£500,000 Arts Council England
	•	£850,000 West Yorkshire Combined Authority
	•	£168,410 National Heritage Lottery Fund, from a full £245,623 grant for a longer term programme
	•	£233,178 UK Shared Prosperity Fund
	•	£90,000 Public Health at Wakefield Council
	•	£74,373 sponsorship (including Leap Club members, Haribo, Tileyard North, YPO)
	•	£2.4 million Wakefield Council Arts Culture Leisure Budgets
The scale of external funding demonstrates the Council's success in deploying a "bold but cautious investment strategy" which successfully attracted additional investment and validates the strategic approach through peer recognition from national and regional funding bodies.
Programme design and resource allocation
Investment was distributed across programme strands using a portfolio approach that mitigated and distributed risk while maximising learning and community engagement opportunities. This approach contrasted with traditional cultural investment models that concentrate resources in single large-scale projects or established cultural institutions.

Our Year did not contribute capital investment to the development of Wakefield Exchange, and is therefore outside the scope of Our Year's financial assessment,
Integration with Wakefield's strategic priorities
Our Year was explicitly designed to support delivery of multiple Council strategic objectives simultaneously, demonstrating how cultural investment can function as integrated public policy rather than separate cultural activity. The programme aligned with Economic Development, Education, Health and Wellbeing, and Community Development priorities through systematic cross-service collaboration and shared outcome measurement.
	•	Integration with the Council's economic development strategy was achieved through procurement which prioritised local suppliers, creative sector employment creation, and systematic skills development programmes. 
	•	The programme's contribution to education strategy was demonstrated through the Schools Programme's systematic engagement between schools and cultural organisations and creation of the Wakefield Place-Based Curriculum as a permanent educational resource.

This scale of public interaction, achieved through a single-year, local authority-led programme, is exceptional. It created an incredibly rare and valuable opportunity for large-scale social impact research.
1.3 International benchmarking
Our Year's cultural democracy approach aligns perfectly with the OECD (2025) findings that successful culture-led regeneration requires, "buy-in from local communities as a critical success factor" and that, "long-term regeneration is incremental and often involves multiple approaches over time." The programme's investment across multiple strands exemplifies the OECD’s recommendation to combine approaches rather than concentrating resources in single large-scale projects.

2. Improving the lives and wellbeing of communities across the district
A primary strategic aim of Our Year was to improve the lives and wellbeing of communities across Wakefield District by fostering a more democratic, participatory, and accessible cultural landscape. The programme was designed not merely to provide cultural experiences, but to empower residents as co-creators, build lasting social capital, and embed a focus on wellbeing into the fabric of the district. The final programme data demonstrates that Our Year was exceptionally successful in achieving these ambitions, creating a powerful legacy of community capacity and engagement.
2.1 Our communities are empowered to deliver and co-create cultural activity
Our Year successfully demonstrated that a strategic shift towards cultural democracy can unlock profound levels of community agency and ownership. The programme moved beyond traditional consultation to embed co-creation in its core delivery, providing residents and local organisations with the resources, skills, and confidence to shape their own cultural lives.
Culture Grants
The Culture Grants programme was a cornerstone of this approach. The programme:
Supported 61 community groups and 15 local artists and creative organisations to deliver their own projects.
Mobilised £359,699 in match funding, representing a 43.1% co-investment rate against its direct budget - a figure that significantly exceeds the 3-5% leverage typical of such programmes.
Embedded the principle of co-creation, with evaluation showing that 96.4% of grant participants felt they could genuinely contribute ideas to the organisation of their projects.
The impact of this devolved model was extraordinary. The financial commitment from the Council and external funders was met with a profound response from the community itself; proving that Our Year was not simply a council initiative, but a shared district-wide endeavour. 
Community Partners
This empowerment was systematically extended across all 21 wards through the Community Partners programme, which built a lasting network of 126 organisations. 
50% of surveyed partners reported organisational development and 55% agreed that Our Year helped publicise their activities to a wider audience, demonstrating effective integration with Wakefield's Economic Wellbeing Strategy 2022-2027, which identifies culture and creativity as priority sectors for economic growth. 
This network acted as a vital piece of social infrastructure, distributing opportunities and ensuring the programme's reach extended into every community. Rather than treating cultural development as separate from economic development, Our Year positioned culture as fundamental to inclusive economic growth that would benefit existing communities rather than displacing them, fulfilling the OECD's recommendation for building on grassroots infrastructure.
Volunteering
The ultimate evidence of this empowerment lies in the scale of active participation. Our Year inspired 1,524 individuals to volunteer, contributing 3,974 hours of their time. This remarkable mobilisation of community effort demonstrates a level of buy-in that goes far beyond simple audience attendance, representing a fundamental success in fostering a culture of active civic participation and shared ownership.
2.2 More residents take part in cultural activities
The programme's democratic and co-created approach was the engine for a dramatic increase in cultural participation in every community in Wakefield. Our Year delivered a total of 640,651 audience and participant engagements, surpassing its headline target of 615,000 and demonstrating the power of a truly district-wide cultural offer.
This success was driven by a combination of ambitious flagship events and a rich tapestry of local activity. The Festivals and Events programme was a major driver of engagement, attracting 214,352 attendees and achieving 107% of its target. Major commissions successfully drew huge crowds and created moments of shared civic experience; the flagship event, The Hatchling, brought a live audience of 30,272 people to the city centre, while the launch programme of the Wakefield Exchange engaged an estimated 93,000 people.
Crucially, the programme succeeded in reaching new and previously unengaged audiences. Survey data from The Hatchling revealed that for 30.5% of attendees, it was their first time attending a cultural event in Wakefield, a strong indicator of the programme's success in expanding the district's active cultural audience. This was underpinned by a systematic commitment to removing barriers. The Culture and Heritage Access Bursary provided direct financial support to schools to overcome transport and workshop costs, enabling students to access cultural experiences they would otherwise have missed.
Ward mapping analysis – "Everyone, Everywhere"
Our Year aimed to be for 'Everyone, Everywhere'. The programme was designed to ensure that activity was distributed throughout Wakefield, particularly in areas that had low cultural participation and perceptions. With so many strands contributing to Our Year activity, a centralised mechanism was needed to track ward level information across the programme:
	•	Each strand was asked to input their activities into a Ward Mapping document, and RAG rate them based on likelihood of delivery and level of resident engagement.
	•	IMD data and results of Wakefield's Participation and Perceptions survey were used to identify wards most likely to experience barriers to engagement.
	•	Scores were assigned to each activity based on likelihood and adjusted for double-counting.
	•	These figures were mapped against the IMD and Wakefield's Participation and Perceptions survey data to identify priority wards.
	•	Strands then targeted priority wards for increased delivery.
This exercise was conducted three times throughout the programme. IMD data was cross referenced with participation and perception survey findings to identify priority wards. Wards which demonstrated:
	•	A large percentage of residents experiencing deprivation
	•	Low cultural participation
	•	Low perceptions of culture
were identified as having barriers to engagement and labelled as a priority. The initial list of priority wards included:
	•	Airedale and Ferry Fryston
	•	Altofts and Whitwood
	•	Crofton, Ryhill and Walton (Ryhill only)
	•	Hemsworth
	•	Knottingley
	•	Normanton
	•	Pontefract South
	•	South Elmsall and South Kirkby
Strand activity was then targeted for delivery on these priority wards. Priorities were reviewed in February 2024 to reflect any evidence of increased activity. All wards saw an increase in activity throughout the programme, but some priority wards saw a significant increase in activity.

2.3 Culture, creativity, and physical activity improve the wellbeing of our communities
Our Year's approach to wellbeing improvement was grounded in community-led programming that recognised cultural participation as a determinant of individual and community wellbeing. The programme reached its many local participants and multiple new resident audiences, providing key lessons in designing and delivering future whole community programmes. Audience animation and partnership learning through marketing communications and mass engagement trials generated strong lessons in delivering both local impact and district-wide collaboration for culturally driven resident wellbeing.
However, it is important to note that Our Year was not designed as a health intervention programme with systematic measurement of health outcomes. The programme's primary focus was cultural democracy and community empowerment, with wellbeing benefits emerging as a secondary outcome rather than a measured primary objective. This distinction is crucial for understanding both the programme's achievements and the limitations of the available evidence.
Moving Festival: Active heritage and community health
The Moving Festival successfully demonstrated how cultural programming could integrate physical activity with heritage exploration, creating innovative approaches to community health and wellbeing. The programme engaged participants in discovering local history through active participation, combining walking and cycling activities with storytelling and heritage interpretation. This approach proved highly effective in reaching people who might not typically engage with either heritage activities or formal exercise programmes, creating new pathways to both cultural participation and physical wellbeing.
The festival delivered 47 managed walks and 70 cycling activities, exceeding its target and demonstrating strong community appetite for active heritage engagement. A key legacy achievement was training 32 new volunteer walk leaders, empowering residents to sustain health-improving activities in their communities long after the programme's conclusion. The programme also improved 2 cycling routes and engaged 62 new cyclists using the "Wonders of Wakefield" routes. Case study: When cultural programming removes systemic barriers
The Wakefield District Cycle Forum's "Wonders of Wakefield" routes programme provides a powerful example of how cultural programming can deliver infrastructure improvements that benefit whole communities beyond participants. The cycling programme encountered both infrastructure and accessibility barriers that limited participation. A physical A-frame barrier on a bridleway in Portobello prevented wheelchair users, parents with pushchairs, and tandem cyclists from accessing routes. As a community organiser explained:
"We've been trying for years to get rid of an A-frame. I ran a tandem and we couldn't actually get the tandem through."
The Cycle Forum used Our Year's profile to advocate for infrastructure change, leveraging the programme's visibility to finally address the long-standing barrier. The removal of the A-Frame brought about immediate and permanent accessibility improvements. On removal day, "two people with pushchairs came through it, and a wheelchair, it's all accessible now." The programme achieved intergenerational participation: "We had three generations - started off with a 10-year-old boy, then his mum and dad, then his grandad."
The programme attracted 192 participants, with 68 being completely new to the Forum. This case demonstrates how cultural programming can deliver infrastructure improvements that benefit whole communities beyond participants, showing that success requires addressing both programmatic barriers (equipment, booking systems) and physical barriers (infrastructure).

Beat the Street
The Beat the Street trial in Featherstone provides strong evidence of health and wellbeing engagement. The programme involved 2,856 participants (approximately 16% of Featherstone's population in the 2021 Census and 190% of the original target), targeted seven primary schools as well as community groups and workplaces. Partners from across the District came together to shape the programme through their collective expertise, insights and knowledge of communities in Featherstone.
During the four-week game period, over 30 events were delivered or promoted, ranging from Knit & Natter to Pebble Decorating, Mindfulness and Walking groups, specifically targeting families and people aged 55+ - priority groups within Wakefield's Health and Wellbeing Strategy. Individual success stories from Beat the Street demonstrate the programme's potential impact. One participant reported:
"I hadn't walked regularly for years due to mobility issues, but the game gave me motivation and I discovered routes I could manage. Three months later, I'm still walking daily."
Another noted:
"My children dragged me out every day - it became family time we'd never had before. We're still doing weekend walks together."
Beat the Street demonstrates both the potential and the challenges of integrating health and cultural programming. While the immediate engagement was exceptional, the limited follow-up data highlights the importance of systematic evaluation design from programme inception. Individual testimonials, while powerful, represent self-selected positive responses and cannot be generalised to the broader participant population. Future programmes seeking to make health claims would benefit from more robust baseline data collection, control groups, and longitudinal tracking to enable confident attribution of health outcomes to cultural interventions.

4.2 Schools are provided with support to deliver cultural education
A key legacy of Our Year is the comprehensive and sustainable support system it has built to empower schools and embed cultural education into the curriculum for the long term. The Schools Programme was delivered through four integrated elements that created a complete ecosystem for cultural learning.
The four pillars of the Schools Programme
SPARK - The Local Cultural Education Partnership
The foundation of the Schools Programme was SPARK, the Local Cultural Education Partnership delivered in partnership with Yorkshire Sculpture Park as part of YSP's National Portfolio Organisation (NPO) commitment to Arts Council England. YSP received NPO funding specifically to coordinate and support SPARK, providing the governance structure and financial management that enabled the partnership to function effectively. This partnership brought together ten leading Wakefield district cultural organisations to collaborate on curriculum development and delivery.
The Wakefield Place-Based Curriculum
Building on the SPARK partnership, Our Year funded the development of the Wakefield Curriculum, a permanent, place-based creative learning resource. YSP subcontracted SPARK to deliver this curriculum development, with all cultural partners contributing content that reflected their expertise and local assets. This digital hub of high-quality, locally relevant resources proved immediately valuable, with 1,802 individual resource downloads in its first 15 weeks of operation.
The curriculum represents a fundamental innovation in place-based education, providing teachers with materials specifically rooted in Wakefield's history, geography, and cultural assets. Resources range from heritage trails that teach map-reading skills to creative writing prompts based on local stories, from science investigations using local environmental data to art projects inspired by the district's industrial heritage.
Continuing Professional Development Programme
To ensure the curriculum was used effectively, Our Year invested directly in building teacher confidence and skills. A dedicated CPD programme trained 146 teachers from Wakefield schools, exceeding the target of 120. The training was delivered through SPARK partners and focused on practical, hands-on skills, with one teacher commenting they "loved how we can apply to settings straight away."
An additional element was the development and distribution of School Creativity Kits - an A to Z of creativity issued to every single school in the district. These kits contained standalone activities contributed by all cultural partners, providing teachers with immediate, practical resources they could use in any setting. The kits were distributed alongside the CPD programme, ensuring teachers had both the skills and resources to deliver creative learning.
The CPD programme addressed a critical barrier to cultural education: teacher confidence. Many primary school teachers, while highly skilled in core subjects, lack confidence in delivering creative activities. The programme provided practical training in areas like storytelling, basic drama techniques, and creative writing, giving teachers the tools they needed to bring the Wakefield Curriculum to life in their classrooms.
Access Bursary Programme
The fourth pillar was the Culture and Heritage Access Bursary, providing direct financial support to remove barriers to cultural participation. This programme supported 61 schools and enabled 1,859 students, including 624 pupils eligible for Pupil Premium and 616 with SEND, to access cultural experiences they would otherwise have missed. The bursary was specifically designed to support students at risk of exclusion, ensuring that the most disadvantaged young people could benefit from high-quality cultural learning opportunities.
Systematic integration and impact
This integrated approach successfully engaged 162 schools Wakefield-wide - more than double the original target of 75 - resulting in 15,128 participation opportunities for pupils. Its success lay in combining multiple support mechanisms: 
High-quality resources (the Wakefield Curriculum)
A skilled teaching workforce (CPD programme)
Financial support to remove barriers (Access Bursary)
Practical tools (creativity kits).
The Schools Programme's reach was further extended through integration with other Our Year strands, with schools participating in Nurture & Nourish food-based learning activities and Moving Festival heritage walks, demonstrating how cultural education could seamlessly connect with health and wellbeing objectives across the curriculum.
The systematic removal of financial barriers was crucial to the programme's success in reaching priority groups. The Access Bursary represented a fundamental shift from expecting schools to find funding for cultural activities to the council proactively removing barriers to participation. The targeted support directly addressed educational inequality and ensured that a child's postcode or family income did not determine their access to high-quality creative learning.
The Schools Programme demonstrated particular effectiveness in reaching communities with previously low levels of cultural engagement. The programme achieved intensive engagement (measured as nine or more sessions across multiple cultural partners) in 13 schools, of which nine (69%) were located in priority wards identified through IMD data and the Participation and Perceptions survey as having both low cultural participation and low perceptions of culture. Within priority wards, the programme achieved sustained engagement across multiple school types: 
Three highly engaged schools were within Airedale & Ferry Fryston (Airedale Junior School with 16 sessions, Airedale Infant School with 13 sessions)
A further three from South Elmsall & South Kirkby (Moorthorpe Primary with 14 sessions, South Kirkby Common Road Infant and Nursery with 13 sessions, South Elmsall Carlton Junior and Infant with 11 sessions)
Two more were from Crofton, Ryhill & Walton (Crofton Academy with 14 sessions, Crofton Infant School with 12 sessions). The other four highly engaged schools were all primary schools concentrated in more affluent areas (Wakefield North, Wakefield East, Wakefield South). 
This distribution demonstrates that the systematic targeting of priority wards through the Community Partners network and targeted bursary support successfully drove intensive, multi-partner cultural engagement in communities where residents had previously reported lowest levels of cultural participation and most negative perceptions of culture as "for people like us."
The role of Yorkshire Sculpture Park (YSP) as the coordinating delivery partner was crucial to this success. As an established Arts Council England National Portfolio Organisation (NPO) with both core funding and experience in education delivery, YSP provided the infrastructure and expertise needed to manage complex partnerships between multiple cultural organisations and schools. Its coordination ensured that the work with schools maintained quality and coherence across all elements while building on existing relationships and expertise within the cultural sector.
Significant internal council support included the introduction of a dedicated team member from Wakefield Council (Schools and Volunteering Officer), providing schools with the confidence and resources to re-engage with cultural learning after the disruption of the pandemic, when many had scaled back their cultural offer due to budget pressures and logistical challenges. The programme was effectively co-designed with partners through a process of sharing data and creating a bursary programme together. The collaborative approach successfully catalysed latent capability and capacity within the sector, going across silos and providing a licence to experiment and innovate within a defined horizon of a sprint rather than ongoing business as usual. 
The programme culminated in an inaugural district Schools Conference, addressed by the Service Director for Education, that showcased the achievements of participating schools and teachers, providing a platform for sharing learning and strengthening the networks that will sustain cultural education collaboration in every Wakefield school. 
Sustainable legacy
The Schools programme has created lasting partnerships between schools and the district's cultural sector, with venues reporting increased bookings and deeper relationships with local schools. This systematic approach ensures that place-based cultural education becomes part of the fabric of education in every part of Wakefield, rather than an occasional add-on activity.
The success of SPARK as a partnership model has continued beyond Our Year, with the Council putting continued effort and resource into collaborative working between cultural organisations and schools, and The Hepworth Wakefield expressing interest in developing major projects using the sculptural resources and mining heritage themes established through the place-based curriculum. The four-pillar model provides a complete, replicable system for embedding cultural learning that continues to operate beyond the programme period, creating a sustainable foundation for cultural education across Wakefield District.
The Wakefield Curriculum's digital format ensures it can be continuously updated and expanded, creating a living resource that grows with the district's cultural development. The involvement of 10 cultural organisations in its development ensures that it reflects the full breadth of the district's cultural offer, from established institutions like Yorkshire Sculpture Park to grassroots community organisations.

This level of engagement created a powerful multiplier effect, with each practitioner becoming an advocate for cultural participation within their own networks and communities.

Creative sector case study: Trust as creative currency
The experience of individual artists within Our Year revealed both structural challenges facing the creative sector and the transformational impact of trust-based commissioning. Artists faced fundamental sustainability challenges, with the grant requirements to work locally coming in conflict with the limited number of suitable venues within Wakefield. As one artist explained: 'When I lost my space, trying to replace that in Wakefield... was really difficult. I found space for free in Huddersfield, Barnsley... but I could not find it in Wakefield.' 
Despite these challenges, artists praised the trust-based approach of the Our Year team: "They've trusted artists... ‘we're gonna trust you to do your thing, you're the artist’." 
This created confidence to take creative risks and engage with "underrepresented audiences." The impact was transformational for individual practitioners:
"If I couldn't carry on doing what it was to be me, I'd have to move - but now I can keep doing it well around here."
The sector recognised that professional sustainability enables community benefit, with artists who took part in a round table acknowledging the increased strength of our creative sector. Our Year demonstrated greatly increased levels of sector connectivity and growth, involving 1,392 creative practitioners (348% of target); while the Creative Wakefield network grew by 225 members over the year.
This case demonstrates that:
Artist retention requires both financial support and suitable infrastructure - funding without venues creates systemic bottlenecks. 
Trust-based commissioning that respects artistic expertise delivers higher quality outcomes than bureaucratic oversight.
The creative sector's strength directly correlates with community wellbeing, making artist retention a strategic priority for place-based development.

Despite these challenges, artists praised the trust-based approach of the Our Year team: "They've trusted artists... ‘we're gonna trust you to do your thing, you're the artist’." 
This created confidence to take creative risks and engage with "underrepresented audiences." The impact was transformational for individual practitioners:
"If I couldn't carry on doing what it was to be me, I'd have to move - but now I can keep doing it well around here."
The sector recognised that professional sustainability enables community benefit, with artists who took part in a round table acknowledging the increased strength of our creative sector. Our Year demonstrated greatly increased levels of sector connectivity and growth, involving 1,392 creative practitioners (348% of target); while the Creative Wakefield network grew by 225 members over the year.
This case demonstrates that:
Artist retention requires both financial support and suitable infrastructure - funding without venues creates systemic bottlenecks. 
Trust-based commissioning that respects artistic expertise delivers higher quality outcomes than bureaucratic oversight.
The creative sector's strength directly correlates with community wellbeing, making artist retention a strategic priority for place-based development.

Our Year's partnership engagement with Unlimited as a Delivery Partner created vital professional development opportunities specifically for disabled artists, addressing the significant barriers this community faces in accessing mainstream commissioning opportunities. The business residency programme provided six disabled artists with intensive, supported placements that enabled them to develop new skills, scale up their practice, and create public work for the first time.
For many participants, this represented a crucial career breakthrough, with one artist noting it offered "exciting career progression" through the opportunity to create public art in a supported environment. The programme's strength lay in providing not just financial support but comprehensive professional development, including mentorship, technical assistance, and the infrastructure needed for disabled artists to engage meaningfully with business environments.
By embedding disabled artists within mainstream organisations rather than segregating them in disability-specific contexts, the residencies challenged assumptions about where and how disabled artists can work while creating authentic opportunities for career advancement. This approach demonstrates how targeted support for underrepresented artists can create lasting change, both for individual practitioners and for the broader cultural sector's understanding of inclusive practice.
Equality, diversity and inclusion in professional development - UNBOUND
Beyond direct audience engagement, the investment in Our Year also catalysed significant professional development within the creative community through programmes like UNBOUND. This artist development initiative, directly maximising opportunities presented by Our Year’s public art commissioning, successfully addressed challenges faced by artists working in public spaces. A cohort of 12 artists from Wakefield and West Yorkshire benefited from artist-led workshops, mentorship from experienced professionals delivering live Our Year mural commissions, and crucial networking opportunities. Participants reported significant gains in skills, confidence, and career direction, with several artists securing new commissions and opportunities post-programme. Notably, UNBOUND achieved strong Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion (EDI) outcomes, with 100% female participation, 42% identifying as bisexual, and 42% from ethnic minority backgrounds, reflecting a commitment to fostering a diverse and inclusive creative workforce.

6. Supporting health of the district's economy by attracting visitors, investment and wider support
Our Year was designed not only as a cultural and social programme but as a strategic economic intervention intended to attract visitors, stimulate local business, and enhance Wakefield's reputation as a creative destination. The final programme data confirms that it was highly successful in this aim, generating a multi-million-pound boost to the visitor economy, directly investing over £1.6 million into local supply chains, and securing a national profile that provides a strong foundation for future economic growth.
6.1 More tourists visiting, staying longer and spending more
The programme's major events were a powerful driver for the district's visitor economy, attracting significant audiences and generating substantial local spend, catalysed by The Festivals and Events programme and the flagship event within it, The Hatchling:
	•	Festivals and Events: 214,352 attendees across the programme are estimated to have generated a direct economic impact of between £11.36 million and £12.22 million.
	•	The Hatchling: 30,272 attendees with an estimated direct economic impact of between £1.60 million and £1.73 million for the local economy.
Combined, these two strands alone generated a conservative estimated visitor economy impact of between £12.96 million and £13.95 million. This represents a significant return on the programme's investment and a major boost for local hospitality, retail, and transport businesses.
STEAM visitor data
The West Yorkshire Combined Authority and its partners, including Wakefield Council, use the STEAM model to gather data on visitor numbers, expenditure, and economic contributions and to inform strategy. WYCA use of the STEAM data and proprietary model shows a significant boom in tourism, with visitor numbers across West Yorkshire reaching 73 million and spending hitting £6.26 billion in 2024, driven by culture events.
Wakefield saw a 10% increase in Day Visits in 2024 (to 10.2 million) driving economic growth of 16.2% (to £655.9 million). There was an increase of approximately 7% in both direct and total employment in the visitor economy, which rose to an estimated 5,376 and 7,579 respectively.
6.2 Culture and creativity activity support the health of wider businesses
Our Year supported the health of the wider business community through two primary mechanisms: direct local procurement and the fostering of new cross-sector collaborations. £1.62 million (42%) of the programme budget was directly invested into Wakefield District-based suppliers and creative practitioners through the council's commissioning framework. The systematic prioritisation of local procurement (supported by organisations such as YPO) provided a direct injection of capital into the district's economy, supporting jobs and building the resilience of local businesses.
Business engagement through creative residencies
Beyond direct spending, Our Year’s Creativity Around the Corner strand successfully fostered new models of business engagement. Three collaborative business residencies with local artists, at NHS Pinderfields, OE Electrics and Wakefield Exchange, each lasted between 1-3 months and produced 6 new commissioned artworks in total. The commissioning and artwork development process engaged 12 businesses and 153 staff or members of the public.
The annual footfall in locations where artwork is installed is estimated at 745,600, demonstrating significant ongoing exposure for the commissioned works. At NHS Pinderfields, the residency with Mo Barrangi and Suzie Cross engaged 67 staff or members of the public in the artwork development process, with 730,000 annual footfall in the location where artwork is installed. Staff feedback was overwhelmingly positive, with one Iranian staff member responding to the mural by saying "This is my home. And this is my home" whilst pointing to the mural, demonstrating the profound emotional impact of the work on medical staff, many of whom are drawn from diverse backgrounds.
At OE Electrics, the residency with Sam Metz and Michelle Duxbury engaged 64 staff in the commissioning process and five staff in in-depth artwork development. The celebration event attracted 74 audience members, with 60% from Wakefield, 40% who don't normally take part in cultural activities, and 100% saying they would take part in more cultural events in the future. Staff described the artwork as offering "connection," being "original, cool, inspiring," and making "the space softer."
Beyond traditional sponsorship: The Leap Club
The Leap Club demonstrates how strategic business engagement can move beyond transactional sponsorship to create genuine cross-sector collaboration. Our Year pioneered an innovative approach to business engagement through the Leap Club, a dedicated network of 20 member organizations plus three major sponsors: Haribo, Tileyard North and YPO.
Rather than simply seeking financial contributions, the programme created a structured platform for ongoing business involvement that spanned diverse sectors - from renewable energy (Ask Renewables) to architecture (Studio J Architects), from media production (Northern Media) to housing (Wakefield District Housing, now VICO Homes).
This model represented a fundamental shift from traditional cultural sponsorship, where businesses typically provide funding in exchange for brand visibility, to a collaborative network where companies could contribute expertise, resources, and professional networks alongside financial support. Haribo's £15,000 sponsorship of Light Up Wakefield Pontefract Liquorice Festival exemplified this integrated approach supporting their local heritage in sweets production.
The diversity of the membership - spanning creative industries, professional services, and public sector organisations - created opportunities for knowledge sharing and cross-sector innovation that extended beyond the programme itself. The Leap Club demonstrates how cultural investment can function as economic development, building business networks and collaborative relationships that strengthen the wider local economy while supporting cultural programming.
Key insight: Cultural programmes can serve as platforms for business network development, creating value for both cultural and economic objectives simultaneously.
6.3 Perceptions of Wakefield District as creative visitor destination enhanced
Our Year successfully enhanced Wakefield's reputation as a creative and cultural destination, achieving a national profile that will support tourism and investment for years to come. The programme's marketing and communications strategy was highly effective, generating 114 pieces of national and regional media coverage with a potential reach of 1.8 billion. This positive media profile has been directly integrated into high-level strategy, with the new Wakefield Destination Management Plan citing Our Year's success as a foundation for positioning the district as the "sculpture capital of the UK" and a "gateway to culture in the north."
This enhanced reputation is not just an external marketing achievement; it is felt by residents themselves. The high levels of civic pride reported in event surveys demonstrate that the perception of Wakefield as a creative place has been strengthened from within. Wakefield’s engagement with regional bodies, including tourism conferences and the Local Visitor Economy Partnership (LVEP), has further embedded this new identity within the wider regional tourism landscape.
The development of "Our Place" as an evergreen social media brand in succession to the Our Year programme represents a significant policy achievement, providing a sustainable platform for ongoing destination marketing that evolved directly through Our Year's success. By building a strong, authentic creative profile, Our Year has created a lasting economic asset for the district.

7. Supporting the fight against the climate emergency
A key strategic aim of Our Year was to ensure that its cultural and creative activities contributed positively to the district's climate emergency response. The programme's approach focused on two main areas: influencing audience behaviour through sustainable event planning and building the long-term capacity of the cultural sector to embed more sustainable practices. While the evidence for quantifiable environmental impact is limited, the programme was successful in raising awareness and creating a foundation for future progress.
7.1 Cultural and creative activity contributes towards net zero target
The programme's primary strategy for contributing to net zero targets was to reduce the environmental impact of its largest events, particularly by encouraging sustainable transport choices. For The Hatchling, a comprehensive Park and Ride service was planned with the ambitious target of accommodating 15,000 users. This demonstrates a clear strategic intent to mitigate the carbon impact of a major event by significantly reducing individual car journeys to the city centre.
To support delivery and Community Partners, a Positive Impacts Toolkit was developed and distributed. This tool, designed to guide cultural organisations in considering and implementing more sustainable practices across all aspects of their event delivery, from waste management and sourcing to energy consumption, was an important resource in building sector-wide awareness.
7.2 Embedding sustainable practices
The most significant environmental legacy of Our Year is the progress made in embedding sustainable thinking into the practices of the Council and its cultural partners. The process of developing the Positive Impacts Toolkit and planning the sustainable transport strategy for The Hatchling has increased the knowledge and confidence of both Council officers and cultural organisations and stimulated a shift in how sustainability is approached in cultural planning, moving it from a secondary consideration to a core part of the event design process. This cross-sector learning has built the capacity of the local cultural sector to deliver more environmentally responsible projects in the future.
There are barriers to this: sustainability considerations present particular challenges for small grants programmes, where community organisations below a certain size threshold are unlikely to carry out carbon impact assessments of their cultural activity. However, the toolkit for measuring and evaluating climate impact could be further developed, including closer working with regional partners to agree consistent and relevant measures. These might include:
	•	Continuing to develop and implement the Museums Sustainability kit across the service with particular focus on the variety of strand experience
	•	Integration with wider council procurement
	•	Roll out of lessons learned through Culture Grant activity and partnership working
	•	Strengthening engagement with WYCA around design and evaluation of cultural programming and creative industries business support, to ensure that for future programmes, environmental impact reporting should be required as a condition for major grants and commissions

8. Programme integration and cross-council transformation
The most profound and lasting impact of Our Year lies not in any single event or output, but in the systemic transformation it has catalysed. The programme was explicitly designed as an integrated system, where multiple distinct strands combined to create an impact far greater than the sum of their parts. This approach has successfully embedded new ways of working within Wakefield Council, forged lasting cross-sector partnerships, and developed an innovative, replicable model for culture-led community development.
8.1 Cross-strand synergies working to embed cultural democracy
Our Year's portfolio of multiple strands was a deliberate strategy to create a resilient and interconnected cultural ecosystem, moving away from reliance on a single 'hero' project. This integrated design, underpinned by the principles of co-creation and cultural democracy, created powerful synergies that amplified the programme's overall impact.
The Culture Grants programme served as the engine of this ecosystem development. By funding a diverse range of community groups across all wards, it created a wave of grassroots activity that fed into and drew from other programme strands. For example, a community group funded by a grant could promote its event through the Community Partners network, showcase the work at a major festival, and contribute its story to the Our Stories heritage platform. This created a virtuous circle where investment in one area generated value across the entire programme.
The collaborative infrastructure provided by a network of Community Partners was key to the programme's success. They acted as connective tissue, ensuring information and opportunities flowed in both directions between the council and hyper-local communities. Shared resources, such as the programme's marketing toolkit and a central pool of more than 1,500 volunteers, were deployed across multiple strands, creating significant efficiencies and fostering a unified sense of purpose.
Evidence of integrated delivery spans all programme strands: 
The Schools Programme connected schools with the district's major cultural venues through the SPARK Curriculum and supported pupils at risk of exclusion through a dedicated Access Bursary
The Moving Festival created walking routes that highlighted heritage sites, 
Nurture & Nourish used food as a medium for creative workshops. 
This contrasts sharply with a siloed approach where each strand would have competed for audiences and resources. Instead, Our Year created a coherent, district-wide offer where participation in one activity served as a gateway to many others, maximising public engagement and delivering on the promise of a truly integrated year of culture.
8.2 Service transformation and cross-council integration
Our Year has catalysed significant changes in how Wakefield Council operates, embedding principles of cultural democracy and an approach to addressing health inequalities into cultural programming and fostering unprecedented cross-departmental collaboration. The transformation of the Council's creative commissioning framework pioneered a new model that moved beyond traditional procurement to prioritise social value and community wealth building. This had immediate impact: £1.62 million (43%) of the programme's spend went to Wakefield District-based suppliers.
The framework has created a more equitable and accessible system that directly supports the local economy. Within that, the Culture Grants programme represents the successful realisation of the ambition to devolve commissioning power directly to communities.
This integrated approach fostered deep collaboration between the Culture team and other key departments, including:
	•	The Schools Programme required partnership with the Education directorate
	•	The Moving Festival and Nurture & Nourish strands involved collaboration with Public Health to achieve shared wellbeing objectives
	•	The enhancement of the district's reputation involved close working with the Economic Development team
Collaborative working has been embedded at the highest strategic level, reflected in the engagement of leaders drawn from departments across the Council in the Our Year Programme Board. The inclusion of cultural participation metrics in the council's corporate State of the District 2024 report demonstrates that culture is now understood as a core tool for achieving district-wide objectives.
Our Year directly informed and demonstrably advanced the district's long-term cultural strategy. Crossings and Gateways' public art commissions, alongside the UNBOUND artist development initiative, actively brought to life and tested the ambitions of the existing “Public Art Framework – Wakefield District”. This provided a vital practical application of the framework's principles and significantly contributed to upskilling the creative sector in public art delivery. Similarly, the 'Our Stories' project, supported by National Heritage Lottery Fund funding within Our Year, was instrumental in furthering collaboration for the protection and celebration of intangible heritage, directly aligning with the objectives of the existing “Our Heritage, Our Stories A Heritage Framework for Wakefield District”.  Our Year’s comprehensive success is cited as foundational evidence in the development of the Destination Management Plan, collectively shaping the district's strategic direction for culture and tourism.
Our Year has successfully demonstrated that cultural investment is not a siloed activity but a highly effective platform for integrated public service delivery. This is reflected in the evolution of social media branding and messaging to "Our Place" as a brand adopted by other Council departments, demonstrating the role of culture in underpinning delivery of all services.
Evidence from case studies demonstrates numerous examples of success in addressing systemic barriers through cultural programming and collaboration:
	•	Evidence of how Culture Grants supported artists to remain and work in the District proves that trust-based commissioning approaches can transform professional sustainability and community engagement simultaneously.
	•	SPARK, the Local Cultural Education Partnership for Wakefield district drawn from partners across the public sector, education and community groups, continues to catalyse collaboration between schools and cultural organisations to ensure that children at risk of exclusion are able to access cultural opportunities.
	•	An increased role for communities in cultural programming, from Beat The Street to Crossings and Gateways, from Creativity Around the Corner to local activity such as removal of an A-frame limiting access to a cycle route, demonstrates the many ways in which cultural activities can catalyse infrastructure improvements benefiting entire communities.
These examples provide concrete evidence of how Our Year's integrated approach delivered benefits extending far beyond traditional cultural outcomes to address infrastructure, social cohesion, and economic development challenges simultaneously.
8.3 Innovation and model development
Our Year has not only transformed Wakefield district but has also developed innovative and replicable models of practice that offer valuable learning for other local authorities. The programme's most transferable innovations include:
	•	Devolved grants model: The structure of the Culture Grants programme, which combined significant investment with light-touch application processes and devolved decision making, is a highly effective and replicable model for empowering community-led activity. The resulting success in leveraging co-investment from match funding provides a powerful business case for this approach.
	•	Community partner infrastructure: The strategy of building a formal network of trusted community organisations as the primary infrastructure for communication and delivery is a transferable model for ensuring deep community reach for any large-scale public programme.
	•	Integrated education offer: The combination of a permanent digital resource (the Wakefield Curriculum), targeted teacher professional development, and a bursary system to remove financial barriers created a sustainable model that continues beyond the programme period. The programme's transition to sustainable ongoing provision demonstrates successful legacy planning.
The launch programming around Wakefield Exchange includes the Rhubarb Festival with heritage talks and community workshops, Sentinel light installation & Heritage commissions celebrating mining heritage, WX Gaming weekend showcasing creative industries, "This Was Our Year" exhibition documenting impact, The Sleeping Tree installation by international artists Invisible Flock, WXcavation family activities in partnership with Wakefield BID, and regular programming including Tai Chi, creative workshops, live music, and community activities. This programming demonstrates successful transition from Our Year's time-limited activities to sustainable, ongoing cultural provision that maintains the programme's values of accessibility, community engagement, and creative excellence.
National context and comparative success
Unlike other Years of Culture that have struggled with evaluation and can face difficulties in legacy development, Our Year has successfully embedded learning and maintained momentum. The programme's ability to continue conversations, transform relationships, and adapt to challenges contrasts positively with programmes that have experienced more significant discontinuity.
While this evaluation shows the outcomes of policy innovation (for example, the estimated £12.96-13.95 million return on total investment measured by impact on the local visitor economy), it does not provide sufficient evidence of how the policies were developed, approved, and embedded to achieve these remarkable results. This represents a missed opportunity to embed learnings in the design and evaluation of future programmes.
A targeted qualitative study involving documentary analysis of key policy documents and senior stakeholder interviews across Culture Leisure and Sport, Economic Development, Public Health, Education, and with WYCA and Arts Council England would provide the rich, narrative evidence needed to fully understand the programme's legacy as a model for public sector innovation.

9. Legacy and sustainability: Creative People & Places bid
Our Year's coherent approach to programme legacy and sustainability has arguably set Wakefield apart. It has established sustainable foundations for continued cultural development, including the creation of a community partnership to bid for Arts Council Creative People & Places (CPP) 2026-2029 funding worth over £1 million.
9.1 Our Year's community engagement legacy
Building directly on Our Year's community engagement model, a bid to Arts Council England’s Creative People & Places (CPP) fund, "Mining Magic & Weaving Stories," was led by a community-led consortium including Featherstone Rovers Foundation (Pit to Pitch Heritage Project), Creative Minds (Open Mind Sculpture), and Spectrum People CIC (Global Majority Women's Group).
This consortium embodied values of:
	•	Graft (resilience)
	•	Nowt but best (ambition)
	•	Quirky (innovative)
	•	All in it together (partnership)
directly reflecting the community leadership principles developed through Our Year. The bid explicitly referenced Our Year's legacy: "Our Enablers are the Arts, Culture & Leisure Service, Wakefield Council building on the legacy of Our Year, Wakefield District 2024."
9.2 Community-led governance model
The governance model developed for the programme was led by an Oversight Board with "at least 55% Community Membership," institutionalising the community leadership principles developed through Our Year. This planned governance structure was designed to ensure that cultural democracy principles became embedded in ongoing programming rather than remaining project-specific.
The programme stated its aims to:
	•	Be Authentic - co-created with community groups and members
	•	Uncover Stories - exploring our industrial heritage from then to now
	•	Happen Everywhere - from neighbourhood allotments to social clubs to sporting arenas
	•	Share Experiences - connecting people and including everyone
	•	Improve Lives - harnessing the power of creativity to improve health and reduce inequality
Although ultimately not successful in securing funding, this approach represented a direct continuation of Our Year's cultural democracy approach.
9.3 Sustainable infrastructure development
Our Year has created lasting infrastructure that will support continued cultural development:
	•	The participation of the District's five largest cultural organisations in the Schools Programme, SPARK and Wakefield Place-Based Curriculum created lasting educational capacity that will continue to benefit young people across the district.
	•	Community Cooking Leaders: Nurture & Nourish trained 22 community leaders, ensuring continued health and wellbeing programming through sustainable community capacity.
	•	Physical infrastructure: The completion of Wakefield Exchange provides greatly increased capacity for a wide range of cultural, creative and community-led activities in the town centre. Permanent public artworks through the Crossings & Gateways programme and Creativity Around The Corner will have a lasting legacy in putting cultural expression at the heart of communities. Other accessibility improvements, including the example of the A-frame barrier removal, leave permanent community benefits.
	•	Business engagement: The Leap Club encouraged private sector partners to look beyond transactional sponsorship models to create genuine cross-sector collaboration. The Creativity Around the Corner residency model, as deployed with NHS Pinderfields, OE Electrics, and WX, has created new templates for cultural/economic integration, with commissioned artworks providing ongoing visibility through 745,600 annual footfall. This model demonstrates sustainable approaches to business-cultural partnership beyond traditional sponsorship.
	•	Creative Wakefield Network: Grew by 225 members, strengthening existing professional infrastructure for ongoing collaboration and development.
	•	Environmental sustainability: Future cultural programming should integrate environmental impact assessment and carbon reduction strategies, aligning with WYCA's net-zero commitments and ensuring cultural investment supports broader sustainability objectives through local sourcing, reduced travel, and environmentally conscious programming choices.
	•	Regional knowledge transfer: The programme's innovative approaches, particularly the integrated Schools Programme model and business residency framework, should be systematically documented and shared through WYCA's Knowledge Transfer networks to support peer learning across West Yorkshire authorities and maximise the regional impact of successful interventions.
9.4 Our Year's model as a template for Cultural Place delivery
Our Year's model represents a template for Cultural Place delivery in line with national policy goals, including Arts Council England's "Cultural Communities" outcome and the West Yorkshire Combined Authority's focus on culture as a central driver for economic growth and placemaking. Our Year demonstrated effective working with regional and national funders and tested an integrated policy delivery framework that systematically aligned diverse stakeholder aspirations with these strategic goals.
This framework recognised and synthesised the aspirations of audiences, grass-roots community organisations, local stakeholders, the Council, and cultural partners into a single strategic programme. For instance, the Culture Grants programme exemplified this by devolving decision-making power directly to communities, with  participants reporting strong input into project organisation. The Crossings & Gateways public art commissions engaged in deep community co-design, such as in Knottingley where residents' creative collages were woven directly into the final mural design. The UNBOUND artist development initiative directly incorporated an artist needs survey to guide its workshop design, ensuring relevance and community ownership.
Our Year's success in achieving community buy-in through systematic co-creation processes, combined with its ability to leverage significant external investment, demonstrates a replicable model for place-based cultural development that addresses national policy priorities while maintaining local authenticity and ownership.
9.5 Wakefield Council internal organisational learning
Wakefield Council's internal organisational learning was supported by diverse representation of Council officers on the Programme Board. The programme has successfully embedded a focus on wellbeing and principles of proportionate universalism into delivery of Cultural services. Moreover, it has influenced new ways of working that extend beyond the Culture service to influence corporate strategy, cross-departmental collaboration, and community engagement approaches across the authority. The result is a greater prioritisation of the role of culture in improving residents' quality of life.
Examples of this include:
	•	The integration of cultural participation metrics into the council's State of the District 2024, demonstrating how Our Year's evidence base has influenced high-level strategic thinking.
	•	The systematic prioritisation of local procurement through the commissioning framework, supported by YPO, which has created new approaches to community wealth building that could be applied across council services.
	•	The Our Year Commissioning Framework established a transparent, auditable system that prioritises local investment and social value, ensuring public funds are used to build the resilience of the local creative sector.

10. Conclusions and strategic recommendations
Our Year - Wakefield District 2024 was conceived as a strategic intervention to transform the district's cultural landscape and embed new ways of working within the Council. This evaluation, based on the final programme data, concludes that it has been an outstanding success. The programme has not only met but exceeded its primary engagement targets, delivering a significant return on investment and creating a powerful, sustainable legacy of community empowerment and cross-sector collaboration. It has successfully proven that strategic cultural investment, grounded in principles of cultural democracy, reducing health inequalities and empowering communities, can deliver transformational outcomes across health, education, economic development, and social cohesion.
10.1 Strategic transformation
Our Year has delivered a demonstrable strategic transformation for the district, achieving significant progress against all six of its strategic aims:
	•	Improving the lives and wellbeing of communities across the district
What we observed: A dramatic increase in community engagement, with residents actively participating in cultural activities that enhanced their quality of life and social connections.
Where's the proof?

10.2 Investment model evaluation and replicability
The £4.2 million investment in Our Year has proven to be highly effective, delivering exceptional value for money and demonstrating a powerful, replicable model for public investment. The programme's effectiveness is further amplified by the new commercial sponsorship opportunities developed through the Leap Club and match funding leveraged through the Culture Grants programme, demonstrating a powerful multiplier effect.
The Our Year model combines several innovative approaches that are both replicable and scalable:
	•	The devolved grants model that achieved levels of community co-investment more than 10x greater than the average for similar projects demonstrates how local authorities can move beyond traditional procurement to genuine community empowerment.
	•	The Community Partner network of 126 organisations provides a template for building lasting social infrastructure that can support ongoing community development beyond specific programmes.
	•	The integrated education offer combining digital resources, teacher training, and financial support creates a complete system for embedding cultural learning that could be adapted for any local authority area.
	•	The portfolio approach to programme design, spreading investment across interconnected strands, provides a model for managing risk while maximising community engagement and learning opportunities.
10.3 Strategic recommendations
Recommendation 1: Embed cultural democracy as a core principle
Make the principles of proportionate universalism and community co-creation central to all future cultural investment, building on the proven success of the Culture Grants model that achieved exceptional community co-investment rates and genuine community ownership.
Recommendation 2: Formalise cross-service integration
Create permanent structures to maintain the cross-departmental collaboration pioneered during Our Year, ensuring culture remains integrated with health, education, and economic development strategies rather than operating in isolation.
Recommendation 3: Maintain community infrastructure
Seek new sources of funding to invest in maintaining and developing the network of 126 Community Partners and the Creative Wakefield network as permanent assets for ongoing community and cultural development.
Recommendation 4: Document and share innovation
Commission detailed documentation of the policy innovation processes that enabled Our Year's success, creating a resource that can inform cultural policy development nationally.
Recommendation 5: Establish regional knowledge transfer mechanisms
The programme's innovative approaches, particularly the integrated Schools Programme model and business residency framework, should be systematically documented and shared through WYCA's Knowledge Transfer networks to support peer learning across West Yorkshire authorities and maximise the regional impact of successful interventions. Future cultural programmes should integrate environmental impact assessment from inception, aligning with WYCA's net-zero commitments.
10.4 Regional and national policy implications
The programme's success in reaching a mass audience, mobilising volunteers, investing in the local economy, and building community confidence is proven by the verified data. This presents opportunities to external funders with an interest in maintaining and growing Wakefield's cultural offer:
	•	For West Yorkshire Combined Authority: Use Our Year as a template for culture-led community development across the region, integrating its community co-creation approaches into future Gainshare investments.
	•	For Arts Council England: Recognise Our Year as a national exemplar of cultural democracy in practice, using its evidence base to inform Creative People & Places and other community-focused funding programmes.
	•	For DCMS and HM Treasury: Consider Our Year's model as evidence for the economic and social value of devolved, community-led cultural investment, particularly its demonstration of exceptional community co-investment rates and economic returns.
10.5 The continuing legacy
There is clear evidence of how Our Year has engendered a wider legacy of community engagement in culture.
	•	The community-led partnership which came together to bid for Creative People & Places funding demonstrates a wider appetite to continue to develop and strengthen Our Year's legacy over the coming years. The planned community-led consortium approach, with over 55% community membership on the governance board, reflects the cultural democracy principles that made Our Year so successful.
	•	The permanent infrastructure created by Our Year - from the multi-partner coalition around the Schools Programme to the Community Partners network and new commissioning frameworks - provides a solid foundation for continued cultural and community development that will benefit the district for years to come.

The programme's success provides a powerful mandate for continuing this approach, scaling its innovations, and sharing its learning with communities across the UK and beyond. APPENDIX C: Evaluation approach and evidence base
C1. The Fifth Sector's independent evaluation role
The Fifth Sector was commissioned to provide independent evaluation of Our Year, bringing external perspective and evaluation expertise to programme assessment. This independent evaluation approach was designed to ensure credible analysis of programme outcomes while providing learning that could inform future cultural investment and policy development.
The evaluation drew on multiple evidence sources to provide comprehensive programme assessment:
	•	Programme monitoring data: Systematic collection of participation numbers, demographic data, and delivery outputs across all programme strands, providing the quantitative foundation and evaluation framework for impact assessment.
	•	Stakeholder consultation: In-depth interviews with programme delivery teams, community partners, and key stakeholders to understand delivery challenges, successes, and lessons learned.
	•	Participant feedback: Analysis of survey responses, testimonials, and feedback forms collected throughout programme delivery, providing insight into participant experience and perceived impact.
	•	Financial analysis: Review of programme expenditure, local procurement data, and economic impact calculations to assess value for money and economic contribution.
	•	Policy document analysis: Examination of strategic documents, committee reports, and policy frameworks to understand programme alignment with wider council objectives and strategic priorities.
C2. What Our Year demonstrated vs. what it could have demonstrated
Using this framework, we can draw a clear distinction between the programme's proven successes and the potential impacts that the evaluation methodology was not equipped to capture.
What Our Year demonstrably achieved
	•	Massive Public Engagement: It successfully delivered 640,651 participant and audience engagements, exceeding its primary target.
	•	Deep Community Buy-In: It mobilised 1,524 volunteers and a network of 126 community partners, proving its cultural democracy model resonated deeply.
	•	Significant Economic Impact: It generated an estimated £12.96m to £13.95m in visitor spend and directly invested £1.62 million into the local creative economy.
	•	Exceptional Community Co-Investment: It leveraged £359,699 in match funding through its Culture Grants, a powerful indicator of community confidence.
	•	Systematic Educational Reach: It engaged 162 schools and trained 146 teachers, creating a lasting educational legacy.
What Our Year could have demonstrated with a different evaluation framework
	•	A Quantifiable Social Return on Investment (SROI): By measuring subjective wellbeing, the programme could have calculated a specific, Treasury-aligned monetary value for the improvement in residents' quality of life.
	•	Precise Economic Value Added: A full economic impact study could have calculated the programme's Gross Value Added (GVA) and the net number of jobs supported, moving beyond estimates to precise figures.
	•	Longitudinal Social Change: A planned longitudinal study could have tracked changes in social cohesion, skills development, and civic pride over a multi-year period, proving the programme's lasting legacy.
	•	A National Case Study on Health and Culture: With systematic wellbeing data, Our Year could have become a national case study on how culture-led regeneration directly addresses public health priorities.
C3. Contribution to cultural policy evidence base
While the evaluation has limitations, the learning from Our Year makes a significant contribution to the wider cultural policy evidence base, particularly in demonstrating the power of a cultural democracy model at scale. The programme's success in mobilising community co-investment, engaging over 640,000 participants across all 21 wards, and creating sustainable partnerships provides valuable evidence for the effectiveness of devolved, community-led cultural programming.
The programme's integration with health, education, and economic development objectives also provides important learning for place-based policy development, demonstrating how cultural investment can function as integrated public policy rather than separate cultural activity.
However, the absence of systematic wellbeing measurement means the programme cannot contribute to the growing body of evidence on culture's direct health impacts, limiting its value to national policy development and academic research.
C4. Framework for future cultural investment evaluation
Our evaluation exposed limitations in assessment of the full social return on investment (which requires systematic wellbeing data), the precise economic impact (which requires a formal GVA study), and the long-term behavioural changes resulting from the programme. We recommend further work with regional and national partners to develop a comprehensive evaluation framework that includes systematic wellbeing measurement, formal economic impact assessment, and environmental impact tracking for all future major cultural investments.
Based on the learning from Our Year, the following framework is recommended for all future large-scale cultural investments to ensure their full impact is captured and articulated.
Systematic wellbeing measurement
Future programmes must integrate robust wellbeing measurement using ONS questions and WELLBY methodology from inception to capture the full social value of cultural investment in health & wellbeing. The most significant methodological gap in the evaluation of Our Year is the absence of systematic subjective wellbeing (SWB) measurement using established frameworks. The programme's success in generating over 640,000 participant engagements presented a nationally significant opportunity to create a robust dataset on the wellbeing impacts of culture-led, community-based regeneration.
As established by the Arts Council England (ACE) Subjective Wellbeing Evidence Review (2025) and HM Treasury's Green Book guidance, best practice for evaluating such programmes involves using the ONS4 wellbeing questions and the Wellbeing-Adjusted Life Year (WELLBY) methodology to quantify social value. Our Year operated without this framework. This represents a critical missed opportunity, not only to quantify the full social return on Wakefield's £4.334 million investment but also to contribute a dataset of national importance to the cultural policy evidence base that both the OECD and ACE identify as urgently needed.
Recommendations for systematic SWB measurement integration:
	•	Integrate from inception: The ONS4 wellbeing questions must be built into the evaluation plan from the very beginning, not added retrospectively.
	•	Establish a baseline: A baseline survey of a representative sample of the target population should be conducted before the programme begins to provide a clear point of comparison.
	•	Use a consistent methodology: All participant surveys across all programme strands should include the ONS4 questions to allow for comparative analysis.
	•	Allocate sufficient resources: A dedicated budget and staff resource for evaluation must be allocated as a core part of the programme's overall budget.
WELLBY methodology application guidance
To translate this wellbeing data into a language understood by economic decision-makers, future evaluations should use the Wellbeing-Adjusted Life Year (WELLBY) methodology, as recommended in the HM Treasury Green Book.
	•	What is a WELLBY? One WELLBY represents a one-point increase in life satisfaction (on a 0-10 scale) for one person for one year.
	•	How is it used? HM Treasury provides a monetary value for a WELLBY, allowing the social impact of a programme to be included in a formal cost-benefit analysis. By measuring the change in life satisfaction for programme participants against a control group, an evaluator can calculate the number of WELLBYs generated.
	•	Why is it important? This methodology allows the social value of culture to be presented alongside its economic value, making a far more compelling and holistic case for investment.
By adopting this framework, future cultural programmes can move beyond simply counting outputs to robustly evidencing their full social and economic value, securing the place of culture as a core component of public policy and investment.
Economic impact measurement enhancement
To capture the full economic value of future cultural programmes requires longitudinal economic tracking: assessment of economic impacts over a minimum two-year period to capture sustained effects.
	•	Gross Value Added (GVA) calculation: Formal economic impact study measuring the programme's contribution to regional economic output.
	•	Employment impact assessment: Systematic tracking of jobs created, both directly and indirectly, through programme activities.
	•	Supply chain analysis: Detailed mapping of local procurement and its multiplier effects through the regional economy.
This comprehensive evaluation framework would enable future cultural programmes to demonstrate their full value across social, environmental, and economic dimensions, providing the robust evidence base needed to secure ongoing investment and policy support.
Environmental impact measurement framework
Future programmes should also integrate systematic environmental impact measurement from the outset:
	•	Carbon footprint assessment: Baseline and ongoing measurement of carbon emissions across all programme activities, including transport, energy consumption, and waste generation.
	•	Sustainable procurement tracking: Systematic recording of local sourcing, sustainable materials use, and waste reduction measures.
	•	Behaviour change measurement: Assessment of changes in participant environmental awareness and sustainable practices.
	•	Partnership with regional bodies: Collaboration with West Yorkshire Combined Authority and other regional partners to develop consistent environmental measurement frameworks.
C5. Policy innovation process documentation
The integration of wellbeing considerations into corporate strategy represents a significant policy achievement. However, the absence of systematic wellbeing measurement means the council cannot quantify the specific impact of cultural participation on resident wellbeing. Future programmes would benefit from integrating established wellbeing measurement frameworks from the outset, enabling the council to make evidence-based claims about the health and wellbeing returns on cultural investment.
The evaluation shows the outcomes of policy innovation (for example, the estimated £12.96-13.95 million return on total investment measured by impact on the local visitor economy), but does not provide sufficient evidence of how the policies were developed, approved, and embedded to achieve these remarkable results. This represents a missed opportunity to embed learnings in the design and evaluation of future programmes.
A targeted qualitative study involving documentary analysis of key policy documents and senior stakeholder interviews across Culture, Economic Development, Public Health, Education, and with WYCA and Arts Council England would provide the rich, narrative evidence needed to fully understand the programme's legacy as a model for public sector innovation.
