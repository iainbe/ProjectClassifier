BRITISH COUNCIL WESTERN BALKANS CREATIVE ECONOMY PROGRAMME
Summary Report: February–December 2025
Prepared by: Iain Bennett, The Fifth Sector Limited
Prepared for: British Council Programme Leadership
Period covered: February–December 2025
Scope: Albania, North Macedonia, Montenegro, and Western Balkans (WB6)

Executive summary
This report documents a disparate but interconnected programme of work undertaken across the Western Balkans to establish strategic frameworks for creative economy development. The programme operated across three nested systems: macro (regional cooperation and policy alignment), meso (national strategy and institutional development), and micro (sector-specific interventions and organisational capacity).
By December 2025, the programme has delivered foundational strategy documents, established governance models, and designed implementable skills interventions. However, the transition from strategy to implementation remains incomplete. Institutional capacity for cross-ministerial coordination varies significantly by country. The most critical gap is the absence of operational funding mechanisms and clear accountability structures for translating policy commitments into tangible outcomes for young people in creative sectors.
Key lesson: Effective creative economy development requires simultaneous work at all three system levels, with explicit connections between them. Working at only one level—however comprehensively—will not generate sustainable change.
Part 1: Programme context and strategic framing
How this programme came about
Throughout 2024 and early 2025, preparatory work on creative economy development had been building momentum across the Western Balkans. The British Council, working with UK government partners and European Commission representatives, had begun mapping creative economy potential, identifying policy gaps, and building stakeholder consensus around the need for regional cooperation on creative industries as a driver of youth employment and economic diversification.
This groundwork culminated in the Western Balkans Ministerial Meeting in Kotor (May 2025), where creative economy development received formal pan-regional recognition and buy-in. Ministers from all six countries endorsed a joint declaration committing to regional cooperation on cultural and creative industries, youth employment through creative sector pathways, digital innovation integration, and culture-tourism synergies. This was a pivotal moment—not the beginning, but rather the point at which preparatory work gained political legitimacy and ministerial commitment.
The Berlin Process, supported by the UK and EU, provided the diplomatic framework within which this ministerial consensus was achieved. This institutional backing was crucial: it signalled that creative economy development was not a niche cultural concern but a strategic priority aligned with EU accession readiness, regional stability, and economic diversification across the Balkans.
The British Council's subsequent role was to translate this political commitment into operational capacity. The programme focused on three interconnected questions:
	•	How can six countries cooperate on creative economy development whilst respecting national sovereignty and EU accession requirements? 
	•	What institutional structures (councils, working groups, policy frameworks) enable countries to act on creative economy ambitions? 
	•	What concrete skills interventions can demonstrably improve youth employment in creative sectors?
An ecological systems approach
The programme operated across nested systems:
Macro (regional)
Joint declaration and policy alignment, cross-country data comparability, EU integration requirements, international positioning.
Meso (national)
Creative economy strategies and policy frameworks, institutional governance (councils, ministries, universities), statistical classification and measurement systems, cross-ministerial coordination mechanisms.
Micro (sector/organisational)
Training pathways, university-industry partnerships, organisational capability development, individual practitioner and entrepreneur support.
Critical insight: Work at each level must be designed to strengthen the others. A regional strategy that doesn't connect to national capacity means nothing. National institutions that don't translate into sector-level opportunities waste resources. This programme's success depends on explicit connections being maintained across all three levels.

Part 2: Completed work
Regional cooperation and alignment
Joint ministerial declaration and regional framework (completed May 2025)
The Kotor meeting produced ministerial endorsement of creative economy as a regional priority, commitment to integrated policy approaches linking culture, tourism, and economic growth, shared principles for cultural heritage monetisation, youth employment targets across the region, and agreement on UK-led technical assistance and capacity building.
Deliverables comprise the Kotor Ministerial Meeting briefing and outcomes document (28 May 2025), the Joint Declaration text (endorsed by all WB6 ministers), regional cooperation framework outline, and strategic significance briefing emphasizing UK positioning as lead partner and EU alignment on creative sector development.
This provides a political context for national-level work. Ministers have publicly committed to creative economy as an economic development priority, not a cultural afterthought. The ministerial consensus creates political space for subsequent work on strategy development, institutional reform, and skills initiatives. It also signals to international donors and EU institutions that the region is serious about creative economy investment.
However, implementation mechanisms to achieve that objective have yet to be agreed across all six countries. The Declaration exists; the structures to monitor and enforce it do not. No regional body currently oversees progress or coordinates implementation of ministerial commitments across countries.
National institutional development
Albania: Creative economy framework and working group
A comprehensive creative economy framework for Albania, designed to operationalise the ministerial commitment made in Kotor, was presented in July 2025. The framework includes stakeholder mapping (government, industry, civil society), budget analysis across relevant ministries, a concept note for Prime Minister presentation (delivered pre-government reshuffle), a working group structure with clear responsibilities, and a five-year strategy outline (2025–2030).
Deliverables comprise:
Albania Creative Economy Working Group Terms of Reference (July 2025)
Stakeholder mapping matrix identifying key ministries and institutions for ongoing consultation
Budget compilation across Ministries of Economy/Culture/Innovation, Finance, Education, Local Government
Concept note for Prime Minister
Agreed actions and responsibilities framework with Key Performance Indicators (OKRs) for creative economy growth including 15% annual sector growth, 500+ young entrepreneurs trained by end 2026, and 5+ university partnerships.
Key decisions made: Lead ministry is Culture, with supporting ministries in Finance, Economy, Education, Innovation & Sports, and Local Government. This governance structure reflects recognition that creative economy development spans multiple policy domains and cannot be siloed within Culture. Fast-track cinematography law reform is led by Jonid Jorgji with British Film Institute support, addressing a concrete barrier to film sector growth. A Kosovo-Albania joint project on skills development has access to £300K Berlin Process funding, creating opportunity for bilateral partnership on creative industries skills. Artist passport research and advocacy have been prioritised, reflecting international best practice in facilitating artist mobility.
This framework, if applied consistently, should enable and accelerate coordination across previously siloed ministries, and provide clear accountability (who does what by when), measurable targets tied to ministerial commitment, and a timeline for legal and fiscal reform.
What remains: Government reshuffle aftermath and political continuity (government changed post-concept note delivery), budget allocation and disbursement mechanisms for planned initiatives, implementation capacity in MEKI to coordinate other ministries effectively, and operationalisation of the Montenegro partnership (concept exists; implementation structures do not).
North Macedonia: Creative economy council models and comparative analysis
What was delivered: A rigorous comparative analysis of two governance models for creative economy leadership in North Macedonia, recognising that institutional design matters but must be tailored to national context rather than prescribed. The analysis examines Model A (Ministry-controlled Council led by Culture & Tourism) and Model B (Independent Sector-Led Council with government observers), with explicit attention to North Macedonia's specific challenges: statistical classification issues (gaming/gambling SIC code misclassification), gaming industry development (distinct from film), digital skills integration (connection to MDT's 21st Century Schools initiative), and gender analysis in creative workforce.
Model A offers strengths in direct policy pathways, established budget access, and regulatory familiarity, but faces weaknesses in political vulnerability, limited sector independence, and cross-ministerial coordination challenges. Model B offers strengths in sector autonomy, diverse representation, agility, and capacity to serve as cross-ministerial coordination hub, but faces weaknesses in resource limitations, implementation authority gaps, and sustainability questions.
Deliverables comprise a comparative analysis with RACI matrices examining 14 key functions across both models, contextual considerations specific to North Macedonia (with detailed analysis of gaming industry needs, digital skills integration, statistical classification reform requirements, and gender equality considerations), risk assessment and mitigation strategies, implementation pathway (phased approach, governance recommendations, priority action areas), and international benchmark comparison (UK Creative Industries Council, Estonian Creative Industries Association, Colombian Orange Economy Council).
Recommendation: Independent sector-led model, with formalised ministry observer framework.
Rationale: The independent model better enables cross-ministerial coordination (provides a structured forum for ministries to align on creative economy issues without one ministry dominating), gaming industry development (can advocate for gaming-specific policy interventions distinct from film, address statistical classification issues, facilitate connections to MDT innovation funding), and digital skills integration (can ensure creative applications are integrated into technical education, facilitate private sector investment, connect educational institutions with industry).
What this enables: Clear governance choice for North Macedonian government grounded in comparative analysis rather than prescription, implementation roadmap for establishing the Council (6-month establishment phase, phased operations), and risk mitigation strategies for sustainability and political relationships.
What remains: Government decision on which model to pursue, formal establishment of chosen council (governance structures, legal status, funding), recruitment of chair and council members, and operational funding (estimated: £45K–£75K for Phase 1; varies by model choice).
Montenegro Creative Economy framework
Plymouth- Montenegro partnership development and concept validation
What was delivered: A structured framework for UK-Montenegro creative economy and blue-green innovation collaboration, grounded in genuine reciprocal learning rather than one-way knowledge transfer. The framework recognises that Montenegro brings substantial expertise (Faculty of Maritime Studies in Kotor, Science & Technology Park in Podgorica, emerging creative ecosystem) whilst Plymouth contributes leading capability (Plymouth Sound National Marine Park, Marine Research Plymouth Alliance, creative economy infrastructure, Culture Plan).
The framework includes a Plymouth–Montenegro Partnership Development Programme proposal, a Collaboration Matrix mapping areas of alignment (sustainable tourism, youth opportunity, marine innovation, blue economy, cultural heritage, creative-tech), and a three-day high-impact visit design for Montenegrin stakeholders to Plymouth. The programme outline covers sustainable coastal tourism learning, creative–blue–green innovation, youth skills and digital participation, and a "Dinner in the Dome" (Market Hall immersive experience) as focal point for relationship-building and co-design. A long-term partnership roadmap targets Horizon Europe, Creative Europe, and WBIF funding.
What this enables: Movement from high-level dialogue to tangible collaboration, relationship-building across key Montenegrin institutions (Kotor UNESCO World Heritage site representatives, national tourism and innovation agencies, Creative Industries Council), early concepts for co-designed projects, and increased readiness for future international funding.
What remains: Securing funding for the visit (estimated cost breakdown: travel, accommodation, venue, catering, staff time, field visits, partnership engagement), finalising guest list and host institution coordination, designing the three-day programme in operational detail, and establishing post-visit accountability structures (who does what by when to progress co-designed concepts into funded projects).

Pan-regional and cross-system connections 
There remains a critical gap in explicit linkages between regional, national and sector/organisational partners. Currently, no mechanism exists to translate regional findings into national planning. National strategies must translate into operational support. Currently, strategies exist in only some places, not others; implementation funding and accountability structures have yet to be finalised in any of the WB6.
Early outcomes from FCDO projects and partnerships between universities and sector organisations should inform policy refinement (feedback loops). Currently, no monitoring or learning systems are in place.
What's needed: An explicit "translation layer" connecting these three levels. This requires quarterly cross-country learning meetings to share outcomes, partnership progress, and organisational capability shifts. Feedback mechanisms from sector and university programmes are needed to inform refinement of national creative economy council operations and government strategy. This should in turn be fed into regional synthesis of lessons learned to inform macro-level policy alignment.
Part 4: Lessons learned
What worked well
Multi-level systems thinking
Operating simultaneously at regional, national and sector levels - and explicitly naming these levels- prevented the programme from becoming disconnected from reality. For example, the North Macedonia council analysis was credible precisely because it grounded governance choices in sector-specific realities (gaming industry needs, digital skills integration, statistical classification issues). A purely abstract governance discussion would have been irrelevant.
Implication: Future programmes should adopt this three-level framing from the outset. It clarifies scope, prevents scope creep, and makes programme logic transparent to stakeholders.
Employer-led evidence over assumptions
The framework for a Creative Economy Skills Initiative, which was delivered in June 2025 and for which procurement of consultants is currently underway, goes further and is more developed than any of the national creative economy council plans. It sets out a principle that rather than assuming what skills are needed, the programme will prioritise gathering evidence from employers, educators, and young people. The resulting Skills Needs Assessment framework explicitly requires employer validation of gaps, not institutional assumptions.
Implication: A similar level of detailed planning for the Creative Economy Councils is needed to provide the basis for consultation with the respective Ministries of Culture on creation of defensible programmes. This work is furthest advanced in Albania, where a proposal was made to the Ministry of Culture in July; but it has yet to be followed through (owing largely to changes in the administration) and we need to agree a roadmap to set up this programme and use it to influence discussions in the other WB nations.
Comparative analysis as decision-making tool
The North Macedonia Creative Economy Council analysis used RACI matrices, contextual considerations, and risk assessment to help government choose between governance models. Rather than recommending a single "best" model, the analysis showed trade-offs and implications of each choice, enabling informed decision-making.
Implication: Comparative frameworks are more useful than prescriptive recommendations. Stakeholders trust decision-making they've participated in; they resent prescriptions.
Reciprocal learning over knowledge transfer
The Montenegro partnership framework explicitly rejects a "UK teaches Balkans" model. Instead, it positions Montenegrin institutions (Faculty of Maritime Studies, Science & Technology Park, emerging creative ecosystem) as bringing genuine expertise to the partnership. This is not rhetoric; it generates engagement and sustainability.
Implication: Programmes framed as knowledge transfer face resistance and sustainability challenges. Programmes framed as reciprocal learning generate local ownership and resilience.
Ministerial recognition as amplifier, not beginning
The Kotor meeting succeeded because it built on substantial preparatory work. Ministers endorsed something real, not an abstract concept. This is why the meeting had impact.
Implication: Political moments are most powerful when grounded in evidence and stakeholder consensus. Rushing to ministerial endorsement before groundwork is complete produces declarations without implementation.
What was challenging
Political volatility and institutional continuity
Albania's government reshuffle mid-programme threatened continuity of creative economy commitment. North Macedonia's protracted government formation delayed council establishment. Montenegro's political sensitivities required careful stakeholder management.
Lesson: Political risk is real and cannot be managed away. Mitigation requires embedding creative economy commitment in multiple ministries (not just Culture), building business sector and civil society constituency (so commitment transcends political cycles), regular revalidation of government support (don't assume continuity), and creating visible early wins that generate political incentive to continue.
Cross-ministerial coordination gaps
In all three countries, creative economy development requires coordination across Education, Finance, Economy, Tourism, and Digital Transformation ministries. However, these ministries rarely communicate on this issue. The formal structures (councils, working groups) designed to bridge these gaps do not yet exist or are not yet functional.
Lesson: Institutional structures alone are insufficient. You also need explicit incentive alignment (how does creative economy priority serve each ministry's objectives?), shared measurement frameworks (so all ministries see same data), senior leadership alignment (minister-to-minister conversations, not just working groups), and external accountability (international partners, EU, donors) to maintain focus.
Funding gaps and sustainability questions
Most of the work completed exists on paper. Implementation requires funding: skills training, university partnership development, research and mapping. Funding sources are unclear. Some work (regional skills assessment) has been designed but not funded.
Lesson: Strategy documents are cheap; implementation is expensive. Programme design must address funding upfront. Who pays for skills training? (Employers? Government? Participants? Donors?) Who funds organisational development programmes? (World Bank? EU? British Council?) How are university partnerships resourced? (Tuition fees? Government grants? Employer contributions?) These questions must be answered before implementation, not after.
Data availability and statistical gaps
The programme repeatedly encountered the same challenge: WB6 countries lack reliable baseline data on creative economy employment, skills provision, graduate outcomes. The gaming/gambling SIC code misclassification in North Macedonia is a specific example. This makes it hard to set targets, monitor progress, or prove impact.
Lesson: Baseline data collection must be a priority, not an afterthought. Without it, you cannot set credible targets, measure progress, attract international funding (donors demand evidence), or advocate for policy change. The regional Skills Needs Assessment was designed partly to address this gap, but it has not been funded or executed.
Scope complexity and strategic clarity
The programme started with a clear regional focus (WB6 cooperation) but expanded to include country-specific strategies (Albania, North Macedonia, Montenegro), sector-specific interventions (bootcamps, university partnerships), and organisational capability work (Hyper Island). This breadth is necessary but created complexity. It was sometimes unclear which country/sector should be priority, or how micro-level work connected to macro-level strategy.
Lesson: Operating at three system levels simultaneously requires exceptional clarity on which level is primary, what are the connection points, and what is in scope versus out of scope. Programme boundaries remain unclear; this has not improved over time.
Systemic insights
Creative economy development requires simultaneous work at three levels
You cannot build a sustainable creative economy by working at only one system level. Macro-only work (regional declarations, policy alignment) generates nice words but no change on the ground. Young people still lack skills. Organisations still operate in silos. Work on aligning national strategies with the development of Creative Economy Council and skills programmes creates institutional structures but risks becoming disconnected from real sector needs or regional opportunities. Funding individual projects helps the organisations and sectors involved but does not scale without supportive national policy or regional benchmarking.
Effective programmes operate at all three levels simultaneously, with explicit connections between them.
Governance models matter, but context matters more
The North Macedonia analysis showed that no governance model is universally "best." The independent sector-led council model could work well for North Macedonia's specific context (need for cross-ministerial coordination, gaming industry challenges, digital skills integration) but met with resistance which means it has yet to be implemented. Even if this can be agreed and implemented in a Macedonian context, it might not work elsewhere. The key insight: governance choices must be tailored to context, not prescribed.
Evidence and employer engagement are non-negotiable
To be credible, every intervention in this programme needs to be grounded in evidence (what does data show about skills gaps, youth employment, sector performance?) and employer engagement (what do companies actually need? what will they invest in?). Programmes designed without these elements face resistance and lower outcomes.
Reciprocal learning beats knowledge transfer
Where national partners understood the recommendations around development of Creative Economy Councils as "the UK teaching the Balkans”’ they stalled. Interventions framed as "mutual learning" (UK and Balkans partners learning from each other) generated engagement and sustainability. This is not just messaging; it reflects genuine reality. The Balkans have expertise (maritime studies, emerging gaming studios, cultural heritage management) that is genuinely valuable.
Implementation gaps are as important as strategy gaps
The programme identified many strategic gaps (creative economy not integrated into economic planning, skills provision not aligned with employer needs, statistical data unavailable). But it also revealed implementation gaps: who will execute the strategy? How will it be funded? Who is accountable for results? These questions matter as much as strategic clarity.

System-level recommendations
For government ministers and officials
Establish quarterly coordination meetings where findings from national creative economy strategies, institutional development, and policy implementation are explicitly connected to regional priorities and EU alignment requirements. This ensures that progress in one country informs policy decisions across the region.
For creative industry employers and sector leaders
Create regular forums where creative businesses can share what they're observing about the creative economy landscape in their country, feed that insight into national strategy development, and influence regional priorities. Make sure sector voices shape policy, not the other way around.
For international donors and EU partners
Establish a feedback loop between national implementation and regional strategy: national councils and working groups generate evidence on what institutional arrangements work, that evidence informs regional policy alignment, regional learning shapes national refinement. This creates accountability and demonstrates impact.
For all stakeholders 
What we learn from establishing councils, mapping the sector, and coordinating across government must directly inform how we help shape regional policies and EU alignment. Establish quarterly meetings to make sure this actually happens.
The principle is: findings from national work feed into regional coordination, and regional learning feeds back into national refinement. 

Conclusion
By December 2025, this programme has created foundational architecture for creative economy development across the Western Balkans. A regional framework and ministerial commitment exist. At national level, three countries have developed tailored national strategies and institutional models. At sector level, the introduction of the in-country consultants and evidence of trans-national collaboration between sector bodies for film and games provides support for implementation of future creative economy councils and skills programmes.
However, the transition from strategy to implementation is incomplete. The critical gap is not strategy; it is funding, institutional capacity, and clear accountability structures. Young people still lack access to relevant skills training. Organisations still work in silos. Governments still struggle to coordinate across ministries.
The next phase must be ruthlessly focused on implementation: launching bootcamps, establishing partnerships, operationalising councils, and building monitoring systems to track progress. Simultaneously, explicit translation mechanisms must connect work at all three system levels, so that micro-level successes inform meso-level strategy and macro-level policy.
The ultimate test of this programme's success will not be the quality of its strategy documents, but the number of young people who find meaningful work in creative sectors, and the number of creative organisations that become more innovative, collaborative, and resilient.

Appendix C: Regional Skills Needs Assessment Framework
Skills interventions (work in progress)
	•	During 2025, the programme designed three complementary approaches to creative and digital skills development. These are detailed in Appendix A as work in progress, reflecting that they have been designed but not yet implemented. They represent the micro-level translation of macro-level ministerial commitment and meso-level institutional development into concrete interventions that improve youth employment in creative sectors.
	•	The three approaches—employer-led digital skills bootcamps, Hyper Island-style organisational capability programmes, and university-creative industry partnerships—are intentionally varied in scale, delivery model, and target beneficiary. This variation reflects understanding that skills development in creative sectors cannot be one-size-fits-all. Young people entering the labour market need different support than organisations seeking to build internal capability. Established universities need different partnership models than emerging creative studios.
	•	Implementation of these three approaches is contingent on: funding commitments from employers, governments, and donors; identification of committed partner organisations in each country; and establishment of clear monitoring and accountability mechanisms. None of these preconditions are yet in place, hence the designation as work in progress.

What was delivered: A refined framework for conducting creative and digital skills needs assessment across WB6, designed to move beyond assumptions and establish evidence-based understanding of what employers, educators, and young people actually need. The framework balances rapid regional scoping (understanding the landscape across all six countries), targeted deep dives (detailed work in priority countries/sectors), employer-led evidence (what skills gaps actually exist), and statistical standardisation (comparable data across countries).
The Skills Needs Assessment methodology exists but has not been funded or executed.
Deliverables comprise the WB6 Creative and Digital Skills Needs Assessment options paper (June 2025), a four-option methodology framework with cost-benefit analysis, and a recommended phased approach: Phase 1 (light-touch regional mapping, 55–75 days), Phase 2 (targeted deep dives in 2–3 priority countries). The framework is grounded in the understanding that WB6 countries lack reliable baseline data on creative economy employment, skills provision, and graduate outcomes—a critical gap that must be addressed before credible intervention design can proceed.
In parallel to the above, the programme established a research and methodology framework for creative economy mapping. A UK Methodology and Research Design Consultant (10 working days) has designed standardised research tools (survey questionnaires, interview guides, mapping templates, quality assurance procedures) for consistent implementation across WB6. A UK Lead Creative and Digital Skills Consultant (30 working days) will lead intervention design, validation, and stakeholder engagement. These roles align with two open British Council calls and are designed to ensure that desk research and validation work completed before Christmas 2025 can be rapidly synthesised into concrete, implementable proposals by end of March 2026.
What this enables: Evidence-based intervention design. Rather than assuming skills gaps, programmes can now be designed on what employers, educators, and young people actually say is needed. This moves the region away from supply-driven thinking (what institutions want to teach) to demand-driven reality (what employers need, what young people need to progress). It also creates the baseline against which future impact can be measured.
What remains: Funding and execution. The framework is designed; it has not been implemented. WB6 countries still lack baseline data on creative economy employment, skills provision, or graduate outcomes.
Concrete, implementable designs over strategic frameworks
	•	The three-part skills model is deliberately concrete: bootcamps have specific duration (8–12 weeks), focus (digital marketing, UX/UI, web development), and outcomes (portfolio-ready graduates, 70%+ job placement). This specificity enabled rapid stakeholder buy-in. In contrast, abstract "creative economy frameworks" generated less engagement.
	•	Implication: Specificity is not a weakness; it's a strength. Concrete designs are implementable and monitorable. Vague frameworks are neither.
Delivery and monitoring
The three skills interventions detailed in Appendix A require the following before implementation can begin:
	•	Funding commitments from employers, governments, and international donors. 
	•	Identification and recruitment of committed partner organisations in each country. 
	•	University curriculum development and formalisation of partnership agreements. 
	•	Establishment of monitoring and accountability mechanisms to track participant progression, job placement, organisational culture shift, and policy impact.
	•	Timeline for pilot launch: April–June 2026. Completion of pilot phase and outcome measurement: September 2026.
National strategies must translate into operational support for bootcamps, partnerships, and organisations (micro). Currently, strategies exist; implementation funding and accountability structures do not.
	•	Early outcomes from bootcamp pilots and university partnerships should inform policy refinement (feedback loops). Currently, no monitoring or learning systems are in place.
	•	What's needed: An explicit "translation layer" connecting these three levels. This requires quarterly cross-country learning meetings to share bootcamp outcomes, partnership progress, and organisational capability shifts. Feedback mechanisms from micro-level programmes to inform meso-level strategy refinement. Regional synthesis of lessons learned to inform macro-level policy alignment.
