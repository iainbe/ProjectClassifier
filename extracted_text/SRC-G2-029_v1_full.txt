Executive summary
A thriving, evolving, and under-reported sector
This report presents a comprehensive analysis of the Creative Industries in the Solent and Hampshire Combined Authority (SHCA) region, revealing a dynamic and resilient sector that is a significant driver of the local economy. Our research, combining official statistics with innovative data analysis, indicates the sector's Gross Value Added (GVA) is an estimated £5.25 billion, with a total workforce of over 80,000 individuals. This is substantially larger than previously understood, primarily due to a large and often uncounted freelance and micro-business community.





Key Findings
Significant economic contributor: The Creative Industries contribute an estimated £5.25 billion GVA when accounting for freelance and micro-business activity. National statistics suggest a 26% increase in Creative Industries GVA between 2020 and 2023.
A Tale of Two Sectors: The overall number of creative businesses registered at locations across SHCA has seen a decline of 17% since its 2020 peak, a figure driven almost entirely by a 31% contraction in the IT & Computer Consultancy sub-sector. In contrast, core creative fields like Advertising and  marketing and Film, TV and Music (which more than doubled between 2010 and 2023) demonstrate strong, sustained growth, indicating a structural shift rather than a sector-wide decline.
The hidden workforce: Official figures (BRES) capture 39,800 Creative Industries employees, an 8.6% decline from the 2022 peak, reflecting changes in employment computer consultancy. However, our analysis of professional network data (LinkedIn) reveals a much larger creative economy workforce of over 80,500 creatives with a further 28,500 workers in related digital supply chains. Our analysis suggests that more than 30% of the sector's creative talent operates on a freelance, contract, or micro-business basis, a critical factor for policy and support initiatives.
Missing businesses: official statistics identify 7,530 active Creative Industries firms registered in Hampshire, Isle of Wight, Portsmouth and Southampton, Analysis of other databases shows more than 1,000 additional businesses registered in SHCA, and a further 2,800 business units active across the region but registered in other parts of Great Britain, making a total business population of 11,420.
Geographic & economic clustering: While traditional metrics show modest clustering of creative sub-sectors across SHCA, particularly in Advertising & Marketing (LQ 1.28) and Publishing (LQ 1.29), deeper analysis reveals powerful, localised hubs of creative activity in sectors including Film and television, Advertising and marketing, IT, software and computer services and Music, performing and visual arts, Furthermore, these creative clusters are often co-located with other professional services, creating a robust, interconnected economic ecosystem.
Wider economic impact of creative employment across the SHCA economy: Strong employment in both Services (31,246) and Tech (21,387) supply chains reflects the extent to which creative occupations form an important part of a professional business services sector wider than just Creative Industries,
Strategic Implications
A large proportion of SHCA’s creative workforce operates outside traditional employment structures, highlighting the need for nuanced data interpretation. The evidence indicates the presence of a highly entrepreneurial and flexible creative workforce, but also highlights potential challenges in accessing traditional business support and finance.
It underscores the need for a nuanced approach to supporting the creative economy. A "one-size-fits-all" strategy is insufficient. Policy and investment should focus on:
Nurturing the freelance ecosystem: Providing targeted support for the 23,000+ independent professionals – 31% of the creative workforce - who form the backbone of the industry.
Strengthening high-growth sub-sectors: Capitalising on the momentum in areas like film, television, and advertising to drive regional growth.
Fostering cross-sector innovation: Leveraging the co-location of creative and professional services to stimulate new business models and opportunities.
In conclusion, Solent & Hampshire Combined Authority’s creative sector is larger, more resilient, and more complex than official data suggests. Understanding its unique composition is key to unlocking its full potential as a driver of economic prosperity and cultural vibrancy for the region.

1.2. 	Data sources and methodology
To build a comprehensive picture, this analysis integrates data from multiple sources, each with its own strengths and limitations.
Office for National Statistics (ONS) - Inter-Departmental Business Register (IDBR): Provides official, reliable data on the number of registered businesses (VAT/PAYE-based) up to 2024. This is our baseline for business counts.
DataCity: A proprietary data platform that uses web-scraping and AI to identify active Creative businesses, including those not registered under Creative industries SIC codes and including branch offices of firms registered elsewhere in Great Britain. This gives a more realistic view of the total number of trading entities.
Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES): The official source for employee numbers, based on PAYE records. This data is robust and provides time series data for longitudinal analysis but does not capture freelancers or the self-employed.
LinkedIn: Provides a rich, real-time dataset of the professional workforce, including freelancers, contractors, and those in "creative roles" within non-creative companies. This helps to quantify the "hidden" creative economy.
Regional productivity data: Provided by ONS at a two-digit SIC code level, this is analysed to provide estimates of output within the SHCA area,
DCMS Economic Estimates: National-level data on the economic value (GVA) of creative sectors, used for top-down analysis and comparison.
By triangulating these sources, we can build a more accurate and nuanced understanding of the sector's true scale and impact.


2. 	The business landscape: Size, scale and trends
The creative industries in SHCA are a significant and evolving part of the regional economy. While headline figures suggest a slight contraction, a deeper analysis reveals a story of resilience and transformation.
2.1. Business population: A tale of two sectors
Analysis of official data from IDBR shows SHCA was home to 7,530 active creative businesses in 2024. This represents a 17% decrease from the 2020 peak of 9,050 businesses. However, this decline is almost entirely attributable to a sharp 31% contraction in the 'IT, software and computer consultancy' sub-sector. We observe similar trends in employment across other English regions: likely causes include post-pandemic market adjustments and the rise of new technologies like AI.
In contrast, other core creative sectors have shown remarkable resilience and growth:
Advertising and marketing: Grew by 7.4% between 2020 and 2024.
Film, TV, radio and photography: Grew by 14.3% in the same period.
Music, performing and visual arts: While volatile, this sector has also seen overall growth since 2010.
This divergence indicates a structural shift within the creative economy rather than a widespread decline.
2.2. Beyond official statistics: The DataCity perspective
Analysis of DataCity's platform, which includes businesses self-identified by “non-creative” SIC codes and branch offices, identifies 11,420 active creative business units in the region – 50% more than official figures suggest. This highlights a substantial base of smaller, micro, and satellite operations that are vital to the ecosystem.
The largest discrepancies between official data and the DataCity analysis are found in:
Publishing: 144% more businesses identified.
Music, performing and visual arts: 97% more businesses identified.
Advertising and marketing: 75% more businesses identified.
This confirms that these sub-sectors, in particular, are powered by a large number of smaller, often unregistered, enterprises


3. 	The creative workforce: Beyond the payroll
The distinction between official employment data and the reality of the creative workforce is stark. While traditional metrics provide a baseline, they significantly under-represent the true number of people working in the creative economy.
3.1. Official employment data vs. the real workforce
BRES Data (2023): Records 39,980 full- and part-time employees within Creative Industries businesses in SHCA. This number has seen an 8.6% decline since its 2022 peak, again driven by the contraction in the IT sector.
LinkedIn Data (2025): Identifies 80,500 individuals working in Creative Industries in the region, including freelancers, sole traders and the self-employed. This figure is nearly double the official employee count.
LinkedIn also provides evidence of a further 28,500 individuals working in related digital supply chains,
3.2. The freelance economy
Analysis of LinkedIn profiles reveals a "hidden" creative workforce: freelancers, sole traders, and creatives embedded in non-creative companies (the "Creative Trident").
 independent professionals:
12,700 individuals identify as freelance or self-employed (17% of the total).
When combined with contractors, this figure rises to over 23,000, accounting for 31% of the entire creative workforce.
Freelance working is particularly prevalent in sectors like Film & TV, Design, and Music, performing and visual arts, explaining the large discrepancy between official employment. These independent professionals are not just part of the workforce; they play a critical role in innovation and knowledge transfer between creative firms and other sectors of the Solent and Hampshire economy and between institutions such as Universities and cultural organisations and the private sector.

SHCA’s Creative Industries generate substantial economic value.

5. 	Geographic clusters and hotspots
The distribution of creative businesses is not uniform across the region. We combined methods of Location Quotient (LQ) analysis and advanced spatial mapping to identify key clusters of activity.
Regional Specialisms: While the overall creative industry LQ is just below the national average (0.94), there are clear specialisations in Advertising & Marketing (1.28) and Publishing (1.29). The Museums & Galleries sector also shows a very high concentration (LQ of 1.75).
Local Hotspots: Heatmap analysis reveals significant clusters of creative activity in and around major urban centres and along key transport corridors.
Sector-Specific Hubs
Film & TV: Concentrated around established studio facilities.
IT & Software: Forms a series of closely-linked clusters.
Advertising & Marketing: Shows a more dispersed pattern, often co-located with other professional services, indicating a strong B2B ecosystem.
Music & Performing Arts: Centred in urban areas with established venues and cultural infrastructure.
This data suggests that SHCA’s creative economy is not a single entity, but a network of interconnected, specialised hubs.

Count of number of active businesses in both Creative Industries and Cultural sectors was made in June 2025 from two sources:
Safeguarded data from the Interdepartmental Business Register (IDBR). This assembles sample data from ONS based on Standard Industrial Classifications (SIC codes) which relate to the DCMS definition of Creative Industries
Companies’ data extracted using DataCity – this is a proprietary platform which combines data from Companies House and other financial analysis with its own AI-based ‘scrape’ of over 2 million UK registered company websites, to provide a more in depth analysis of company and the sector to which it belongs, including those who may have registered the business under a ‘non-Creative Industries” SIC code (e.g, those registered under ‘operation of head offices’) but which clearly demonstrate that this is their main area of business.
1.1	Creative Industries - IDBR
Very little overall change in business population and LQ over the period 2010-24
Number of active businesses peaked (at 9,050) in 2020, driven by a spike in IT-related companies, which at that point represented 58% of Creative Industries businesses in SHCA.
Subsequent decline (to 7,530 companies) in 2024 – a fall of 17% from 2020 - almost exactly mirrored a drop in number of IT-related firms (1,660), which by 2024 represented only 48% of active Creative Industries (CI) firms.

We have seen similar patterns in business numbers in SIC 62020 in other places (including Surrey and across the Enterprise M3 geography), which reflects structural changes to the sector post-pandemic, when many independent consultants sought ‘safety’ of employment with larger firms, plus changes in technology (e.g., influence of AI and ‘NoCode’ development) reducing demand for more traditional forms of IT consultancy.
Other CI sectors were more resilient, with growth in Advertising and marketing (7.4%) and Film, TV, radio and photography (14.3%) over the period from 2020,
Film, TV, radio and photography, though still the highest growth sector (63%) overall, reflected fluctuations in demand for new productions, growing from 515 to 915 active firms between 2010 and 2022 before falling back to 840 in 2024.
Music, performing and visual arts showed similar volatility – from 645 to 700 between 2015 and 2020, peaking at 950 in 2022 and then falling back to 745 in 2024

Appendix  3: Analysis of creative workforce using LinkedIn
Relevance of using LinkedIn
We use LinkedIn as, effectively, a massive pre-filled sample. It is not a census of employment, but provides a much larger sample than other sources; and whilst some sectors of the Creative Industries (including cultural occupations in the arts, crafts, museum staff and librarians) may be less well represented than others, the overall penetration of LinkedIn, and the fact that it includes both employees and freelance/self-employed members of the workforce, makes it a powerful tool for analysis.
The total number of LinkedIn subscribers in Solent & Hampshire was 829,074 at time of the survey (April 2025). For comparison, the total working age population for Solent in Hampshire was 1,194,960. So, LinkedIn represented 69.3% of working age population. Total number of employees for all sectors was 897,885 (BRES 2023), meaning that LinkedIn’s subscriber base is equivalent to 92.3% of PAYE-registered employees.
The number of subscribers giving their location as a named place in Solent and Hampshire and citing a Cultural or Creative Industries occupation (80,500) represented 9.7% of all LinkedIn subscribers in the region. This compares with ONS data which identified 4.6% of all employees in Solent and Hampshire within the Cultural and Creative Industries (BRES 2023). 
Methodology
LinkedIn data analysis for workforce mapping offered a deep search methodology, based on LinkedIn's data categorisation, which was used to identify the scale and composition of the Cultural and Creative Industries workforce for SHCA. This included full-time and part-time employees, contractors, self-employed and freelance creative professionals, interns and students. 
LinkedIn Industry codes were aggregated up within five supply chains as follows:
Content – ‘traditional’ sectors of film, television, broadcast media, music, photography and publishing whose main output is in the form of creative IP.
Experiences – reflects the input of cultural and creative producers to live events and cultural experiences including performing arts, libraries and museums.
Originals – fine artists, craft makers, fashion designers and others whose original IP is in the form of a physical artefact, plus retail art galleries.
Services – creative professional services relating to advertising, marketing, architecture and design whose output is primarily ‘B2B’.
Tech – videogames, software development and technology and information service providers where the main input is related to the ‘creative’ function of software development. 
We also regrouped LinkedIn industries to reflect DCMS sector definitions for the purposes of comparative analysis with other datasets.

Evidence of a larger freelance and self-employed creative workforce
There are two main factors which account for the larger creative workforce identified in LinkedIn:
Creative workers in ‘non-creative”  sectors of the economy – e.g., those with specialist skills in disciplines including advertising, marketing and design.
Sectors with a high number of freelance and self-employed  workers, such as Film, TV, radio and photography, Design and designer fashion and Music, performing and visual arts
The first of these is comparable with the ‘Creative Trident’ identified by Nesta in its analysis of employment in the creative economy, as distinct from the creative sectors identified by DCMS. LinkedIn Industry definitions are self-selected by subscribers – and as the platform is designed to help people identify work opportunities across the economy, this means that they are as likely to reflect the discipline in which the individual specialises as the sector  within which they work. For example, our analysis of LinkedIn suggested that SHCA was home to more than six times as many people working in advertising and marketing and eight  times as many designers as identified by BRES, which counts employees within Standard Industrial Classifications. There was no evidence from companies’ data to suggest such an extraordinarily high degree of agglomeration of those sectors of the creative industries in the region; although, combined with evidence of clustering of business locations (see above), it did point to the presence of strong clusters of diversified professional services, including employers in creative, engineering, financial, manufacturing, scientific and technical sectors.
The second point reflects that many of the freelance and self-employed people in creative occupations may have this as part of a portfolio of work, and therefore not all of their output will be attributable to creative industries. In fact, many industry and union surveys in sectors such as film/TV and music highlight that a significant proportion of those workers will earn less than £10,000 per annum from their creative occupation.

Conclusions from analysis of LinkedIn
The LinkedIn analysis produces some expected outcomes, some surprises and raises some questions.
It demonstrates that the workforce in Film, TV, radio and photography, Crafts and Music, performing and visual arts is much larger than shown in both ‘raw’ and scaled-up ONS data – an outcome we might expect for sectors known to contain a very large freelance workforce.
Museums, Galleries and Libraries workforce, where many are public sector employees and may either not use LinkedIn or classify their main industry in a different way, is typically underestimated in LinkedIn – but here is much closer to the 2023 BRES figure than typical in similar surveys of other places.
Workforce for Publishing and IT, software and computer services closely reflects ONS data, and is lower than the scaled-up estimates for both sectors. That may arise from LinkedIn subscribers describing their main industry in a way which doesn’t immediately translate into DCMS sector definitions – e.g., people involved in printing of newspapers and magazines may associate themselves with the manufacturing process, or with design, rather than publishing.
The outcomes for Advertising and marketing and Design and designer fashion are of a completely different order. The number of LinkedIn subscribers giving a Hampshire location and citing their main industry as one of Advertising Services, Public Relations and Communications Services or Marketing Services – 17,910 in total – was more than six times greater than the ONS total employees within those sectors (2,695). For design occupations, the discrepancy was larger still (10,783 vs 1,315, a factor of more than eight). These proportions are outliers in terms of similar studies we have carried out in other English regions, 
This is most likely the result of a combination of other factors, including:
Digital marketing employment in firms classified under an IT or software SIC code
Employment of advertising and marketing professional roles in ‘non-creative’ sectors – part of the ‘Creative Trident’ of employment identified by Nesta
Remote working by SHCA-based professionals whose main employer is based outside the region
Separating out employment in those terms would require a manual input beyond the scope of this commission, did point to another potential strength of SHCA’s creative economy – significant clustering of professional service occupations, including accountants and lawyers alongside creative workers in advertising, marketing, architecture, design and software development – something analysed further in the section below.

Appendix 5:  Evidence of clustering
Location Quotient (LQ), which measures the respective density of either business units or employees within a specific geography relative to the national sector average, was used to look for evidence of  ‘clustering’ of creative industries. An LQ of 1 represents the average density of sector businesses or employment for Great Britain; given the concentration of Creative Industries in London, which distorts the GB average, any LQ of more indicates the presence of agglomeration effects, which may in turn suggest higher levels of specialisation, knowledge exchange and productivity within and between sector businesses. 
Our findings are indeterminate. Creative Industries business LQ ranged from 1.00 (in 2015) to 0.94 (in 2024) – although DataCity evidence suggested both a higher LQ (of 1.08) for the Creative Industries and stronger evidence of clustering in sub-sectors such as Publishing (1.29, versus an IDBR-derive LQ of 0.80) and Advertising and marketing (1.28 vs. 0.92, respectively). 
LQs for the number of creative employees across all Creative Sectors were very similar to those recorded the number of businesses; but differed greatly within individual subsectors of Advertising and marketing (with a lower LQ of 0.52, suggesting smaller business units) and IT, software and computer consultancy (LQ of 1.32 in 2023, suggesting larger business units). 
LQs for employees in Cultural sectors were again consistent with those for businesses overall, but with greater concentrations in Museums and Galleries (LQ of 1.41) and Operation of historical sites (1.61), and lower in Film, TV and Music (LQ 0.53), suggesting smaller business units.
Sub-sector clusters
Analysis of LQ for Creative Industries businesses (compared to baseline of IDBR 2024) suggested stronger evidence of clustering in Advertising and marketing, Music, performing and visual arts and Publishing.
Summary
ONS data provided weak evidence of clustering in Creative industries, which showed overall LQ < 1 for the whole period. Exceptions were Architecture (1.06) and IT, software and computer services (1.12, even taking into account falls in the number of computer consultancy firms).
Museums and galleries (Cultural sector) showed strongest evidence of clustering, with an LQ of 1.75.
Evidence of sustained growth in Advertising and marketing, Film, TV and Music and Arts – but (on ONS data) none of these demonstrated a ‘cluster’
DataCity provided clearer evidence of clustering in Advertising and marketing (LQ of 1.28) and Publishing (1.29)
Localised clusters
The LQ analysis for employment and workforce was drawn across the pan-Hampshire area and did not, therefore, reflect geographical clusters of creative businesses within specific districts. 
Initial analysis was carried out from DataCity evidence of companies’ postcodes, which was then geocoded to provide a physical location. 
The methodology to establish physical clustering reflected that used in the PEC Creative Radar study. It employed the HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) algorithm - a density-based machine learning clustering technique that improves upon traditional cluster analysis algorithms by using a hierarchical approach which takes into account both the distance between points and local point density. The algorithm was run with a range of distance thresholds to assess how company groupings emerge at different spatial scales. The final map, shown below, used a distance threshold of 1km and a minimum cluster size of 50 companies. Identified clusters were then converted from point-based groupings into polygon shapes to represent their spatial extent more clearly. 
Constraints and caveats on the data
Of over 100 postcode areas in Hampshire with more than 10 registered companies, all but three contained accountants’ offices, each with multiple businesses, which makes the exact extent of clustering uncertain. 
However, the presence of those accountants does point to another valuable phenomenon: that of combined professional services clusters, including elements of creative services such as advertising, marketing, architecture, design and software development.  This is explored in more detail in analysis of LinkedIn workforce and economic outputs, below.

Comparison of Creative Industries and Visitor Economy clusters
The Visitor Economy (Tourism and related activities) had 11,345 firms in 2023 in SHCA employing some 113,000 workers making it one of the largest local employers. The County area accounts for around two thirds of SHCA visitor economy businesses and employment. 
Figure 14 shows that high business concentrations (marked in darker green) are found on the Isle of Wight and several concentrations exist across Solent and neighbouring authorities. Employment concentrations are far more widespread, with the south coast an obvious cluster that also extends contiguously into Dorset, but also with high employment concentrations in parts of North Hampshire. Since this sector includes hospitality and a range of tourism related activities that are found in most places across the country it is not unusual to find relatively few concentrations relative to the national average. Relative concentrations may be few but employment effect of this sector on SHCA is large.
