Our understanding of the brief
West Midlands Combined Authority (WMCA) is one of three areas to have agreed a Sector Deal for Creative Industries. This includes funding for a creative business scale-up programme, to boost growth and productivity in the sector across all parts of the region.
The scale-up programme is likely to be organised in the form of a number of cohorts, each of ten to twenty businesses drawn from a similar sector or across a supply chain for creative content, services, products and experiences. BOP has previously carried out a mapping of the creative economy in Greater Birmingham & Solihull, using these definitions as a baseline and assessing the strengths and opportunities within those sectors and supply chains. This mapping exercise builds on that experience and adds a number of experimental methods to provide both a fine-grained understanding of ‘scale-up potential’ and contact, financial and employment details of individual creative businesses.
Definition of creative businesses
The starting point of our definition of creative businesses is the DCMS Creative Industries sector: a list of official creative industries SIC codes based on the Dynamic Mapping methodology developed by Nesta and adopted by DCMS. 
These definitions have provided the basis for creative industries mapping studies, including Nesta’s Creative Nation report (2018) and many studies carried out by BOP over the years, for the last twenty years. It breaks down Creative Industries into nine sub-sectors: advertising and marketing; architecture; crafts; design; film, television, video, radio and photography, IT, software and computer services; publishing; music, visual and performing arts; and museums, galleries and libraries. 
The sector definitions are arrived at through analysis of the proportion of creative occupations within those industries. Both industry and occupational definitions will be used to inform both statistical analysis and selection of keywords for searches of other sources, as described in the methodology set our below, to help overcome the limitations of national statistics.
A listing of DCMS Creative Industries sector groupings and occupations is provided in the Appendices below.
Addressing gaps in the definition
The weaknesses of Office of National Statistics’ Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES) data as the basis of creative industries mapping are widely acknowledged – including by DCMS itself, which commissioned an extensive survey in 2017 to identify potential ways to improve on the way in which it aggregates and analyses data on the sector. Those weaknesses include the fact that BRES represents only a sample of businesses; and that it takes into account inly registered companies, which means that it tends to underestimate the contribution of self-employed and freelance workers who may form an important element of the talent pool and capacity for innovation in the creative industries.
Furthermore, in our recent work for Coventry & Warwickshire LEP, GBS LEP, Digital Catapult, UK Research and Innovation and others, we have established that an significant proportion of businesses engaged in production of creative content, services and experiences and manufacture of creative products do not register themselves under one of the 31 DCMS Creative Industries SIC codes, either because they have selected the ‘wrong’ SIC code for their activity, because the main activity of the company has evolved over its lifetime or because the company’s activities cover other areas (eg, manufacturing, retail, head office of a group of companies) as well as creative industries. Examples of this include:
A digital creative business engaged in creation of immersive entertainment content or software, recorded under SIC code 63990: Other information services activities not elsewhere classified (included in the DCMS Digital Industries definition, but not in Creative Industries)
A bespoke gents tailor, who may also have a retail premises and manufacturing capacity, registered under 47710: Retail sale of clothing in specialised stores (rather than, say, 74100: Specialised design activities.)
The parent of a group of post-production and visual effects companies that has registered itself under 70100: Activities of head offices
All three business could have scale-up potential and could benefit from forming part of a cohort of other creative businesses with scale-up potential: eg, a group of immersive content developers, a fashion cohort or a group of film and television production companies, respectively.
Jewellery
	•	We know that the extent of jewellery trade activity within DCMS Creative Industries definition is limited to the SIC code 32120: Manufacture of jewellery and related articles. Other ‘adjacent’ codes are excluded on the basis that these industries do not contain a sufficiently high proportion of ‘creative’ workers (determined by the list of Standard Occupational Classification codes used to determine which SIC codes meet the definition of a Creative Industry sub-sector.) These may include:
	•	47770: Retail sale of watches and jewellery in specialised stores
	•	32130: Manufacture of imitation jewellery and related articles
We feel that there may potential in Birmingham, with one of the UK’s largest and most strongly clustered jewellery sectors, to form a cohort of jewellery designers within its ‘scale-up’ programme; but some of the businesses most likely to scale may be defined by their retail or manufacturing activities, rather than by design. 
We will use a mixture of methods, described in our methodology section below, to seek to identify and validate creative businesses in this sector.
Digital creative businesses
 We would therefore look to add to our initial scan of businesses within DCMS Creative Industries SIC codes a limited number of SIC codes from within DCMS’s Digital Industries definition which, in our experience, include a significant minority of companies with creative outputs that are appropriately aligned with other Creative Industries firms – including:
62090: Other information technology and computer service activities
63910: Other news agency activities (also part of the related DCMS definition of the Audio Visual sector)
63990: Other information services activities not elsewhere classified
The methodology set out below will explain how we will then combine other commercial and public data sources to arrive at a list of creative businesses which takes account of the significant proportion of digital businesses that can be considered as part of the creative industries supply chain and which in turn provide evidence of their scale-up potential..
Approach and methodology
Schedule of work
The work will be divided into three phases, each subdivided into work packages. Individual work packages are described in more detail below; here, we describe the sequence and logic chain for our proposed activities.
Phase One - Identification
Using a mix of public, commercial, online and private sources to identify the total pool of creative businesses within the WMCA geography:
	•	WP1: Identify all creative businesses (defined by DCMS Creative Industries definitions and including some Digital SIC codes, as outlined at 1.2 , above) in WMCA constituent and non-constituent member and partner areas
	•	WP2: Identify creative businesses within UK Creative Industry and sector networks and WMCA hubs (including Meetups)
	•	WP3: Identify creative businesses who have received or are looking for private finance
	•	WP4: Identify creative businesses who have received or are looking for public finance, including funding for R&D
Phase Two – Analysis and assurance 
Using a mix of commercial and free data sources in an experimental methodology to, (a) assure that the companies identified in Phase One are active creative businesses and (b) find additional evidence to define their scale-up potential and (c) identify contact details:
	•	WP5: Scrape of all websites of companies within the list
	•	WP6: LinkedIn analysis – including keyword search
	•	WP7: Social media analysis 
	•	WP8: Recruitment analysis 
 Phase Three – Ranking  
This process will result in a list of creative businesses ‘RAG rated’ (red, amber, green) against ‘scale-up’ common application gateway criteria and confidence factors. (see Figure 3, below for examples):
	•	WP9: Scale-up score
Optional survey
We have considered the potential to design and implement an online business survey as a further refinement of the validation and ranking process of eligible businesses that meet the common application gateway criteria. However, we have not included it in our main proposal as it may not need to be deployed unless there are insufficient businesses that pass the common application gateway criteria and/or have a high or very high scale-up score identified by the initial three phases of work.
Methodology
Use of innovative data sources
We describe this as an ‘experimental’ methodology – in that it uses a mix of national statistics, commercial and publicly-available data source. But in fact, we have refined this approach over the course of the last two years across several projects, at local authority, regional and national level, as described at Section 4, below.
Our approach moves away from dependency on a single set of sample-based national statistics (as enshrined in the methodology for DCMS sector estimates) to use commercially available data that are collected on an individual company and job basis and updated in real time. Keyword search
Use of sector definitions is augmented by keywords that interrogate in more detail the sub-sector, market vertical, product, services and roles within the creative industries. The exact structure of primary and secondary keywords is determined by the specification and outputs in the ITQ, but in this case they may include:
	•	Industry sector (which can be correlated against Companies House records of SIC codes and LinkedIn industry codes)
	•	Skills (which can be correlated against SOC codes)
	•	Themes
	•	Technologies (eg, Augmented or Virtual Reality)
	•	Markets
	•	KPIs of the ‘scale-up’ programme
Development and refinement of data
We have already tested the approach proposed here by means of an initial high level scan of the proposed sources. 
Agile project management
The project will be managed using ‘agile’ techniques, defined by a number of sprints, some of which will overlap (as they will be carried out by different teams.) This will allow us to continuously improve our method and improve accuracy of our results by iterative refinement of the findings through combining and comparing the different data sources.

Phase Three: Scale-up scoring
We will collate the outputs of Phase Two in a table which will give a ‘red, amber, green’ score for each of the common application gateway criteria and a high growth indicator: 
Common application gateway
	•	Creative Industries Sector – company’s main activity is registered at Companies House under one of the SIC codes identified within DCMS Creative Industries sector
	•	Financial growth – actual figures, based upon accounts from previous 3 years, to identify growth of at least 10% 
	•	Employees – estimate of number of employees and contractors to assure that the company meets the gateway criterion of 8 employees 
Scale-up indicators
	•	Private sector investment – volume of investment and potential IP registered on commercial investment websites; seeking investment 
	•	from professional and ‘angel’ investors is a strong indicator of ambition to grow the business, irrespective of amount
	•	Public investment –receipt of public funding for product development or collaborative R&D is an indicator that the company is looking to invest in development of IP 
	•	Social media index – indicator of businesses with marketing budget and collateral i.e. client testimonials and case studies 
	•	Recruitment index – to identify which companies have used online recruitment sites in the last six months, the number of posts and level of seniority of the role(s) advertised

WP9: Scale-up scoring   
Based upon the data on each company we will now be able to create final spreadsheet of companies, scoring their eligibility for the scale up programme and a confidence level for their growth.

Repeatability, extensibility and transferability of methodology
Repeatability
The method proposed is based upon work previously carried out by BOP Consulting and Curator Technologies working together to address problems of identifying populations and clustering of creative businesses, including nascent sectors such as immersive creative content developers.
Whilst we describe it as an ‘experimental’ methodology, in that it moves away from reliance on national statistics to incorporate more up-to-the-minute commercial evidence of company performance and real time social media and recruitment information contributed by employers and employees working within the sector, it has been proven to be robust both in its application to studies of creative industries within the UK and in its use within City Curator, a commercial product that uses the same sources as the basis of investment benchmarking of 27 cities across UK, Europe and North America.
The method combines use of national statistics, Companies House data (parsed via DueDil), LinkedIn and commercial databases of investment (eg, Crunchbase) and recruitment (eg, Glassdoor, Adzuna, Indeed.)  LinkedIn is a global product, owned by Microsoft, with 26 million UK members. The commercial platforms used are stable businesses; but if one or more of them were to fail, the same data would be available through other sources. 
Extensibility into other geographies
Elements of the methodology have previously been used at different levels of geography, from local authority (London Borough of Havering) to national studies (sector analysis for evaluation of Audience of the Future, Digital Catapult study of barriers to growth of UK immersive content producers). 
Both public and commercial datasets are available that cover the whole of the UK, and the study can be repeated at any level of geography, including bespoke geographies – for Audience of the Future, we came up with a listing of the top 20 UK cities, which involved determining functional economic geographies around core and key cities in all four nations, and compared these with results at NUTS1 level to understand where clustering of immersive creative content producers was happening.
Transferability into other sectors
The methodology is informed by the development of a commercial product, City Curator, which uses a similar set of data sources to provide international benchmarking of 27 UK, European and North American cities for companies looking to invest or relocate. City Curator already includes a wider definition of the digital sector amongst the options for comparison, including emerging sectors such as fintech and edtech. A new commercial application is in development that will see its coverage extend to advanced manufacturing and other business and professional service sectors.
In its international application, City Curator also ranks university and private sector R&D and innovation centres, which is of direct relevance both to other sectors and to the ‘grand challenges’ within UK Industrial Strategy.
Report of methodology
BOP Consulting and Curator Technology will prepare a report of the methodology to be shared within the client organisation and with the Creative Sector ‘scale-up’ programme sponsors at DCMS, subject to an agreement that reflects commercial confidentiality and prior intellectual property rights.
Previous experience
In addition to BOP’s 2017 mapping of the Creative Economy of Greater Birmingham & Solihull LEP, which highlighted the importance of spillovers between the creative industries and other high-growth sectors of the West Midlands economy, the last three years have seen BOP develop a portfolio of innovative approaches to mapping of creative industries sectors, clusters and firms. Our work in this respect is action-focused and informed by a strong awareness of ‘place’ – in particular, looking to identify ‘smart specialisations’ and supply chain relationships within localities and regions that offer actual or potential competitive advantages that can drive future productivity growth.
Sector analysis for Audience of the Future
BOP and Curator are currently working together to carry out the analysis of the immersive creative content sector as part of an evaluation of the Audience of the Future programme for UK Research and Innovation. 
BOP and Curator developed an experimental methodology that combined national statistical data from the Business Register and Employment Survey with analysis of Companies House records (parsed via DueDil), LinkedIn records (to identify the size of the UK workforce with immersive skills, including technical skills) and commercial investment and recruitment databases.
Data was recombined at national, NUTS1 and a bespoke ‘top 20’ cities level to help analyse clustering of immersive firms and the impact of other Industrial Strategy Challenge Fund investments, notably Arts & Humanities Research Council awards to nine university consortia to form R&D partnerships with creative businesses, on growth of the sector.
London Borough of Havering
BOP and Curator collaborated on mapping of cultural and creative industries for London Borough of Havering. An innovative methodology combined analysis of the Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES) via NOMIS with commercial data sources including DueDil and Crunchbase to help identify emerging strengths and sources of resilience in the local creative economy. Companies were mapped down to Medium Super Output Area (MSOA) level in order to identify specific clusters and risks of displacement from regeneration plans for Romford town centre. Our recommendations also highlighted potential for Rainham to become a strategic location for creative manufacturing within the Thames Estuary Production Corridor (TEPC) – an opportunity that had been overlooked in a wider mapping exercise carried out on behalf of TEPC by other consultants.
Mapping the Creative Economy of Culture Mile
BOP worked in partnership with placemaking consultancy Publica to map the creative economy of the Culture Mile within the City of London. Mapping of creative firms down to a Local Super Output Area (LSOA) level, backed by a street-by-street analysis of the different typologies of creative business across the City, highlighted distinct clusters of creative businesses. This combined approach helped identify the supply chain and research and development relationships between them and with the larger cultural organisations, Barbican, London Symphony Orchestra, Museum of London and Guildhall School of Music & Drama. This pointed up a much stronger and more direct relationship between public investment in innovation in the arts and knowledge transfer through creative businesses and into the wider tech and knowledge economy than had previously been suspected. The findings were expressed in the form of a Theory of Change 
Digital Catapult
BOP and Curator jointly developed an experimental methodology to establish the population of immersive content and technology companies in the UK, identifying clusters and smart specialisms in locations across the four nations to provide the basis of an analysis of barriers to growth in access to investment, production techniques and distribution networks for the sector.
A blueprint for growth of the videogames sector in the West Midlands
BOP was commissioned by Coventry & Warwickshire LEP and Ukie, the national videogames industry association, to carry out a mapping study of the games sector in and around Leamington Spa. The BOP team, led by Iain Bennett, combined  mapping, primary research with local businesses and stakeholders and case studies of other international digital clusters. The final report highlighted the importance of smart specialisms in technology and IP development in Leamington Spa in building the strength of the West Midlands digital sector. The final action plan contained recommendations for skills, capital and cluster development for the sector, as well as actions to harness the cultural impact of games in education and the visitor economy.
