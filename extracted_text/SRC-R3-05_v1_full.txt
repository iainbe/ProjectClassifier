Purpose of this review
Derby is about to make decisions about culture under more pressure than at any point in a generation. Three changes converge on the same eighteen months. Local Government Reorganisation will replace Derby City Council with a new unitary authority around 2028, so the budgets this Council sets over the next year are among its last. Arts Council England has retired Let's Create and will decide a new National Portfolio in 2027 for funding from April 2028. And EMCCA's devolution, its £1bn visitor-economy ambition and the cultural strategy going to its board during 2026 open a regional investment tier that did not exist when Derby last took stock in 2021. The decisions that position Derby for the 2028 settlement are taken now, even if the money arrives later. The interim report you have asked for in late September must make the investment case land inside this Council's budget-setting and the EMCCA strategy while both are still being written. Whoever you appoint must understand that the clock, as much as the brief, defines the job.
Why The Fifth Sector
We bring together four things no other supplier offers as one team.
First, we wrote the foundation this review builds on. In 2021–22 we authored Derby's Cultural Masterplan, the Cultural Compact and its governance model, and the Culture Derby Manifesto, from stakeholder interviews and an original creative-economy evidence base including comparative Arts Council investment analysis. Objective 1 — defining the civic, economic and social ambitions for culture within Vibrant City priorities — descends directly from work Derby already owns. We need no discovery phase to learn who runs what or where the tensions sit. And we will mark that work honestly: it was built for a context since overtaken — EMCCA did not exist, reorganisation was not on the table, the industrial-strategy and Arts Council changes were years away.
Second, the Foresight platform, described below, treats Derby's cultural system as something that changes over time and captures the events that change it.
Third, independence. We are independent of all five Derby institutions and of the Arts Council, so an options appraisal with genuinely difficult choices carries no conflict of interest.
Fourth, current policy fluency. We will position Derby's NPOs for the framework that sets their funding from 2028, not the one being retired. We know what the Creative Industries Sector Plan and Invest 2035 mean for a city whose cultural identity is fused with advanced manufacturing and making — the Derwent Valley Mills World Heritage Site, the Museum of Making on the world's first modern factory site, Rolls-Royce, Toyota and rail. But that identity is broader than machinery. Derby's textile trade heritage shaped not only its economy but its communities: it was a primary force in attracting and sustaining the city's South Asian population, a connection the city has not yet fully recognised in its cultural narrative. That fusion of making, manufacturing and community is Derby's most fundable distinctiveness — aligning the cultural case with industrial strategy, EMCCA's inclusive-growth ambitions, UNESCO Learning City status, and the National Lottery Heritage Fund's arts-heritage collaboration agenda. It also positions Derby to work with the Heritage Fund as an essential partner, given that the city's heritage assets extend well beyond Derby Museums to the Derwent Valley Mills WHS — much of which falls outside the city boundary and is largely absent from the Council's cultural narrative, its public profile and visitor draw benefiting the wider valley more than the city itself.
Foresight: analysing culture as it changes
Most reviews photograph the sector once. Foresight runs it as a film — a customisable platform built across successive commissions, holding organisational data alongside deprivation, earnings and home-ownership measures, and open data on non-registered venues that conventional sector data misses. Because it is built on a time series, it answers a question an options appraisal usually cannot: what happened, and to whom, when something was put in or taken away.
For Derby that means we capture events on one timeline: Arts Council funding decisions read against what each did to investment per head and its distribution; organisational change modelled with measurable before-and-after effects; and planning and regeneration decisions — the Assembly Rooms fire and demolition, the opening of Vaillant Live, Derby Theatre's consented £20m redevelopment — mapped against footfall, deprivation and reach. Foresight overlays this with who culture does and does not reach across Derby's neighbourhoods — making diversity, inclusion and participation measurable, not aspirational. It can show which communities are underserved and how that maps against deprivation, ethnicity and the geography of Derby's South Asian population in particular.

Method
The review runs in five strands across seven months: understand, then generate options, then recommend.
Strand 1: Re-baselining and evidence (from inception). We update the 2021 baseline rather than rebuild it, refresh the creative-economy data through Foresight, and read the five institutions as primary sources — accounts, business plans, reserves, Arts Council agreements, and their place in the live regeneration picture. The evidence base also maps Derby's creative SME economy, including the notable creative businesses that have clustered in market towns such as Wirksworth rather than in the city centre — a pattern the review will connect to the city-centre regeneration picture (Assembly Rooms, Vaillant Live, Derby Theatre) to identify what would attract and retain creative businesses in the city core.
Strand 2: Infrastructure review and options appraisal. For each of Artcore, Derby Museums, Derby Theatre, Derby LIVE and QUAD we assess financial sustainability, alignment to Vibrant City, and governance — cost bases, reserves and balance-sheet resilience, the public-funding mix and exposure to short-term project income. These are very different vehicles — a university-owned theatre with a consented £20m redevelopment; independent trusts (Derby Museums' Museum of Making, QUAD with its FORMAT festival); the community-rooted Artcore; and Derby LIVE, a council in-house operation alongside Vaillant Live — and we respect that the independent institutions are not the Council's to consolidate. Structural integration is one option on a spectrum from continued independence through shared services to deeper combination; the test is always the offer to the city and long-term resilience, not consolidation for its own sake.
Strand 3: Benchmarking. We benchmark Derby against comparable authorities — a comparator set agreed at inception, drawing on cities such as Portsmouth, Southampton, Plymouth, Wakefield and Nottingham — analysing relative public investment in culture, funding mix and organisational models. Foresight pairs this with participation and reach data, so the case rests on who Derby's culture does and does not serve. That means examining diversity and inclusion as evidence: which communities participate, which are underserved, and how investment reaches — or fails to reach — Derby's South Asian and other minority communities compared with peer cities such as Leicester and Nottingham. Unlike Leicester, Derby lacks a critical mass of diverse-led cultural organisations — Artcore is virtually the only one — and the review will need to address this structural gap, not merely measure it.
Strand 4: Cultural Investment Strategy. We establish realistic levels of public, private and contributed income to sustain the infrastructure and deliver the defined outcomes, and propose practical mechanisms to grow private and philanthropic investment and cut overreliance on short-term project funding. The strategy is designed to operate within reorganisation and the EMCCA context, and to position Derby for Creative Industries Sector Plan and Industrial Strategy investment. It also identifies opportunities to lever Heritage Fund investment, building on the arts-heritage collaboration agenda and Derby's underexploited heritage assets — including those within the city beyond Derby Museums, and the Derwent Valley Mills WHS, whose visitor draw currently benefits the wider valley more than the city itself.
Strand 5: Narrative, roadmap and reporting. We define the ambition for culture within Vibrant City priorities, write the strategic narrative built on Derby's making-and-innovation identity and its World Heritage and Learning City assets, and set out a policy-alignment and implementation roadmap. The narrative will recognise Derby's textile trade heritage and its role in attracting and sustaining the city's South Asian community, and will make diversity, inclusion and participation central to the case for investment — not as a compliance add-on but as a strategic asset aligning with the inclusive-growth ambitions of EMCCA, the Arts Council and the Heritage Fund. Deliverables include an executive summary for members and trustees and a publication-ready final report.
Consultation runs across all strands — structured interviews, wider engagement through Arts Derbyshire networks, and a short survey — reaching beyond the five named institutions to creative SMEs, the National Lottery Heritage Fund, and organisations serving Derby's South Asian and other minority communities. The full consultation list is agreed at inception. We surface what has and has not worked to design the next model, neither defending nor conducting an inquest on the old one.

The interim report in late September is the document that has to land inside Council budget-setting, so we prioritise it from day one. Evidence and re-baselining run through July–August with institutional review and first consultations starting immediately; options appraisal, consultation and the investment strategy continue through autumn with monthly check-ins; draft and final reports follow in January and February 2027. Assumptions and exclusions
The Council will provide, at inception, access to the five institutions' financial and governance information, a named internal contact for coordination, and introductions where helpful to the institutions, members and officers.
The comparator set and final consultation list will be agreed at the inception meeting. The consultation list will include, as a minimum: leadership and board chairs of Artcore, Derby Museums, Derby Theatre, Derby LIVE and QUAD; council members and officers; the University of Derby; EMCCA cultural strategy leads; Arts Council relationship managers; the National Lottery Heritage Fund; business and philanthropic voices; creative SMEs operating in and around Derby; and organisations serving Derby's South Asian and other minority communities. Wider sector engagement will run through Arts Derbyshire networks, supplemented by a short online survey.
Pricing assumes the institutions engage in good faith and make accounts and business plans available; we will not charge for delay caused by access or governance issues on the client side.
The review is forward-looking and does not include a retrospective audit of past spending or decisions. It does not include primary capital appraisal, options modelling for the Assembly Rooms site, or a full creative industries economic impact assessment, though the evidence base will speak to the creative economy context.
Day rates are blended across the team. Travel and subsistence within the East Midlands are included within the fee. Travel to London for Heritage Fund meetings is included within the fee.
