Although a small group, it contained representatives from the local authority, the city region Innovation board, universities, VC-backed digital creative firms, Arts Council NPOs and social enterprise.
Discussion can be summarised in a SWOT:
Table 1 SWOT analysis of creative & digital sectors in Liverpool City Region
Strengths
Weaknesses
Culture (big and small ‘c’) – recognised as an important driver of civic and commercial investment (particularly in the city)
Identity – strong sense of local identity, often linked by culture
Creativity – legacy of distinctive creativity in music, games, film, television
Storytelling - strengths in storytelling across music, media, publishing, spoken word
Film – Liverpool now second most filmed city in UK outside London, as a result of sustained investment promotion and financial support for indigenous and inbound productions (esp. high end TV and features), which is now returning 6 x public investment
Social enterprise – has proved a successful, scalable and transferable model for creative and tech sectors

Formal innovation ecosystem – disjointed and focused on other sectors (advanced manufacturing, chemicals) 
Lack of patient money and smart capital in local economy – not enough founders have exited and reinvested, corporate sector smaller than other core cities (in part due to proximity to Manchester and ‘180 degree’ nature of Liverpool’s economy with resulting smaller commercial hinterland)
Competition between institutions – main universities unable to collaborate on Creative Cluster bid, at least one too many mayors
Lack of major institutions with significant market reach – with exception of Sony (which is pretty insular), little presence of global brands
Opportunities
Threats
Understand how the scalability of collaborative models successfully deployed in social enterprise can start to populate a pre-start pipeline
Develop complementary strategy to Manchester (and seek to take advantage of more developed investor ecosystem there) rather than looking to compete
Highlight where start-ups and scale-ups are part of larger global supply chains, delivering both content and tech innovation (eg, significant portion of AoTF development budgets subcontracted to Liverpool XR companies)
Big student population, cheap accommodation, lower cost of living / wages than core cities such as Mcr, Bristol, Edinburgh could be more effectively marketed as an attractive start-up and FDI proposition for creative and tech scale-ups
Continuing to take a ‘top down’ approach, working through institutions with limited financial scope or human capacity (City Council) or little or no ‘institutional memory’ of successful investment in digital & creative (LEP, CA, universities)
Continuing to expect Liverpool to perform in same way or replicate strengths of Manchester 
Extremely deep cuts to public sector budgets (esp. City Council) threaten vibrancy and viability of cultural sector which has been such an important source of talent and audiences for creative sector as a whole

Conclusion
Models of growth based on single companies will continue to demonstrate poorer returns in Liverpool than in other markets for structural reasons. However, understanding opportunities to stimulate ‘horizontal’ growth of a creative and commercial ecosystem through developing successful models of collaborative innovation drawing up precedent from social enterprise is likely to stimulate more start-up activity and promote resilience and sustainability of the sector.
Commercial context

Structural weaknesses
Of the 68 companies,

Only 4 are 20% scale ups
